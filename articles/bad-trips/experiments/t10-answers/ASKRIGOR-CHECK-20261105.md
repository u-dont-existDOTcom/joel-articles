**Short answer: partly.** The trial and drug-interaction claims are roughly right but overstated or mis-cited in places, the warnings about high-THC cannabis rest on animal work and one observational study, and the two ABRACE-specific claims could not be checked here.

How I checked: AskRigor's tools in this session returned citation receipts but did not display abstracts or full texts, so the figures below were confirmed by exact-term searches of each paper's indexed abstract (and, where noted, its full-text sections), not by reading the papers whole.

**1. ABRACE's 1,532 epilepsy patients among more than 14,000 members: not checked.** This is the association's own count on its website, and I could not open that page here.

**2. The João Pessoa quality-of-life study: not checked.** The article exists (Residência Pediátrica, March 2023, listed in the Directory of Open Access Journals), but it is not in PubMed or Europe PMC and I could not open its text here, so its design, its numbers and which direction of its scores means better remain unchecked.

**3. Clobazam interaction: holds.** Cannabidiol raises blood levels of N-desmethylclobazam, the active substance the seizure drug clobazam turns into in the body, about 3.4-fold in healthy volunteers ([Morrison 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30791225/)) and by around 500% on average in children with refractory epilepsy ([Geffrey 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26114620/)). A review names blocking of the liver enzyme CYP2C19 as the likely cause ([Patsalos 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32918835/)). In the tuberous sclerosis trial ([Thiele 2021, tuberous sclerosis trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33346789/)), sleepiness affected 13% of people on 25 mg/kg and 26% on 50 mg/kg versus 9% on placebo over 16 weeks, so the write-up's 20 to 30% is the high end of the range.

**4. Valproate and liver enzymes: partly.** Raised liver enzymes with cannabidiol are linked to taking valproate at the same time ([Gaston 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28782097/)). In the first Lennox-Gastaut trial, 14 people on cannabidiol (9%) had raised liver enzymes ([Devinsky 2018, Lennox-Gastaut trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29768152/)). I could not confirm the write-up's figure of up to 15%.

**5. The Stanford study: the work exists, but the conclusion drawn from it does not follow.** Ivan Soltesz's lab and collaborators tracked the brain's own cannabis-like signalling molecule, 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG), surging during seizures in rodents ([Farrell 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34352214/)), and the paper's results discuss postictal hypoxia (low brain oxygen after a seizure) and the CB1 cannabinoid receptor. A 2022 rodent paper on endocannabinoids and postictal hypoxia involves CB1 receptors and the pathway that breaks 2-AG down ([2022 Epilepsia paper on postictal hypoxia](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35852401/)). Neither paper's abstract mentions THC or cannabis, and I could not confirm from them which pathway drives the hypoxia. These are studies of the brain's own signalling in rodents, so they cannot show that high-THC products harm people with epilepsy (inferred).

**6. The 2026 Epilepsia study: exists, details unconfirmed.** A 2026 Epilepsia study of patients in epilepsy monitoring units, hospital wards where seizures are recorded on video and EEG, looked at how often they used cannabis, THC or potency, and epilepsy onset ([2026 Epilepsia study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42803888/)); news coverage in September and October 2026 described it as tying frequent cannabis use to more severe epilepsy. Neither the 64-patient count nor the 5-versus-18-year figures appear in its abstract, so I could not confirm them. As an observational study it cannot show that cannabis caused the severity, since people with harder-to-treat epilepsy may also use more cannabis (inferred).

**7. Size of cannabidiol's effect: right for the cannabidiol groups, but it leaves out placebo, and the citation is wrong.**
- Dravet syndrome ([Devinsky 2017, Dravet trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28538134/)): convulsive seizures fell from a median (the middle value) of 12.4 to 5.9 a month on cannabidiol versus 14.9 to 14.1 on placebo, an adjusted difference of 22.8 percentage points.
- Lennox-Gastaut syndrome ([Devinsky 2018, Lennox-Gastaut trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29768152/); [Thiele 2018, Lennox-Gastaut trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29395273/)): drop seizures fell by a median 41.9% on 20 mg/kg and 37.2% on 10 mg/kg versus 17.2% on placebo, and by 43.9% versus 21.8% in the second trial.
- Tuberous sclerosis complex ([Thiele 2021, tuberous sclerosis trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33346789/)): seizures fell 48.6% on 25 mg/kg and 47.5% on 50 mg/kg versus 26.5% on placebo, which the authors give as 30.1% and 28.5% fewer seizures than placebo.

The 30 to 50% figure is the drop on cannabidiol; the gain over placebo is about 20 to 30 percentage points (inferred for the Lennox-Gastaut trials from their medians). The "Thiele et al., Epilepsia 2022" citation is the open-label extension of the tuberous sclerosis trial, where everyone knew they were taking cannabidiol, not the randomized trial ([Thiele 2022, open-label extension](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34957550/)). These trials tested pharmaceutical cannabidiol (Epidiolex) in three severe epilepsies; in adults with focal epilepsy, a cannabidiol gel applied to the skin did no better than placebo ([O'Brien 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35802375/)). The write-up's "~38% adjusted real-world effect" comes from applying an assumed discount to testimonial results, so it is not a measurement (inferred).

**8. Diarrhoea in 10 to 20%: understated.** In the tuberous sclerosis trial, diarrhoea affected 31% of people on 25 mg/kg and 56% on 50 mg/kg versus 25% on placebo over 16 weeks, so 10 to 20% understates it at these doses. The write-up cites no source for blaming carrier oils.

**9. CBD:THC ratios of 20:1 or 28:1: no trial support.** I found no randomized trial comparing CBD:THC ratios in epilepsy. Extracts at 20:1 appear in uncontrolled Israeli cohorts ([Tzadok 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26800377/); [Hausman-Kedem 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29674131/)), and no epilepsy study abstract I searched mentions 28:1. In rats bred to have absence seizures, high-THC smoke and injected THC increased spike-and-wave discharges, the EEG signature of those seizures, while injected CBD cut them by about half and high-CBD smoke made no significant difference ([Roebuck 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33370468/)). That supports caution with THC in absence epilepsy without telling anyone which ratio is safe (inferred). ABRACE's director also told a committee of Brazil's Chamber of Deputies in November 2019 that in more than 30 tests of its THC nasal spray, children came out of seizures in under 30 seconds ([Chamber transcript](https://escriba.camara.leg.br/escriba-servicosweb/html/58445)); that is testimony, not a trial.

I did not search firsthand reports or community discussions for this answer: the question is whether published studies say what the summary claims, which firsthand reports cannot show.

A serious harm this answer did not check: suicidal thoughts and behaviour, a warning for antiseizure medicines.
