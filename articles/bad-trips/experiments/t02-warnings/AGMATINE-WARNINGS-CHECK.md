**How.** PubMed, 2026-11-03: agmatine with each drug and condition named, its human trials, and what it does in the
body. According to PubMed, the human evidence is small: one dose-escalation study and a randomized trial of 1.3 to 3.6 g
a day for up to three weeks, where safety measures stayed normal and the only side effects were mild-to-moderate diarrhea
and mild nausea in three people at the top dose, gone when they stopped
([Keynan et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1526-4637.2010.00808.x)); and two people, the report's own authors, taking 2.67 g
a day for five years with normal checkups
([Gilad & Gilad 2014](https://doi.org/10.1089/jmf.2014.0026)). Everything else below is animal or lab work. "No study
found" means the searches turned up nothing either way.

## In short

- **Something is behind it:** opioids and kratom, clonidine, guanfacine and blood-pressure drugs (and low blood
  pressure), nitrates and PDE-5 inhibitors (weakly), psychosis, and the stomach side effects.
- **Nothing found, or the studies point the other way:** MAOIs, bipolar disorder, unstable panic, relapse risk, and most
  of the "stop immediately" list.
- **Generic label lines, your call:** pregnancy and breastfeeding, kidney or liver disease, "complex psych stacks".
- **The protocol's own logic, not warnings:** the 1 g/day cap, and keeping agmatine away from D-serine and DCS days.

## Warning by warning

1. **What agmatine does** ("a multi-target neuromodulator that can affect NMDA/glutamate signaling, imidazoline/alpha-2
   adrenergic tone, nitric-oxide pathways, and opioid reward/tolerance"). Supported. It binds the same alpha-2 and
   imidazoline receptors as clonidine ([Regunathan & Reis 1996](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.pa.36.040196.002455)),
   blocks NMDA receptors and inhibits nitric oxide synthase
   ([Peterson et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymthe.2023.01.022)), and changes opioid pain relief, tolerance and
   dependence in animals ([Regunathan 2006](https://doi.org/10.1208/aapsj080356);
   [Wu et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10571-007-9164-y)). **Keep.**
2. **Opioids and kratom** (your version: "will reduce tolerance"). In animals agmatine made morphine's pain relief
   stronger, reduced dependence and withdrawal, and changed how tolerance builds (the same two reviews), so the same
   dose of an opioid could act stronger, which matters for someone who doses to their tolerance. Kratom wasn't studied with it, but it acts on
   opioid receptors too. No human report. **Keep**: "will reduce tolerance" is what the animal work found.
3. **Clonidine, guanfacine and blood-pressure medication.** By mechanism this one stands: agmatine binds clonidine's
   receptors, so with these drugs the pressure could drop further. No study of the combination, and the human trials'
   safety measures stayed normal. **Keep** (the "low blood pressure" caution in point 8 is the same point).
4. **Nitrates and PDE-5 inhibitors** (Viagra-type drugs). No study of agmatine with either. The thread is thin: in the
   lab agmatine relaxed rat arteries through nitric oxide
   ([Musgrave et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1196/annals.1304.027)) and raised nitric oxide in human blood-vessel cells
   ([Joshi et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0506824104)), while in rats with inflammation it lowered it
   ([El-Awady et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.etap.2017.08.009)). **Fold into "blood pressure lowering
   medications"**, which already says it, or cut.
5. **MAOIs.** No study of agmatine with an MAOI. MAO doesn't break agmatine down (neither MAO-A nor MAO-B, in rat brain),
   so the reason MAOIs are dangerous with some amines doesn't apply; the one link found is that phenelzine, an old MAOI
   antidepressant, blocked the enzyme that does break it down (diamine oxidase) in the same lab study
   ([Holt & Baker 1995](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0079-6123(08)61215-7)). For your readers "MAOIs" means harmalas: the
   only studies I found that gave agmatine with harmaline are in rodents, where agmatine reduced harmaline's tremor
   ([Akman et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neulet.2021.135881);
   [Pirmoradi et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/brb3.70241)). **Cut.**
6. **"Complex psychiatric medication stacks"** (yours: "other complex “psych” stacks without having a physician
   competent to consider the likelihoods of problematic interactions"). No particular drug behind it. Your clause is your
   own and read Human. **Your call**; it can carry the general caution for whatever is cut.
7. **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** No study either way; it's the standard supplement-label line. **Your call.**
8. **The conditions:**
   - *Psychosis history*: some basis. One study found more agmatine in the blood of 18 people with schizophrenia than in
     19 controls ([Uzbay et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2013.04.004)), and its lead author thinks
     excess agmatine may play a part in psychosis ([Uzbay 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2011.08.006)); a
     later study found less of it in early-onset schizophrenia
     ([Baytunca et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.schres.2020.05.006)). No report of agmatine supplements causing
     psychosis. **Keep, or soften.**
   - *Bipolar disorder*: no report of agmatine causing mania; a review proposes it as a possible add-on for bipolar
     depression, from animal work ([Watts et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/14728222.2019.1581764)). **Cut, or leave
     it with psychosis.**
   - *Unstable panic*: in animals agmatine acted against anxiety (Uzbay 2011). Nothing found. **Cut.**
   - *Low blood pressure*: by mechanism, as in point 3. **Keep.**
   - *Kidney or liver disease*: no study found. **Your call.**
   - *Relapse risk*: the animal work points the other way: agmatine eased withdrawal from several addictive drugs
     ([Uzbay 2012](https://doi.org/10.2174/092986712803530601); Regunathan 2006). No report of it raising relapse. **Cut.**
9. **When to stop** ("flatness, anhedonia, emotional blunting, new depression, agitation, insomnia, dizziness,
   palpitations, nausea, loose stools, diarrhea, or increased craving"). Only the stomach ones showed up in the trials.
   The rest aren't in the studies I found, and in animals agmatine acted against depression (Uzbay 2011). **If flatness
   or any of the others is your experience, or reports you trust, it stays**; otherwise the list shrinks to the stomach
   ones, and "If agmatine makes the world more gray, it is the wrong tool for that phase" keeps the protocol's point.
10. **The dose cap** ("cap at 1 g/day unless clinician-supervised"). The studied doses were higher (1.3 to 3.6 g a day
    for up to three weeks; 2.67 g a day for five years in two people). The cap is the protocol's own choice. **Your
    call**; "unless clinician-supervised" could go.
11. **Not on D-serine or DCS days** (12 to 24 hours apart). The protocol's logic, not a safety line: D-serine and DCS
    boost NMDA receptors and agmatine blocks them (Peterson 2023). No study of the combination. **Keep.**
