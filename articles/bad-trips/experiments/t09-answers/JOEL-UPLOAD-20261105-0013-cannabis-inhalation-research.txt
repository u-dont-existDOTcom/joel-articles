Cannabis inhalation for rapid seizure relief: new research
This continuation uncovered the strongest human evidence so far connecting smoked cannabis with objectively measured reductions in epileptic seizure activity. It also uncovered an experimental comparison showing that inhaled THC-rich cannabis can increase epileptic activity.

The central finding: Cannabis inhalation may rapidly suppress epileptic activity in some individuals, but the evidence does not yet establish which cannabinoids are responsible, which epilepsy types respond, or whether inhaling cannabis after seizure onset actually terminates that seizure.
1. A 2017 human EEG study is particularly relevant
Sivakumar et al. · Journal of Clinical Neurophysiology · PMID 27763968

Researchers followed a 24-year-old woman with drug-resistant generalized epilepsy using three long-term EEG recordings.

During two of those recordings, she smoked cannabis three times daily on specified days and abstained on other days.

The study therefore provides a real physiological signal, rather than simply asking patients whether cannabis helped. 
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov


However, three limitations are decisive.

First, it followed one person. The hourly observations are repeated measurements, not independent patients.

Second, she smoked while awake on scheduled days; researchers did not deliberately administer cannabis at the onset of a documented seizure to see whether that individual seizure stopped.

Third, the authors assumed their indica preparation had more CBD than the mixed indica/sativa product, but never chemically analyzed either product. In fact, a recent systematic review finds indica/sativa labels unreliable indicators of cannabinoid composition. 
Springer Nature Link


The appropriate conclusion is that smoked cannabis correlated with lower EEG seizure activity in this individual, not that vaporized CBD alone was responsible.

In the first patient, seizures were relatively controlled during habitual smoking. On admission to an epilepsy-monitoring unit, cannabis was stopped while his usual carbamazepine was continued.

The second patient experienced five EEG-confirmed focal seizures within 12 hours of cannabis cessation, three of which progressed to bilateral convulsions.

These are striking individual observations, although withdrawal, altered sleep, natural fluctuations and other circumstances cannot be completely separated.

Crucially, the cannabis reintroduction in the first patient was oral, not smoked or vaporized. It does not prove an immediate inhalation-rescue effect.

Read the 2012 paper


2015: EEG discharges disappeared again following resumed inhalation

A conference report described a 33-year-old man with refractory epilepsy who was taking levetiracetam and had an implanted vagus nerve stimulator.

His three-day ambulatory EEG while using inhaled marijuana contained no spike-wave discharges.

After more than a week abstinent, he had an average 29 spikes per ten-minute EEG epoch, with considerable variability. Following resumed cannabis use, discharges diminished, reaching zero during the last 12 hours of recording.

Because this comparison unfolded over days and included treatment changes during hospital monitoring, it is preliminary evidence of a cannabis-associated EEG effect, not proof of immediate seizure rescue. 
Neurology


Read conference abstract


These three reports are interesting together because they involve measured EEG rather than exclusively patient impressions. Yet they do not distinguish the effects of CBD, THC, other cannabis constituents, withdrawal and background treatments.

I also located a 2022 report of a person who already had a cannabis pipe in their hand when an aura began. They described taking a puff and the aura stopping immediately.

These cases are unusually pertinent to your question because they describe administration after recognizing early symptoms, rather than regular prevention. However, none includes a contemporaneous EEG, chemically tested composition, or a reliable comparison with similar untreated episodes.
The negative reports are equally important
A participant in a 2025 discussion said CBD by itself was not effective, whereas smoking cannabis sometimes helped on days with focal clusters.

Other participants reported that THC concentrates or vape cartridges appeared to provoke seizure symptoms, and some said their seizure control improved after stopping cannabis.

The evidence currently supports a narrower hypothesis: some people may benefit from inhaled mixed cannabis at an early aura or during clusters, but the effect appears variable, and CBD alone has not been identified as the necessary active component.
4. A real Brazilian THC rescue spray, but no validated effectiveness data
A separate lead deserves attention even though it is intranasal administration, not lung inhalation.

The Brazilian organization ABRACE Esperança historically offered a THC-rich product called Spray Resgate.

Its 2021 catalog explicitly described it as intended for control of convulsions already in progress. Different historical catalog versions show different formulations and concentrations, so I cannot establish one standard contemporary product. 
Medicina Clínica


A person posting on Mayo Clinic Connect said they had used a high-THC nasal spray since August 2020 to interrupt focal seizures.

That is a patient report hosted on a medical community website, not a Mayo Clinic clinical trial or endorsement. 
Mayo Clinic Connect


There is also a historical video about a child using the ABRACE THC nasal spray. I verified the video's title and associated claim, but its transcript and comments were unavailable, so I could not independently verify the depicted outcome.

Research significance: This establishes that some clinicians, patients and cannabis organizations have pursued THC-based on-demand treatment. It does not establish that the formulation works or acts fast enough.

Nasal delivery also cannot be assumed to have the same pharmacokinetics as pulmonary vaporization. The published pharmacokinetic evidence is specific to individual formulations and routes.
5. The animal inhalation results are not uniformly positive
The most relevant experiment is a study of inhaled cannabis in genetic absence-epilepsy rats.
Exposure	Actual result
High-THC cannabis smoke	Increased EEG spike-wave discharges
Low-THC/high-CBD cannabis smoke	No statistically significant suppression
Injected purified CBD	Approximately 50% reduction in discharges
Injected THC	Dose-dependent increase in discharges to over 200% of baseline


PubMed
This study raises an important pharmacological possibility: CBD could be anticonvulsant in that model, yet the amount absorbed during smoke exposure was insufficient to reproduce the injected CBD effect.

It also demonstrates why a claim that smoked cannabis is universally anticonvulsant is incorrect.

There is a seemingly contradictory 2003 temporal-lobe epilepsy rat study in which injected THC completely suppressed spontaneous seizures during the observation period. That study involved chronically epileptic rats and different seizure mechanisms.

These are opposing-direction results in different models, not evidence that one study must be wrong. The difference might depend on seizure syndrome, dose, exposure history, route or receptor signaling.

Neither experiment tested vaporized CBD after an ongoing human seizure began.
6. Safety and withdrawal: separating three mechanisms
A 2021 case report described a first generalized convulsive seizure in an adolescent who had been vaping a reported high-THC concentrate. The initial EEG was abnormal, then normalized after approximately one week without the product.

The concentrate was not chemically tested, and underlying vulnerability cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, the observation deserves consideration alongside favorable cannabis testimonials. 
PMC


A 2025 French pharmacovigilance study collected 127 eligible seizure-related reports associated with cannabinoid exposure. Approximately 3% of cannabinoid adverse-reaction reports involved seizures. That is emphatically not a 3% seizure risk among cannabis users. Most reports contained other plausible seizure-risk factors, and causation was not established. 
PMC


Withdrawal is a separate issue. A 2026 report describes convulsive status epilepticus roughly a week after abrupt cessation of long-term cannabis use. This is a case report, not proof that withdrawal caused it.

By comparison, a randomized CBD withdrawal study in healthy volunteers found no identifiable withdrawal syndrome after abruptly stopping four weeks of purified oral CBD. That does not resolve the effects of stopping decades of THC-rich cannabis exposure.

Finally, a 2022 prospective observational study of 29 people with refractory epilepsy found no significant overall benefit from oral high-CBD/low-THC formulations on seizure frequency, duration, recovery time or rescue-medication use. This was a maintenance study, not a test of inhaled rescue.

7. What the larger patient surveys and YouTube comments add
The 2025 Australian cannabis survey recruited 126 respondents who had already used cannabis for epilepsy. Among 124 who answered the outcome question, 83 reported improvement. Adult users frequently inhaled THC-containing preparations, while caregivers tended to administer oral CBD. 
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov


That is encouraging community signal, but it cannot estimate population response or acute rescue effectiveness because the participants were self-selected, formulations varied, and the question concerned overall change.

I also reviewed another 137 available YouTube comments and replies across four videos, including Cannabis & Seizures and Smoking's Impact on Epilepsy. Commenters reported both apparent seizure relief and suspected seizures after smoking or vaping. Several could not distinguish nicotine, THC, CBD and other exposures.

These comments added cautionary examples but no independently verified case of pure CBD inhalation terminating an active electrical seizure.
Synthesis: what should research prioritize?
Research candidate	Present evidence for rapid rescue
CBD-only vapor	Rapid absorption verified; post-onset seizure termination unproven
Mixed CBD:THC vapor	Direct aura/cluster testimonials; no objective rescue trial
THC-rich flower smoke	Some EEG associations with suppression, but significant contradictory safety evidence
High-THC concentrates	Insufficient rescue evidence and concerning provocation reports
Intranasal THC formulation	Historical rescue use documented; clinical efficacy and onset unverified


My research priority would be a controlled trial of analytically verified CBD-only inhalation and, separately, a characterized CBD:THC formulation, administered only by an eligible and fully alert patient at the onset of a recognized focal aware seizure. It would need objective EEG measures, minute-by-minute outcomes, an appropriate control and continued access to established rescue therapy.

The central distinction is between relieving an aura's unpleasant sensations, preventing it from progressing, and terminating the actual electrical seizure. The 2017 EEG study makes that research approach especially worthwhile, but it does not already answer the question.

An ongoing convulsive seizure or impaired awareness is not an appropriate situation to attempt smoking or vaping; first aid and prescribed rescue should take precedence. Convulsions lasting five minutes, or seizures recurring without recovery, require emergency attention.
