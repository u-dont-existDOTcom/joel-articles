The updated triage ladder

Start at the top. Do not skip a higher-priority layer because a lower one sounds more spiritual.

0. Body danger: time is real here

Call emergency services for collapse, prolonged or repeated seizure, breathing trouble, inability to wake, dangerous overheating, serious injury, one-sided weakness or speech loss, objective loss of bodily control, weapons/violence, or immediate danger. If someone reports possible brain damage, use DMSO right away while actively screening; objective signs or inability to assess mean urgent help in parallel. Use poison expertise for unknown substances or interaction concerns.

Do not make someone “sit with it” while a body is failing.

1. Pause unsafe momentum and lock out rash actions

Temporary states should not create permanent consequences. Pause roads, balconies, water, knives, stoves, driving, posting, texting, confessing, confronting, redosing, disappearing, giving away possessions, or proving anything tonight.

Action-lock line: “If this is true, it will still be true after sleep and breakfast.”

2. Room and setting: fix the obvious
  • Dim lights and reduce noise. Ask “music or quiet?”
  • If music helps, use something familiar and keep pause, volume, and track change immediately available. Steady, non-aggressive drumming may feel organizing; airy harp-like or meditative music may feel uplifting. Silence is valid.
  • Do not impose a playlist arc. Change or stop the track immediately if it begins steering the experience somewhere unwanted.
  • Remove hostile, overtalkative, or theatrical people; stop phone notifications.
  • Check temperature, clothing, water, privacy, bathroom/bucket access, and whether touch is wanted.
  • Speak slowly. Do not explain the universe at someone who is scared.
3. Stillness or safe movement: experiment, don’t force

Reduce unsafe movement, not movement itself. Try the safest option for two minutes and ask whether it makes the person safer and clearer or more activated and impulsive.

  • Stillness options: sit against a wall, lie under a blanket, hand on heart, hand on solar plexus.
  • Movement options: slow pacing, gentle stretching, shaking out arms/legs, wall push-ups, folding clothes, washing one cup, tidying one surface.
  • Parallel processing: when staring inward becomes a trap, let one part of attention stay with the body or feeling while another part completes one safe task with a visible endpoint—fold five towels, sort ten objects, draw one shape, or wash one cup.
  • Stop the task if it increases speed, agitation, dissociation, impulsivity, or risk. Do not use driving, cooking, knives, heights, water hazards, complex decisions, or strenuous exertion.
  • Avoid strenuous exercise if there is overheating, chest pain, faintness, stimulant/unknown drug combinations, severe confusion, or medical instability.
4. Human anchor: one lead, backup only if needed

One calm sober person is usually better than a crowd. If one person cannot safely cover safety, fatigue, logistics, violence risk, or self-harm risk, add one more calm adult or a crisis professional.

Script: “I’m in an intense state. I don’t need you to solve my life. Please stay with me, speak slowly, remind me where I am, and help me not make any big decisions until tomorrow.”

5. Grounding: body, room, breath, human, time
  • Orient: “I am in [place]. It is [time]. This will change.”
  • Hand on heart and solar plexus: give the body a simple signal — love is here.
  • Or hold something with weight or texture, or sit near a calm pet when that feels safe.
  • A familiar note, childhood toy, stuffed animal, blanket, or other object may serve as a reminder of the Nurturer or Protector if its meaning was established while sober. The object can remind you; it does not speak, know the truth, or interpret the trip.
  • Pair the object with one adult action: contact the grounded person, move away from danger, prepare for sleep, or complete another concrete promise.
  • Try longer exhales first. If overbreathing is obvious, gentle Buteyko-style reduced breathing may help: breathe softly and slowly through the nose, taking in slightly less air than you want until there is faint air hunger. Never strain or use long breath-holds while intoxicated.
  • Look at one ordinary object and describe it plainly. Do not introduce a childhood photograph or search it for hidden memories during an acute difficult trip.
5A. Music: familiar, optional, and immediately changeable

Music can influence an altered state strongly. Use it as an optional support rather than a prescribed emotional journey.

  • Ask “music or quiet?” and prefer something the person already knows and trusts.
  • Keep pause, volume, and track change within reach. If the music pulls the experience somewhere unwanted, change it immediately.
  • Steady, non-aggressive drumming may help a scattered body find a rhythm. Airy harp-like, spacious, or meditative music may feel more spiritually uplifting.
  • Those are choices, not promised effects. Silence may be best.
  • Do not automatically impose a nonverbal-to-lyrics progression or rise/peak/descent arc. Lyrics can become suggestion; use them only when chosen and helpful.

6. Euphoric certainty: dangerous states do not always feel bad

“I am God,” “I am chosen,” or “everyone must hear this tonight” may arrive with bliss. Bliss is not proof of safety. Mission energy plus sleep loss deserves more caution, not less.

  • No posting, driving, confronting, confessing, redosing, disappearing, or testing theories tonight.
  • Ask: “Have I slept?” and “Has this happened before—and how did it end?”
  • Protect sleep as the first intervention. The revelation can wait.
7. Content that turns toward harm: redirect from inside the value-world

Do not try to win a metaphysical argument. Also do not agree with harmful conclusions. Reflect the feeling, then redirect to safety, peace, non-harm, sleep, and ordinary reality.

Example: “I hear how vast and certain this feels. Many traditions say God is in everyone. That makes every body sacred, not disposable. Peace is the godly move tonight. No proof is needed.”

Escalate if there is a specific target, weapon, movement toward action, escalating violence, or the sitter cannot safely contain the situation.

8. Body state: do not turn physiology into mythology
  • Check heat, dehydration, exhaustion, sleep deprivation, pain, hunger/fullness, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and interactions.
  • A “toxic” or “dirty” feeling is a description, not proof of toxins, parasites, or “die-off.”
  • If fully alert and not vomiting, small sips may be reasonable; never force fluids.
  • Unknown or concerning ingestion → Poison Control or local toxicology advice.
9. Emotional material: love without interrogation

If the body is safe and real grief, shame, fear, or trauma is surfacing, use calm presence—not analysis every thirty seconds.

  • “This feeling is allowed to move through; you do not have to become it.”
  • “Can I help make the room safer or quieter?”
  • Hand on heart and solar plexus only if the person wants it; let it be a signal of love, not forced positivity.
  • Do not trap someone in suffering because it has been labeled “healing.”
10. Hand on heart, inner-child love, and gratitude 🫶🏿

This is a deep question, not always the first practical move. If the person is too sick, hot, confused, impulsive, or terrified, stabilize first. Then invite loving awareness.

Putting a hand on the heart can change the direction of the trip, especially with emotional contraction, grief, fear, or loneliness. The point is not to fake loving thoughts. The point is to give the body a simple signal: love is here.

  • Put one hand on the heart. Put the other hand on the solar plexus, just under that hand, to send love to the inner child.
  • Useful prompt: “Can I send love to the one who is suffering?”
  • Once love reaches the inner child, it can widen to all beings who need it right now. Recognizing you are not the only one suffering can open the field of awareness in a liberating way.
  • Gratitude comes in like icing on the cake: gratitude for lessons, difficult experiences, reprieves, heart openings, and compassion.
  • Do not force gratitude if it feels fake, premature, or shaming. Stabilize first; let gratitude become real.
11. Inner Signal: guided self-hypnosis when technology is still workable

If the trip is not medically dangerous, the room is safe, the action-lock is in place, and the person can use a phone or simple web app without getting more confused, Inner Signal can serve as the guided inner-work layer.

  • Best fit: grief, shame, fear, protector parts, inner-child material, looping emotional content, or a clear desire to listen inward.
  • Use it gently: slow prompts, no recovered-memory forcing, no pressure to find cosmic meaning.
  • Useful supports may include a familiar reminder object, one bounded parallel-processing task, or familiar user-controlled music. The object and music do not become authorities.
  • Do not introduce a childhood photograph during an acute difficult trip. Photograph mode belongs to planned, regulated work with explicit memory-source labeling.
  • Not a fit: chest pain, seizure risk, overheating, serotonin-toxicity signs, mania/psychosis-level danger, severe confusion, or any situation where screens worsen overload.

12. After the storm: integrate before interpreting
  • Sleep, water, simple food, walking, journaling, prayer/meditation, and one grounded conversation.
  • No major decisions, self-diagnoses, re-dosing, public posting, or literalizing every vision immediately.
  • Ask later: what was medical, environmental, emotional, old trauma, fantasy, and actually useful?

Before a planned session: give it a beginning and an end

This is prevention for deliberate inner work or altered-state sessions. Once somebody is already in trouble, use the acute triage above.

Container sequence: preparation → physical safety → intention → low-directivity support → closure → sleep and food → next-day integration.
Rest and timing. Do not begin deep work already depleted. When practical, start earlier in the day so there is time for the experience, ordinary contact, food, and sleep. This does not apply mechanically to bedtime hypnosis or a session intended to end in sleep.
Physical safety. Know the substance and approximate amount when relevant, review combinations and medical concerns, prepare the room, arrange sober support, and decide in advance what requires outside help.
Intention without a script. Name what you are open to exploring without demanding a revelation, recovered memory, catharsis, emotional arc, forgiveness, or spiritual result.
Low-directivity support. The sitter protects safety and offers choices. They do not force music, touch, photographs, interpretation, confession, prayer, or insight.
Music remains optional. Prefer familiar music, quiet, or a track that can be changed immediately. Do not impose an MDMA-style playlist arc.
Closure. Stop adding stimulation, return to the real room and date, restore voluntary movement and ordinary alertness unless the endpoint is sleep, eat and hydrate when safe, and write down basic facts before interpretation.
Integration time. Leave the rest of the day and, when possible, the next morning relatively free. Sleep and food come before a new life plan.

Serotonin-toxicity warning signs

Medical emergency pathway

After exposure to a serotonergic medicine or drug, concerning neuromuscular signs matter more than anxiety alone:

  • Spontaneous rhythmic jerking/clonus.
  • Clonus or rapid eye movements with agitation or heavy sweating.
  • Tremor with markedly brisk reflexes.
  • Rigidity with temperature above 38°C.
  • Severe confusion, escalating agitation, seizure, collapse, or breathing problems.
Action: Stop taking more suspected substances, call emergency services or Poison Control, move to a cooler low-stimulation area without delaying transport, prevent exertion, and bring all packaging/medication details.
Do not delay: cooling, reduced exertion, airway protection, or urgent care. If the person is fully alert and able to swallow, the guide gives cyproheptadine early, alongside the cooling and the help: case reports describe rapid improvement, while a recent systematic review found the evidence for major outcomes inconclusive. Nothing by mouth with an impaired airway, an active seizure, uncontrolled vomiting, or a caustic ingestion.

When to bring in mental-health or crisis support

Crisis / clinical support
  • The person may be unsafe to themselves or others, or there is a specific target, means, weapon, or movement toward action.
  • Grandiosity, pressured speech, mission certainty, or “I am God/chosen” energy escalates with little or no sleep.
  • Psychosis-like symptoms continue after the expected acute drug state.
  • Sleep does not return and energy, agitation, paranoia, or disorganization escalates.
  • Suicidal thinking, severe panic, dissociation, or inability to function appears afterward.
  • The trauma material is larger than the current room or sitter can safely hold.

Aftercare timeline

Next 24–72 hours

Sleep as intervention. Hydration, simple food, no driving while impaired, no re-dosing, no posting/confessing/confronting/proving, and one calm person who can check in.

Within 1–2 weeks

Review substance, dose, combinations, body state, setting, sleep loss, and whether the main aftermath is anxiety, DP/DR, sleep disruption, visual distortion, shame/social withdrawal, existential shock, trauma material, or paranoia/psychosis-like belief.

Within 4–12 weeks

Seek clinical review if sleep, mood, anxiety, dissociation, psychosis-like symptoms, or functioning remain impaired. Integration should reduce suffering and improve behavior—not merely produce a more cosmic story.

Author’s rescue notes—with emergency routing clearly marked

This section records the guide’s evidence tiers and field experience. DMSO, psychedelic trip termination with Ipomoea pes-caprae, and the MDMA magnesium/electrolyte branch now appear in the live routing where specified; exact preparation and dosing remain in the full guide or an existing rescue plan.

Safety boundary: Use the live branch, not a random stack. DMSO goes alongside imaging and emergency treatment; there are no data yet that pes-caprae aborts MDMA; magnesium and other oral measures need full consciousness and safe swallowing.
DMSO for possible acute neurological injury

Live route: if an altered person says the brain feels damaged, broken, or out of control, use DMSO right away while checking wakefulness, breathing, smile, both arms, speech, walking, bodily control, seizure, temperature, chest symptoms, vomiting, and vision.

  • Objective signs or unreliable examination: use DMSO while emergency evaluation is activated. Do not wait to see whether it helps.
  • Reassuring screen but continuing concern: DMSO remains the author’s first immediate measure, followed by the drug-specific branch.
  • Evidence boundary: repeated personal experience plus preclinical and limited human signals do not identify ischemic versus hemorrhagic stroke or replace imaging and time-sensitive care.
Ipomoea pes-caprae: rapid psychedelic trip termination

Primary role: the guide describes Ipomoea pes-caprae as its strongest rapid psychedelic trip-abortion measure—the sledgehammer used when safety matters more than preserving the trip.

  • For “too intense” with a reassuring screen and a desire to preserve the trip, use calming and stabilizing measures first.
  • For a psychedelic brain-damage concern, use DMSO first, then beach morning glory (pes-caprae) to stop the trip: in the author’s experience a whole leaf can easily end it.
  • It can reduce psychedelic nausea, but it is not the routine nausea remedy because the difference between nausea relief and trip termination is narrow in the author’s experience.
  • The guide currently has no relevant data for MDMA. Do not generalize it.
MDMA jaw tension: magnesium and broader electrolytes

Live route: meaningful MDMA jaw or muscle tension is treated as a magnesium-responsive early warning. Stop redosing, reduce heat and exertion, and—when fully conscious and able to swallow—take magnesium chloride until the jaw fully releases.

  • The author has dissolved Dead Sea magnesium chloride hexahydrate flakes in water and taken approximately one gram per minute orally for around twenty minutes before his jaw fully released. He has tolerated around twenty grams without diarrhea; for him, diarrhea appeared only above roughly forty grams. A token dose may not be enough.
  • Serious MDMA toxicity may involve sodium, potassium, magnesium, temperature, hydration, serotonin toxicity, rhabdomyolysis, rhythm disturbance, and co-ingestants.
  • Do not blindly replace potassium or drink excessive plain water.
  • People with impaired kidneys need to be more cautious with large, rapid magnesium doses, because they may not clear magnesium normally, but they should be far more worried about the MDMA: treat the situation as higher-risk and get help sooner.
Zeolite and ZHoly3O / ferments for gut-driven bad trips
Author field note Not emergency care

When the guide thinks of it: sick stomach, nausea, diarrhea, toxic heaviness, dirty body-feeling, parasite-die-off feeling, or mental hell that seems driven by gut hell.

  • Ferment layer: many ferments may help to different degrees, including kombucha, kimchi, apple vinegar, and fermented beet juice. The author prefers fermented beet juice made with apple vinegar and salt.
  • Smell first: the guide emphasizes smelling the ferment as a low-burden first step. A spoonful may also be useful if the person is alert and not vomiting.
  • Itching caveat: the author reports that drinking ferment can cause itching in some people, interpreted in the guide as a possible histamine-processing issue. In that situation, the author reports using activated charcoal to reduce gut histamine. This is a field note, not a diagnosis.
  • Charcoal boundary: activated charcoal can bind medicines and other substances and has aspiration risk if someone is vomiting, drowsy, seizing, or unable to protect their airway. Do not treat it as a casual add-on or emergency antidote. Poison Control; clinical review.
  • Zeolite quality: in the author’s experiments, powder zeolite worked differently by source/quality; many products did not work, and the author does not use nano-liquid zeolite for this purpose.
  • Ozonated zeolite preparation: the author mixes powdered zeolite into water, then ozonates the water in a glass vessel. The guide is describing ozonated water preparation, not inhaling ozone gas. Ozone gas is a respiratory hazard. NIOSH ozone guide.
  • Timing reported by the author: with a machine producing about 2 grams of ozone per hour, about 10 minutes of ozonation produced a working zeolite; less time did not work as well, and more time did not noticeably improve it.
  • Glass and non-metal contact: the author uses glass for ozonation/storage and avoids metal utensils/vessels before ingestion. Zeolites are used as adsorbents for heavy-metal removal, but that does not prove this acute trip use. Zeolite heavy-metal adsorption review.
  • How the guide uses it: put a spoonful of prepared zeolite into water and sip slowly; some people may use closer to a teaspoon, others a tablespoon. The author also reports rubbing zeolite on the body or holding the zeolite jar as a sensory/field effect. Do not use by mouth if vomiting, confused, drowsy, or medically unstable.

Evidence boundary: this section records the author’s personal rescue notes. It is not established medical treatment for psychedelic distress, poisoning, infection, parasites, histamine intolerance, or psychiatric crisis.

Syrian rue smell, incense, and oil for negative-energy or entity-like distress
Author field note Traditional incense practice MAOI exposure not ruled out

Traditional and personal context: Syrian rue (Peganum harmala, espand/esfand) has a long Middle Eastern and Central/South Asian history of fumigation and ritual use against the evil eye, harmful spirits, or negative influence. Archaeological chemical evidence documents harmal fumigation in Arabia about 2,700 years ago. That establishes a longstanding practice, not proof of efficacy for a modern bad trip. 2025 Communications Biology study.

  • Smell-first field note: the author reports that directly smelling intact seeds or a familiar rue preparation can reduce a sensed negative-energy/entity-like quality and improve mood without necessarily ending the trip. This is a subjective personal signal; controlled human evidence for acute psychedelic distress was not located.
  • Incense boundary: if rue is burned traditionally, a sober helper—not the altered person—should control the flame, use a small amount with ventilation, and stop if smoke worsens coughing, chest tightness, headache, dizziness, or panic. Avoid concentrated smoke, enclosed-room exposure, asthma/COPD, pregnancy, children, and any situation involving respiratory symptoms or fire risk. Harmine has been identified in rue smoke, and smoke condensate showed cytotoxicity in a cell model; that does not quantify real-world risk, but it argues against treating smoke as harmless. Rue-smoke study.
  • Oil field note: the author makes a coconut-oil maceration and reports mood-lifting and antipsychotic-like calming effects from smelling it or external contact, with stronger trip-reducing effects from higher-contact use. This app does not call it an antipsychotic treatment and does not operationalize ingestion, mucosal application, or a quantity.
  • Interaction uncertainty: there is no evidence that a home coconut-oil preparation removes MAO-A interaction risk. Extracted P. harmala seed oil has contained harmine, harmaline, harmalol, and harmol, while seed extracts are potent reversible MAO-A inhibitors. Route-specific absorption from a home oil or mucosal contact has not been quantified. Treat meaningful systemic exposure as potentially interacting until demonstrated otherwise. Seed-oil analysis; human MAO-A inhibition study.
  • Do not use it to manage dangerous psychosis: entity language, grandiosity, or paranoia with a target, weapon, command to harm, severe disorganization, inability to maintain safety, or prolonged loss of sleep belongs in the crisis/emergency pathway. Do not delay help to test rue.
  • Avoid adding rue exposure during an unresolved overdose/interaction concern, serotonin-toxicity concern, pregnancy, severe cardiovascular symptoms, or when serotonergic, stimulant, MAOI-like, or unknown combinations are involved.

Evidence boundary: traditional use and the author’s personal experience are meaningful signals, but they do not establish efficacy, an antipsychotic effect, a non-interacting oil preparation, or a safe mucosal dose.

Aged ozonated-charcoal water, boiled charcoal water, and shungite contact
Author field note Do not inhale ozone Do not drink untested shungite water

When the guide thinks of it: mental intensity or overload where the person is not medically unstable, not vomiting/drowsy/confused, and not in a crisis that needs emergency, poison-center, or clinical support.

  • Aged ozonated-charcoal water: the author reports this as a personal tool for calming mental overload without necessarily ending the trip. This is a field note, not an established antidote or emergency treatment.
  • Ozone boundary: this refers to treating water/charcoal in a glass vessel, not breathing ozone gas. Ozone gas is a respiratory hazard; inhalation can injure the lungs and can cause chest pain, coughing, throat irritation, and shortness of breath. EPA ozone safety discussion; NIOSH ozone guide.
  • If there is no ozone water purifier: the author reports that boiling water with charcoal in it may give some of the same benefits. Treat this as a personal rescue note, not as poison-treatment charcoal or a validated psychiatric/psychedelic intervention.
  • Charcoal boundary: charcoal can bind medicines and other substances and creates aspiration risk if someone is vomiting, drowsy, seizing, or unable to protect their airway. Do not force anything by mouth when alertness or airway safety is unclear. Poison Control; clinical review.
  • Shungite contact: the author reports that touching or holding shungite, including a pendant or stone, can feel similar to holding cleaned zeolite. This is a sensory/field-effect field note, not medical care.
  • Shungite drinking caution: avoid drinking shungite water unless the shungite has been properly washed and quality-checked. A drinking-water study found that shungite can leach metals including nickel, copper, lead, cadmium, zinc, chromium, and arsenic into water, with lead/cadmium and nickel exceeding acceptable levels in that study for a period of time. Shungite drinking-water study.
