## Section by section

### When to Bring In Outside Help

**Standard (19 lines).** The call-medics list, the mental-health list and the emergency-number lines match standard
guidance: [CDC seizure first aid](https://www.cdc.gov/epilepsy/first-aid-for-seizures/index.html), the
[American Heart Association's heart attack signs](https://www.heart.org/health-topics/heart-attack/warning-signs-of-a-heart-attack),
the [American College of Emergency Physicians' warning signs](https://www.emergencyphysicians.org/siteassets/files/pdfs/isitanemergency.pdf),
an [NHS ambulance service's 999 list](https://www.swast.nhs.uk/when-to-call-999/), and
[NAMI's 988 page](https://nami.org/nami-988-faqs-updated-07-12-2023) (988 counselors "help de-escalate the crisis"; physical
danger is for 911). For "psychosis-like symptoms outlast the acute state", a review finds lasting psychosis after
psychedelics rare but recognised ([Schlag et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/02698811211069100)). **Keep.**

1. **"Concerning symptoms after an MAOI plus a serotonergic substance."** Supported. Serious serotonin toxicity comes
   mostly from a serotonin-raising drug taken with an MAOI ([Malcolm & Thomas 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-021-05876-x)),
   and a review of 219 publications lists deaths from MAOIs with MDMA, tramadol, 5-MeO-DMT and others
   ([Rached et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40263-025-01256-7)). **Keep.**
2. **"The trauma material is bigger than the room can hold"** and **"identify the local emergency number and crisis line
   before you ever need them."** Nothing to check. **Your views; they stay.**

### Acute Medical and Neurological Rescue: the opening

3. **"Some severe but physically stable altered states may respond faster to field rescue than to hours in an emergency
   room"** and **"for a physiologically stable psychedelic crisis it may be a poor environment."** No study compares the
   two. A 2026 framework paper places peer support for "medically stable but psychologically distressed" people
   "alongside rather than in place of" medical care; its field data, surveys of medical staff at two events, are staff
   perceptions, not measured outcomes ([Veiseh et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1927839)). **Your view; it
   stays.**
4. **"calming and stabilizing the trip may be enough"** (when breathing, temperature and the rest are fine). Consistent
   with that approach, and with classic psychedelics' low medical risk (Schlag 2022). **Keep.**
5. **Benzodiazepines in the ER** ("don't reliably address psychosis and can cause paradoxical agitation or fear in some
   people"). Supported. A Swiss emergency guide uses them first for agitation
   ([Bless et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxics13121034)); a Cochrane review calls the trial evidence for
   benzodiazepines alone in psychosis "not good" ([Zaman et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD003079.pub4));
   paradoxical reactions happen in under 1 in 100 people
   ([Mancuso et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1592/phco.24.13.1177.38089)). **Keep.**
6. **"Antipsychotics may work better for psychosis, but they carry their own risks and interactions."** The risks are
   real: chlorpromazine can drop blood pressure hard, and antipsychotics can themselves set off serotonin syndrome
   ([Scotton et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1178646919873925)). "May work better" rests on thin trials: one small
   study favoured olanzapine, and haloperidol did no better than benzodiazepines (Zaman 2017). **Keep**; "may" already
   carries that.

### If Someone Says "My Brain Feels Damaged"

7. **"Do not dismiss that report because the person is high."** Supported: drug users have more strokes of both kinds,
   mostly with stimulants, less often with cannabis and other psychotomimetic drugs
   ([Fonseca & Ferro 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11910-012-0325-0)). **Keep.**
8. **Standard (6 lines):** the neurological check (it covers the balance and vision checks that cut missed strokes from 14%
   to 4.4%, [Aroor et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1161/STROKEAHA.116.015169)), "a person who cannot answer ... has not
   passed the screen", "while someone calls medics", "DMSO cannot identify whether the problem is a clot, a hemorrhage",
   the treatment window, and "Bring in medical help in parallel"
   ([AHA/ASA stroke guideline, Powers et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1161/STR.0000000000000211)). **Keep.**
9. **Your bleed lines** ("If someone is worried about a bleed, I'd probably skip DMSO..."): settled, they stay.
   **"Do not manufacture reassurance from missing information":** generic; it waits for the humanizing.

### Active Seizure

10. **Standard:** timing it, the side position, nothing in the mouth, no restraint, and the call-medics list (CDC); "the
    established first-line drug class for status epilepticus", which the cited review says
    ([Kienitz et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40263-022-00940-2)). **Keep.**
11. **"An ordinary pill is not the same thing as a ... rescue preparation, and respiratory depressants in the mix change
    the risk."** Supported. The rescue forms are the fast routes (Kienitz 2022), and the FDA warns that opioids with
    benzodiazepines can cause "slowed or difficult breathing, coma, or death"
    ([FDA, 2016](https://www.fda.gov/Drugs/DrugSafety/ucm518473.htm)). **Keep.**
12. **"transdermal CBD reaches systemic circulation, although it has not been validated as rapid seizure rescue."** The
    cited trial does find blood levels that "overlap those reported with oral cannabidiol", but its gel did no better than
    placebo against focal seizures over 12 weeks in 188 adults
    ([O'Brien et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.20189)). **Change** (card W-CBD): "In a 12-week
    trial, a CBD skin gel reached the blood at levels like oral CBD's but did no better than placebo against focal
    seizures, and it hasn't been tested as rapid seizure rescue."
13. **DMSO in the two seizure models.** Both cited studies say what the line says: less electrically induced seizure
    activity in the rat temporal-lobe model ([Carletti et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neulet.2013.04.031)), and
    more absence seizures at high doses in a rat absence-epilepsy strain
    ([Kovács et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jneumeth.2011.02.005)). Human case reports add to the
    caution: seizures during infusions of DMSO-preserved stem cells
    ([Maral et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10561-018-9718-x)). **Keep.** "I treat it as a plausible experimental
    adjunct" is yours.

### MDMA: Jaw Tension Is the Magnesium Alarm

14. **"Magnesium chloride is also generally less likely to cause diarrhea than several other replacement salts."** The
    cited review says it, in its full text: gluconate and chloride "cause diarrhea less often than other salt forms"
    ([Costello et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.advnut.2023.06.008)). **Keep.**
15. **"Jaw tension should also mean no more MDMA, less exertion and heat, and closer monitoring."** Supported. MDMA's
    side effects rise with dose, and 19% of 166 volunteers went over 38°C
    ([Vizeli & Liechti 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0269881117691569)); in an Amsterdam ICU, the most common reason MDMA
    users were admitted was jaw clenching so severe it threatened the airway, in 47% of 74
    ([Zuidema et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/15563650.2025.2453619)). **Keep.**
16. **"Worsening headache, confusion, vomiting ... raises concern about sodium, potassium, temperature, hydration,
    kidneys."** Supported: these are the complications MDMA patients arrive with (Zuidema 2025; Bless 2025). **Keep.**
17. **MDMA and the kidneys, and low sodium after a single dose.** Both cited studies say this: kidney injury through
    muscle breakdown, malignant (emergency-level) high blood pressure and inflamed vessels
    ([Bora et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5126214/), no DOI in PubMed), and low sodium in 31% of 96
    people after one dose, none of the 15 whose fluids were limited
    ([Atila et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.45278)). "MDMA can cause overheating,
    rhabdomyolysis ..." is the same evidence. **Keep.**
18. **"People with impaired kidneys do need to be more cautious with large, rapid magnesium doses."** Supported: magnesium
    poisoning "commonly occurs in patients with renal dysfunction"
    ([Si et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2024.1416956)). The case reports add that it has happened with normal
    kidneys, after a very large dose ([Nordt et al. 1996](https://doi.org/10.3109/15563659609013838)) or in an older
    woman with a blocked bowel ([Kontani et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.2169/internalmedicine.44.448)). **Keep**; you
    could add a blocked or slow gut.
19. **"Kidney impairment is a strong reason not to take MDMA."** No study of MDMA in people with kidney disease; it
    follows from MDMA's kidney injury (Bora 2016). **Keep.**
20. **Your experience (9 lines):** jaw tension as the earliest warning, the people who avoided complications, one gram a
    minute, 20 and 40 grams, less euphoria, your second wife's potassium, the broader electrolyte emergency, and what
    you'd tell someone with bad kidneys. Studies that bear on them:
    - No trial tests magnesium for MDMA jaw clenching. It is one of the most common things users take
      ([Allott & Redman 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2005.09.009);
      [Goldy et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-026-07187-5)), and the ICU series in item 15 backs treating
      the jaw seriously.
    - For scale: the flakes are about 12% magnesium, so 20 g is about 2.4 g of magnesium in twenty minutes. The
      1997-2022 trials Costello reviewed used up to 1.2 g a day; an older study gave up to 4.7 g a day over meals. With
      normal kidneys and gut, dangerous levels from magnesium by mouth are rare (Costello 2023; Nordt 1996).
    - Potassium: after one 100 mg dose, 6 of 30 people had mild, passing low potassium (2 of 15 healthy volunteers and 4
      of 15 patients with central diabetes insipidus;
      [Atila et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(23)00120-1)).

### Chest Pain and Injury

21. **"DMSO can also block pain signals."** Supported: on cat nerves, DMSO blocked the fibres that carry pain from 9%
    ([Evans et al. 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3940(93)90522-m)). **Keep.**
22. **"Mild, familiar, non-progressive discomfort ... is different from new, severe ... pain."** The second branch matches
    the AHA signs, and "arranging medical evaluation" is standard. The AHA says "Call 911 if you have any symptoms of heart
    attack", notes that some heart attacks "start slowly with mild pain or discomfort", and adds "Even if you're not sure
    it's a heart attack, get checked out". So leaving the first branch without a call is **your call**: card W-CHEST
    suggests one line for the cases a reader can't place.
23. **Your erythritol angina** is yours. A study bears on it: in people, higher blood erythritol was linked to heart
    attack and stroke, and in lab tests, on human platelets and in mice, it made blood clot more readily
    ([Witkowski et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-023-02223-9)).

### Recent Oral Toxic Ingestion

24. **Charcoal "may help after some recent oral ingestions"** and **"It does not bind every toxin, can adsorb necessary
    medication, and creates aspiration risk."** The cited review says it doesn't bind every toxin, that it carries an
    aspiration risk, and that it works within about the first hour
    ([Zellner et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3238/arztebl.2019.0311)); the Swiss guide adds the aspiration risk "in
    agitated or sedated patients" (Bless 2025), and charcoal lowers an antidepressant's levels, for one (Knadler 2011, item
    34). **Keep.**
25. **Standard:** asking what was swallowed, and poison control. **Keep.** "I do not treat it as a mandatory ritual" is
    yours; the cited review itself says "a poison control center should be consulted" about charcoal.

### Suspected Serotonin Syndrome (SS)

26. **"Psychedelics, MDMA, DXM, many antidepressants, tramadol, linezolid, MAOIs, and some opioids can contribute."** All
    are on Scotton 2019's table of drugs tied to serotonin syndrome, LSD and Syrian rue with harmine included. But
    "Psychedelics" is broader than the risk: true toxicity typically needs an overdose or an MAOI, and serotonergic drugs
    without an MAOI "are low risk in combination with psychedelics that also do not contain MAOIs" (Malcolm & Thomas
    2022). **Change** (card W-SS): "Ayahuasca and other MAOI brews" in place of "Psychedelics", and "other MAOIs".
27. **Cyproheptadine.** Both cited studies say what the line says: 3 of 5 cases fully resolved within 2 hours
    ([Graudins et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0736-4679(98)00057-2)), and a 2025 review found its "efficacy was not
    established" ([King & Rotella 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/1742-6723.14554)). **Keep.**
28. **"Fever reducers do not solve heat generated by extreme muscle activity"** and **"muscle activity can worsen heat and
    rhabdomyolysis risk."** Supported: fever reducers "have little effect and are not indicated", and holding someone down
    makes heat and acid build up (Scotton 2019). **Keep.**
29. **"Niacin is unsafe for serotonin syndrome."** No study of niacin in serotonin syndrome. But the caution has a basis:
    a niacin compound released serotonin from human platelets, and in a rat model of the flush, niacin raised blood
    serotonin and serotonin blockers cut its temperature rise by 85-90%
    ([Papaliodis et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1124/jpet.108.141333)). That's the flush's warming, not shown to be the
    heat that matters in serotonin syndrome. **Keep**.
30. **Standard:** the Hunter criteria ([Dunkley et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1093/qjmed/hcg109); the eye movements are
    "fine or coarse, oscillatory" in Scotton 2019, so "rapid" is close enough), stopping the drugs, calling for help,
    cooling (ice packs at the neck and armpits in [the NHS's advice](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heat-exhaustion-and-heatstroke/),
    and at the neck, armpit and groin in [a CDC heat-illness page](https://www.cdc.gov/nceh/hsb/extreme/Heat_Illness/page492.html)),
    no bath (CDC treats a seizure in water
    as an emergency), no forced fluids, and the hospital list. **Keep.** "This is a recognition aid" and "do not necessarily
    treat it" are generic and wait for the humanizing; "DMSO is also not part of this branch" is your view; oral magnesium
    in SS is yours (no study found).

### My Acute Rescue Hierarchy

"Try all these things in very low, increasing doses" is yours, from your round-1 answer.

**Zeolite, ferment and charcoal**

31. **The ferment "may cause itching in somebody low in histamine-processing bacteria, which then requires activated
    charcoal."** Partly supported. Fermented foods can be high in histamine, and itching is a sign of histamine
    intolerance, but the review on it puts the intolerance down mainly to low levels of DAO, an enzyme of the gut lining
    (one study it cites found low DAO in 80% of 316 people with symptoms); the bacteria it discusses are the ones that make
    histamine, in food ([Comas-Basté et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom10081181)). Charcoal took out 61% of the
    histamine in fish sauce ([In et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2024.140169)); no study tests it in the
    gut, so that part stays yours. **Change** (card W-ITCH): "somebody who breaks down histamine poorly" in place of
    "somebody low in histamine-processing bacteria"
32. **"never inhaling ozone gas"** and **"I don't recommend inhaling ozone."** Supported: "When inhaled, ozone can damage
    the lungs" ([US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/ozone-generators-are-sold-air-cleaners)). **Keep.**
33. **"Zeolite adsorbs metals."** Supported: clinoptilolite is the zeolite most used to take lead, cadmium, mercury and
    arsenic out of water ([Velarde et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemosphere.2023.138508)), and in rats it lowered
    metals in the organs ([Dolanc et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology12020193)). **Keep**; keeping it off metal is
    your precaution.
34. **Charcoal "adsorbs drugs" and "can also adsorb medications somebody depends on."** Supported (Zellner 2019); it lowers
    an antidepressant's levels, for one ([Knadler et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.2165/11539240-000000000-00000)).
    **Keep.** "It can also flatten or reduce a trip" is yours; it would work only on what is still in the gut, mostly in
    the first hour (Bless 2025).

**Coffee cherry**

35. **"I wouldn't reach for it first when somebody is already becoming manic."** Yours, and the studies back it: coffee
    cherry contains caffeine ([Fitriansyah et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3923/pjbs.2026.289.299)), and in case reports
    caffeine has preceded manic switches in bipolar disorder
    ([Frigerio et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/bdi.12990)).

**Beach morning glory (*Ipomoea pes-caprae*)**

36. **The narrow margin, the skin effect, "a whole leaf", "no data on MDMA"** are yours; no study of its effect on trips
    exists. One study bears on its safety ([Llerenas-Aguirre et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18142248), read in
    full): mice carrying human breast tumors, bred without a working immune system, drank a tea of its leaves every day, at
    a dose scaled from the traditional Mexican tea of 12 to 14 dried leaves in a liter a day. By day 10, 4 of 10 had
    blistering skin sores; by day 15, 7 of 10 had died, and the tea was stopped. A single large dose did 7 healthy mice no
    immediate harm, and the authors warn that sick mice may be more vulnerable than people. That's daily dosing in sick
    mice, not a nibble, but the guide has no word on the plant's own safety: card W-GLORY offers one.

**Metta NMT sniffer, humic acid, ocean water:** no warnings in these subsections.

**Shungite and charcoal water**

37. **"Shungite should not be drunk unless it has been properly washed of heavy metals."** The warning has a basis:
    shungite carries lead and arsenic in its structure ([Gusmão et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/chem.201703974)).
    It is also used to filter metals out of water and milk ([Skrypnik et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/antiox10071121);
    [Bazanova et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods15193413)). No study found measures what gets into water it sits in,
    or what washing does. **Keep**: it's a precaution with a basis, and "unless washed" is neither backed nor contradicted.
    Smelling ozone by mistake is yours.

**DMSO, side effects**

38. **Skin irritation, nausea and body odor.** Supported: neat DMSO gave the strongest skin reactions of the substances
    tested on 86 volunteers ([Coverly et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0536.1998.tb05658.x)); 13 people taking
    7-15 g a day for months had "Mild nausea and an unpleasant breath odour" and nothing serious
    ([Ravid et al. 1982](https://doi.org/10.1136/ard.41.6.587)); the US label notes a garlic-like odor on breath and skin
    for up to 72 hours ([RIMSO-50 label](https://www.drugs.com/pro/rimso-50.html)). **Keep.** "Upper half of the body",
    skin that takes a week to look plump (peeling is documented,
    [Ozkaya-Bayazit et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-4362.1998.00600.x)), no smell in milk, and "a close second"
    for safety are yours.
39. **"This is proven in rats"** (about shorter sleep). The cited rat study found something else: one injection of the two
    higher doses made sleep lighter, less deep, for 3 hours; it didn't test shorter sleep, late doses or build-up
    ([Cavas et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.toxlet.2005.02.003)). **Change** (card W-SLEEP): "In rats, higher doses
    made sleep lighter." Your own sleep lines stay as yours.
40. **"DMSO can also burn dry mouth tissue."** No study found on the mouth. On the stomach lining of rats, DMSO left for
    10 minutes damaged the surface of a third of it at 5% and nearly all of it at 100%
    ([Sørbye et al. 1993](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01296073)), and its burning on skin is documented (Coverly 1998).
    **Keep.**

**DMSO, interactions**

41. **"Don't put it on dirty skin; it can carry chemicals inward."** Supported: carrying other substances through skin is
    what DMSO is used for ([Marren 2011](https://doi.org/10.3810/psm.2011.09.1923)). **Keep.**
42. **"Don't take it with drugs that should not absorb rapidly, including corticosteroids and NSAIDs."** The evidence
    points the other way for both classes. An FDA-approved diclofenac (NSAID) solution contains DMSO
    ([FDA label, 2025](https://nctr-crs.fda.gov/fdalabel/services/spl/set-ids/609c5344-9c97-4fa8-ad53-d0f622ce64ec/spl-doc);
    Marren 2011), and DMSO with triamcinolone, a corticosteroid, is a bladder treatment tested in a randomized trial
    ([Moss et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/nau.25142)). The label of sulindac, an NSAID, does warn against it:
    "Concurrent use of dimethyl sulfoxide and sulindac is not recommended"
    ([UK label](https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/8783/smpc/print); the
    [US label](https://medlibrary.org/lib/rx/meds/sulindac/page/5/) adds nerve damage reported with the pair). The rule
    itself holds: DMSO's own label says "Some data indicate that dimethyl sulfoxide potentiates other concomitantly
    administered medications". **Change** (card W-NSAID): "Don't take it with drugs that should not absorb rapidly: DMSO
    can strengthen other medicines taken with it." Sulindac is offered on the card, if you want a named drug.
43. **"Don't nebulize too much DMSO when your lungs are full of smoking-related tar ... Weakness is an early symptom."**
    No study found of inhaled DMSO carrying tar, or of weakness from it. DMSO does dissolve cigarette smoke particles (labs
    use it to extract them, as in [Huang et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1159/000350866)). But in sheep, nebulized DMSO
    after smoke inhalation protected the lungs: all 7 given DMSO with heparin survived, and all 7 untreated died within 72
    hours ([Brown et al. 1988](https://doi.org/10.1097/00004630-198801000-00007); also
    [Kimura et al. 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2458858/), no DOI in PubMed). **Cut, unless it's your
    experience** (card W-TAR).
44. **"DMSO is also cholinergic."** Supported in the lab: DMSO slows the enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine
    ([Obara et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1248/bpb.b22-00791);
    [Kubota et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0014-2999(98)00313-6)); no human interaction report found. **Keep**;
    you could say "in the lab".
45. **"Supplement extra choline because DMSO depletes it."** No study found that DMSO lowers choline, in people or in
    animals. The nearest: in a dish of rat nerve endings, DMSO at 2 to 10% slowed their uptake of choline
    ([North & Mrak 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2626218/), no DOI in PubMed). **Cut, unless it's your
    experience** (card W-CHOLINE).
46. **The harmala timing** is your round-1 answer; no study of DMSO with harmalas, and it fits DMSO's carrying effect.

**DMSO, contraindications**

47. **"both anticonvulsant and proconvulsant effects depending on dose and seizure model."** Supported (item 13). **Keep.**
48. **"It may worsen a cluster headache."** No study of DMSO and cluster headache. A thin thread: histamine injections set
    off cluster attacks ([Bogucki 1990](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1468-2982.1990.1002071.x)), and DMSO's label says it "can
    initiate the liberation of histamine". **Keep**: the line says only "may", which is what a thin thread supports, and the
    histamine line follows it.
49. **"It may release histamine in some people. Use caution with MCAS."** Supported by the label and by guinea pigs, whose
    DMSO swelling antihistamines blocked ([Swanston et al. 1982](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-4274(82)90272-7)). Two human
    studies point the other way: an antihistamine didn't block DMSO's skin wheal
    ([Lahti 1987](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0536.1987.tb01429.x)), and bladder DMSO didn't raise urine histamine
    ([Stout et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0090-4295(99)80295-7)). **Keep**: for MCAS, the label is enough reason.

**Niacin**

50. **The flush, heartburn, drowsiness, the liver, and avoiding slow-release and no-flush forms.** Supported. In a trial
    of 46 adults, slow-release niacin harmed the liver in 12 of 23, immediate-release in none; people quit for flushing,
    fatigue and stomach symptoms ([McKenney et al. 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8309029/), no DOI in PubMed).
    No-flush products contained no free nicotinic acid
    ([Meyers et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-139-12-200312160-00009)). In mice, niacin increased sleep
    ([Szentirmai & Kapás 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-53648-7)). **Keep.** "Terrible sleep quality",
    niacinamide's hyperfocus and the trips aborted are yours; a 1955 paper by Hoffer and a colleague reported nicotinic
    acid changing LSD's effects ([Agnew & Hoffer 1955](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.101.422.12); title only).
    "Sensitive physiology" and "A vitamin is not automatically harmless" are generic and wait for the humanizing.

**Some Remedies Require More Caution**

51. **"Iboga ... potentiates many drugs and can be dangerous."** Supported: 27 deaths after ibogaine, heart rhythm
    problems even without heart disease, and interactions through the liver enzyme CYP2D6
    ([Litjens & Brunt 2016](https://doi.org/10.3109/15563650.2016.1138226)); in animals it strengthened cocaine and morphine
    ([Szumlinski et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1007/s002130051053);
    [Sunder Sharma & Bhargava 1998](https://doi.org/10.1159/000028246)). **Keep.** "Male iboga" and doubling the dose are
    yours.
52. **Humic acid eaten, cinnamon, *Acacia confusa* with *Mimosa hostilis*:** yours (no study found for humic acid; the
    other two not searched). "Useful substances can still be poor rescue tools" is generic and waits for the humanizing.
