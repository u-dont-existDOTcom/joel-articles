You are reading one paragraph of an article for the person it is written for. Here is what the paragraph is for:

WHAT IT IS FOR: the reader is sitting with someone showing serotonin toxicity and is deciding whether to give cyproheptadine now, and how much to rely on it while doing the other steps

HEADING: Suspected Serotonin Syndrome (SS) > Do this immediately when serotonin toxicity is reasonably suspected (step 2 of 8; step 1 is stopping all serotonergic substances, step 3 calling emergency help for moderate or severe signs, step 4 cooling)

PARAGRAPH:
S1: If the person is fully alert and able to swallow, I'd give them cyproheptadine right away.
S2: It's an old allergy medicine that also blocks serotonin receptors, which is why it can help here.
S3: The evidence for it is only case reports so far (nobody has compared it against people who didn't get it), but in a small series of five ER patients, three had no symptoms left within two hours, and the other two took a bit longer.
S4: Even so, I wouldn't wait to see if it works before cooling them down and getting help for moderate or severe signs.

For each sentence, answer in one line: what does the reader need this sentence for, given what the paragraph is for?
Then list the sentences the reader doesn't need.

A sentence the reader doesn't need is one they could lose and still do what the paragraph is for, with nothing
missing. These are not reasons to keep a sentence: it is true; it has a source; it is interesting; it is a careful
hypothesis or a testable guess; it explains a disease or a case the reader doesn't have; it qualifies another claim the
reader didn't need either; it was already in the text. Read also for sense: a sentence whose words don't make sense
together, or that mixes pictures (a dial, a door, a floor and a leak for one thing), goes on the list too, with why.
