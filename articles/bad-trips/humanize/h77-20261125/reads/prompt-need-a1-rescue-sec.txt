You are reading one paragraph of an article for the person it is written for. Here is what the paragraph is for:

WHAT IT IS FOR: a reader learns what the list of rescue remedies that follows is for: acute trouble where the body, the gut or the brain is what is going wrong, as against a mainly emotional crisis; every paragraph but the last is the writer's own and stays; read the last paragraph (from "If a trip comes with diarrhea"), which is a new version

HEADING: My Acute Rescue Remedies (the section's opening, before its list of remedies)

PARAGRAPH:
S1: This hierarchy is for acute situations, including emergencies--not merely uncomfortable trips.
S2: In general it's good to try all these things in very low, increasing doses before they are needed in crisis.
S3: Sometimes the mind isn't in a metaphysical crisis.
S4: The body feels awful and the altered mind writes cosmic horror to explain it.
S5: Nausea, heat, exhaustion, dehydration, overstimulation, and sleep loss all get read as spiritual content very quickly.
S6: A horrible thought isn't automatically a revelation.
S7: The body may need quiet, water, cooling, warmth, rest, or a calmer room.
S8: The body is part of the spiritual field.
S9: The hierarchy appears before the mystical sections because love doesn't require us to ignore the body.
S10: If a trip comes with diarrhea or nausea, a toxic heaviness or a dirty body-feeling, jaw tension, loss of bodily control or neurological disorganization, the mind may feel like it's going crazy only because the gut or the brain is.
S11: That's what the remedies below are for, while the shadow work further down is for a mainly emotional crisis.
S12: I say this because a gut-driven mental hell is often mistaken as shadow work.

For each sentence, answer in one line: what does the reader need this sentence for, given what the paragraph is for?
Then list the sentences the reader doesn't need.

A sentence the reader doesn't need is one they could lose and still do what the paragraph is for, with nothing
missing. These are not reasons to keep a sentence: it is true; it has a source; it is interesting; it is a careful
hypothesis or a testable guess; it explains a disease or a case the reader doesn't have; it qualifies another claim the
reader didn't need either; it was already in the text. Read also for sense: a sentence whose words don't make sense
together, or that mixes pictures (a dial, a door, a floor and a leak for one thing), goes on the list too, with why.

Before you list a sentence for giving the reader nothing to do, ask what it means they should do. A caution, a risk
or a difference between people often implies an act the sentence doesn't say ("Sensitivity varies" means: start
with less): that sentence is needed, and its NEEDS line says the act it implies, so the writer can add it.
