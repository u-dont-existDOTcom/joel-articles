- S2: 1, 4 — It's a bare noun phrase ("itchy sunburn-like heat") that never says what happens. "Niacin flush" is also never explained: skin reddening and warmth, mainly face and chest, starting shortly after the dose. His way would put that in a full clause with what the person feels and when.
- S3: 7, 10 — "This" could mean the flush, the itch or the heat, and "it" could mean the flush or niacin. "Normal" is also no use to the reader without what to do about it, such as whether the flush fades or when it's a reason to stop. His way names the flush and says what to do.
- S4: 1, 2, 4 — It's a fragment with no verb, and "too much at once" gives no amount or what to do. It repeats the shape of S2 and S5, a symptom followed by a qualifier. His way would give it its own clause, such as "You may get heartburn if your stomach is sensitive or if you take a lot of niacin at once", plus what to do.
- S5: 1, 2, 8, 10 — It's another fragment in the same shape as S2 and S4, and "Drowsiness, usually with terrible sleep quality" uses a model-style comma pair. A reader could take it as drowsiness during the day and bad sleep that night, or as the drowsiness itself being poor sleep. His way would say which, using "and" and a full clause.
- S6: 1, 4, 6, 7 — It packs several ideas into one sentence: "higher or repeated doses", "liver problems, alcohol, medications, or sensitive physiology". "Medications" and "sensitive physiology" don't say which medications or what counts as sensitive. It ends on a list of risks with no action, such as what warning signs to watch for or when to stop and get help. His way would split it up, name the signs (for example yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, belly pain), and end on what to do.
