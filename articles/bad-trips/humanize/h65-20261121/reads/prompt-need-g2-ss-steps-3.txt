You are reading one paragraph of an article for the person it is written for. Here is what the paragraph is for:

WHAT IT IS FOR: doing the right things, in order, for a friend who may have serotonin syndrome, while calling for help and waiting for it

HEADING: Do this immediately when serotonin toxicity is reasonably suspected:

PARAGRAPH:
S1: Stop all suspected serotonergic substances, meaning anything that raises serotonin, like:
S2: their antidepressant, if it's an SSRI, SNRI or MAOI
S3: tramadol for pain, or the antibiotic linezolid
S4: another dose of MDMA, or of any stimulant
S5: 5-HTP, St John's wort or SAM-e
S6: harmala, or an ayahuasca-type MAOI
S7: even DXM cough syrup
S8: If the person is fully alert and able to swallow, I would give cyproheptadine early, since it blocks serotonin receptors.
S9: So far, the evidence for this med has only been case reports, and a recent review found them insufficient to show that it works.
S10: I wouldn't wait to see whether it does before doing the other steps.
S11: Call emergency help and arrange transport.
S12: Don't drive yourself.
S13: If you can't tell what they mixed, call a poison control line as well, once help is on the way.
S14: Cool them down without holding up transport:
S15: Get them into a cool room and take off any extra clothing.
S16: Put a fan on them and cool damp cloths, and if they're hot, ice packs wrapped in cloth at the neck, armpits or groin.
S17: Don't put them in a bath if they're confused, seizing or very weak.
S18: Fever reducers do not help with heat generated by muscles working this hard.
S19: Keep the room calm and keep them still:
S20: No crowds or arguments around them.
S21: No pacing or exercise to "burn it off," since muscle work makes more heat and raises the risk of rhabdomyolysis (damaged muscle leaking into the blood, which can hurt the kidneys).
S22: If they feel a strong urge to keep moving (akathisia), stay with them and help them keep still anyway.
S23: Give fluids only if they're fully alert and not vomiting, and then only small sips of an electrolyte drink.
S24: Don't force fluids, food, pills or charcoal on someone who's confused, vomiting, sleepy or at risk of a seizure, since they could choke on them.
S25: Gather what the ER will need:
S26: their pill bottles and packages
S27: exactly what they took, how much and when, including their last dose and any dose they recently increased
S28: any supplements or recreational drugs
S29: their temperature, pulse and blood pressure, if you can take them
S30: While you wait, don't give them niacin: it's this guide's trip killer, but it's unsafe with serotonin syndrome.
S31: DMSO isn't one of these steps either.
S32: Once they're fully alert, I'd consider magnesium chloride by mouth, as in the MDMA section above, especially for MDMA-related jaw tension or muscle twitching.

For each sentence, answer in one line: what does the reader need this sentence for, given what the paragraph is for?
Then list the sentences the reader doesn't need.

A sentence the reader doesn't need is one they could lose and still do what the paragraph is for, with nothing
missing. These are not reasons to keep a sentence: it is true; it has a source; it is interesting; it is a careful
hypothesis or a testable guess; it explains a disease or a case the reader doesn't have; it qualifies another claim the
reader didn't need either; it was already in the text. Read also for sense: a sentence whose words don't make sense
together, or that mixes pictures (a dial, a door, a floor and a leak for one thing), goes on the list too, with why.

Before you list a sentence for giving the reader nothing to do, ask what it means they should do. A caution, a risk
or a difference between people often implies an act the sentence doesn't say ("Sensitivity varies" means: start
with less): that sentence is needed, and its NEEDS line says the act it implies, so the writer can add it.
