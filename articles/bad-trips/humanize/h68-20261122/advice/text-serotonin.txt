S1: ## Serotonin Syndrome (SS)?
S2: Serotonin syndrome overlaps with several symptoms above, but the rescue measures in the other branches do not necessarily treat it.
S3: It can follow serotonergic medication changes, combinations, or excessive exposure.
S4: Ayahuasca and other MAOI brews, MDMA, DXM, many antidepressants, tramadol, linezolid, other MAOIs, and some opioids can contribute, especially in combinations.
S5: ### How to Check for SS
S6: To diagnose serotonin syndrome, we can use the Hunter criteria, the same checklist clinicians use.
S7: Using Hunter, one or more of these patterns makes the diagnosis more likely:
S8: - Spontaneous clonus: involuntary rhythmic muscle spasms, with no stimulation needed to set it off, would be one full pattern
S9: - Inducible clonus, spasms set off by stretching/flexing a muscle, plus agitation or sweating would be counted as one pattern altogether.
S10: - Ocular clonus (rapid involuntary eye movements) plus agitation or sweating, another pattern together.
S11: - Another is tremor (shaking) along with hyperreflexia (abnormally brisk reflexes).
S12: - Lastly, Hypertonia (muscular rigidity) with fever above 38°C (100.4°F), as well as ocular or inducible clonus.
S13: So if you see any of these patterns, call medics.
S14: ### Act Now on SS
S15: 1.
S16: Serotonin syndrome comes from too much serotonin, so I'd stop anything suspected of adding more (serotonergic substances).
S17: So no more MDMA or other stimulants, of course, but also no DXM cough syrup, the painkiller tramadol or the antibiotic linezolid.
S18: It doesn't matter if they are prescribed an antidepressant (an SSRI like Prozac, or an SNRI or MAOI), it's too risky right now.
S19: If they drank ayahuasca or another harmala brew, like Syrian rue, no more of that either, since those work the way MAOIs do.
S20: And check whether they take supplements people don't think of as drugs, like 5-HTP, St John's wort or SAM-e.
S21: 2.
S22: If the person is fully alert and able to swallow, I would give cyproheptadine early, since it blocks serotonin receptors.
S23: So far, the evidence for this med has only been case reports, and a recent review found them insufficient to show that it works.
S24: I wouldn't wait to see whether it does before doing the other steps.
S25: And most people won't have it at home anyway, since it needs a prescription in the US, though a telehealth doctor might write one quickly, and only some countries sell it without one.
S26: 3.
S27: When you call medics, ask for an ambulance, since the person can get worse fast, with seizures or dangerous overheating.
S28: It may seem faster to drive them to the ER yourself, but don't: paramedics can start treating them right there, and if they have a seizure in your car, you can't help them and drive at the same time.
S29: And while you wait, call poison control if you don't know what was mixed, as they might be able to help you work that out, even if they can't treat serotonin syndrome.
S30: 4.
S31: Since bodily overheating is the most dangerous part of serotonin syndrome, you should address their twitching and tense muscles that are making a lot of heat, while you wait for the ambulance.
S32: So I'd get them somewhere cool, take off their extra clothes, and set a fan blowing over cool damp cloths on their skin.
S33: If they still feel hot, put ice packs wrapped in cloth where they can absorb the most heat: at the neck, armpits and groin.
S34: Although a cold bath would cool them faster, they could slip under and breathe in water if they're confused, seizing, or very weak, so it's not advisable.
S35: And while you might think fever reducers like Tylenol or ibuprofen could work by reducing body heat in general, they don't work on the heat generated by muscles.
S36: 5.
S37: Since they're already overstimulated, it helps to clear out the crowd and stop any arguing.
S38: While it's natural for people who are overstimulated to start pacing or exercising to "burn it off," the problem is that creates more body heat, and it raises the risk of their muscles breaking down into the blood, which hurts the kidneys (rhabdomyolysis).
S39: But if they feel a strong urge to keep moving (akathisia), I'd have them do some fidgeting (especially of the calf muscle, flexing the feet), which can soothe that urge without the over-activity risk.
S40: 6.
S41: Overstimulating also dehydrates them, but we can't give fluids unless they're fully alert, able to swallow and not vomiting, and then just small sips of an electrolyte drink (a hypertonic one, more concentrated than an ordinary one, may be required if they still have symptoms of lack of electrolytes).
S42: 7.
S43: The ER doctors will want to know exactly what they took, so before the ambulance comes, I'd gather their pill bottles and packages.
S44: I'd also jot down how much of each they took and when, street drugs and supplements included.
S45: Keep track of how their breathing is doing, as that's one of the main indicators of how dangerous the seizure is, according to the CDC.
S46: 8.
S47: While you wait, you might think of this guide's other remedies, like niacin for ending a trip or DMSO for brain injuries, seizures and chest pain, but I wouldn't use either one here.
S48: Niacin is actually unsafe with serotonin syndrome, since at the doses used to end a trip it can cause flushing, dizziness and low blood pressure (NIH).
S49: I'd also leave out this guide's Syrian rue oil for now: made at home it has almost none of the harmala alkaloids, but I couldn't find any research on it with MDMA, so it could help or harm here.
S50: Magnesium chloride is the one I would consider, since many people with serotonin syndrome run low on magnesium (a nursing review).
