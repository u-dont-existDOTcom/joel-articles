You are reading one paragraph of an article for the person it is written for. Here is what the paragraph is for:

WHAT IT IS FOR: knowing what to do, during a trip, with a dark thought or feeling about yourself that shows up

HEADING: Dealing With Shadow Thoughts

PARAGRAPH:
S1: During a trip, a dark thought can show up that feels like the plain truth about you, like suddenly hating someone you love, or being sure you're a fraud.
S2: It's usually not the truth, just the opposite of whatever you were working so hard on, coming into focus.
S3: Shoving it down only makes it come back louder.
S4: Tell the person sitting with you if you want to, but don't go texting your whole family about it at 3 a.m., and don't decide you've got some lifelong disorder either.
S5: Just notice the thought and the feeling that came with it.
S6: They're intense, sure, but they aren't all of you.
S7: Then come back to the room you're in, your body, the person with you, what time it is, and when you can, love.

For each sentence, answer in one line: what does the reader need this sentence for, given what the paragraph is for?
Then list the sentences the reader doesn't need.

A sentence the reader doesn't need is one they could lose and still do what the paragraph is for, with nothing
missing. These are not reasons to keep a sentence: it is true; it has a source; it is interesting; it is a careful
hypothesis or a testable guess; it explains a disease or a case the reader doesn't have; it qualifies another claim the
reader didn't need either; it was already in the text. Read also for sense: a sentence whose words don't make sense
together, or that mixes pictures (a dial, a door, a floor and a leak for one thing), goes on the list too, with why.
