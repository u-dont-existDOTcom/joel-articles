## What I checked (PubMed, 2026-11-09)

- The plant's main alkaloids are the beta-carbolines harmine, harmaline and harmalol, and harmine is the most studied
  (Moloudizargari et al. 2013, "Pharmacological and therapeutic effects of Peganum harmala and its main alkaloids",
  [doi:10.4103/0973-7847.120524](https://doi.org/10.4103/0973-7847.120524); round 28's card cited it).
- Harmine is among the plant's volatile compounds: a 2024 study of its aerial parts found it by needle-trap sampling
  ([doi:10.1016/j.jchromb.2024.124232](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jchromb.2024.124232)). That fits his caution that vaping
  whole seeds might give an MAOI effect.
- Two studies of the seeds' fixed oil measured its fatty acids (mostly linoleic acid) and tocopherols, not its alkaloids
  ([doi:10.1097/MJT.0000000000000402](https://doi.org/10.1097/MJT.0000000000000402),
  [doi:10.3390/molecules25194569](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules25194569)). They neither confirm nor rule out harmine
  in an oil infusion.
- A traditional Syrian rue oil, rubbed on the knee in a 2015 trial for osteoarthritis, "has been analyzed and
  standardized by high-performance liquid chromatography"
  ([doi:10.1177/2156587214566867](https://doi.org/10.1177/2156587214566867)). For this plant that usually means its harmine
  and harmaline, but I couldn't open the full paper to see what was measured or how much: the publisher's page needs his
  OK to fetch.

So his point that a home-made oil carries little harmine or harmaline is his experience and his own reading, and I found
nothing that confirms or rules it out. The guide says it as his, in his entry, and one paragraph of mine after it says
to treat the oil as a possible MAOI anyway (card RUE-WARN, which he can reject).
