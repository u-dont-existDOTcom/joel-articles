# Reading a table of contents

You're testing the table of contents of a long online guide, "Trip-Sitting: Medical Triage, Shadow Work, Therapy", written
for people having a hard time on psychedelics or in another altered state, and for the friends sitting with them. It's
about 12,800 words. You'll see only its table of contents: each heading at its level (#, ##, ###, ####), with how many
words are under it. You won't see the text, and you shouldn't guess at it beyond what a reader skimming the contents
would.

Read the table of contents file and the situations file your task line names, then write your answer file as Markdown,
in three parts:

1. **Where you'd look.** For each situation, the heading you'd go to first, exactly as the contents has it; "sure" or
   "guess"; and, if it's a guess, the heading you'd try next. Answer quickly, as someone in that situation would.
2. **What gets in the way.** Headings that don't say what's under them or could be read two ways; sections that seem to
   be in the wrong place, at the wrong level, or under the wrong parent; anything that looks like it's in two places.
   Say why for each.
3. **The contents you'd want.** The same sections, in the order and at the levels you'd put them, as a table of
   contents. Rename a heading only when a reader can't tell what's under it, and then keep the writer's words where you
   can: he writes playfully on purpose ("Hang up the phone, get off the Healing Treadmill", "Metta NMT Sniffer Makes the
   Trip Chill & Lovey!"). Don't add sections the guide doesn't have. You may merge two headings, split one, or move one
   under another. After it, list each change with its reason in a line.

One thing the writer has decided: the guide's greeting to the reader ("Hello, Lovey Bunny! ...") opens the part about
the psychological side, after the medical and remedy parts, because that part needs a softer voice. Keep that part's
start where it can carry the greeting.
