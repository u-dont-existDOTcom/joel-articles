WHAT IT IS FOR: someone reading the DMSO profile to decide whether and how to use DMSO to steady a bad trip, weighing what the research says it does and its risks.

HEADING: DMSO (a remedy profile in the part "Mental Stabilizers That Don't Abort the Trip")

PARAGRAPH:
S1: One famous 1967 study by Ramirez & Luza found it more effective than the best antipsychotic drugs at the time, slashing mental patients' psych ward stays in half, and that included patients with severe anxiety, though it hasn't been tested (that I know of, yet) for PTSD.
S2: It's routinely used in humans who receive stem cell transplants, as it is the best cell preservative that we've found so far.
S3: There is evidence (Kortleve, et al, 2023) that using too much DMSO (10% vs 5%) in stem cell transfusions increases mild side effects in adult humans.
S4: Young brains may be at risk from DMSO, though: it killed brain cells in mice up to a month old, even at a low dose, and in a dish of rat neurons (Hanslick, et al, 2009).
S5: Translating mouse research to humans isn't straightforward.

NEEDS:
S1: the evidence that it works, which is the reason to try it on a bad trip: in a 1967 study it beat the antipsychotics of its day and halved psychiatric patients' hospital stays, severe anxiety included (the closing clause on PTSD, a condition the reader isn't treating, gives them nothing).
S2: nothing
S3: the one risk measured in adults: more DMSO brings more side effects, and they're mild, which is what an adult weighs and a reason to use less rather than more (10% and 5% are strengths used in transfusions, not a dose the reader can use).
S4: who should stay away from it: young people, since it killed brain cells in young mice even at a low dose (the text doesn't say how young that is in people).
S5: nothing

UNNEEDED:
- S2: It's routinely used in humans who receive stem cell transplants, as it is the best cell preservative that we've found so far. — Why transplants use it is about a treatment the reader isn't having; the one thing they could take from it, that DMSO is given to people, S3 already shows (adults given it in stem cell transfusions had only mild side effects).
- S5: Translating mouse research to humans isn't straightforward. — S4's "may" and "in mice" already tell the reader the risk to young people is unproven in humans; this adds no human evidence either way and changes nothing they do.
