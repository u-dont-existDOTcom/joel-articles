THE DRAFT (what it is for: a friend sitting with someone on a drug who says their brain feels damaged has just done the stroke checks and is deciding whether to trust DMSO as the first remedy; this paragraph gives the author's reasons and the evidence for it (section "If Someone Says 'My Brain Feels Damaged': DMSO + Humic Acid + Electrolytes First")):
S1: I believe, based on my experience and those of others I've heard from, as well as the books I've read on this, that DMSO works better for most acute brain damage than anything else readily available.
S2: The clinical evidence is incomplete, but it leans this way.
S3: In animals, when given after a stroke, it shrank the area of the brain that died (which is a good thing).
S4: In one human trial, some clot stroke patients seemed to recover better on DMSO, and doctors have used it in a small series of patients to bring down dangerous pressure inside the skull.
S5: But more relevant to the drug overdose issue this article deals with, DMSO calms NMDA- and AMPA-mediated excitotoxicity (brain cells getting overexcited to death by glutamate), at least in rat brain cells in a dish.

MARKED:
- S2: 3, 4 — "incomplete, but it leans this way" sets a hedge against a reassurance where it could just say what the evidence is, and "clinical evidence" and "this way" leave unclear what counts and what it shows (most of what follows isn't in people, and none of it compares DMSO with other remedies); his way would name the evidence plainly (mostly animals and brain cells, a little in people) and let the studies that follow show which way it points.
- S4: 2, 8, 1 — opens "In one human trial, ..." the same way S3 opens "In animals, ..."; "one" where "a" would do (or "only one" if the point is that there's just one); "clot stroke patients" and "a small series of patients" are packed medical shorthand; his way would build it unlike S3 (e.g. start from the people: "Some patients whose stroke came from a clot seemed to recover better on DMSO in a trial") and say "a handful of patients".
- S5: 1, 7, 4 — leads with the jargon "NMDA- and AMPA-mediated excitotoxicity", leaves what happens to a bracketed noun phrase with glutamate unexplained, and tacks the hedge on the end; the receptor names give the friend nothing to act on; "the drug overdose issue this article deals with" claims relevance without saying how; his way says what happens first, in plain words and its own clause (glutamate, a chemical brain cells use to excite each other, can overexcite them until they die, and DMSO calmed that in rat brain cells in a dish), cuts "NMDA- and AMPA-mediated", and names which overdoses overexcite brain cells so the friend can tell whether it fits their companion.
