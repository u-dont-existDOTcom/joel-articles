1. Serotonin syndrome comes from too much serotonin, so I'd stop anything suspected of adding more (serotonergic substances). No more MDMA or other stimulants, of course, but also no DXM cough syrup, the painkiller tramadol or the antibiotic linezolid. Even if they take an antidepressant every day (an SSRI like Prozac, or an SNRI or MAOI), I'd skip it until a doctor says to restart it, since missing a dose is much less risky than more serotonin right now. If they drank ayahuasca or another harmala brew, like Syrian rue, that counts too, since those work the way MAOIs do. And check whether they take supplements people don't think of as drugs, like 5-HTP, St John's wort or SAM-e.
2. If the person is fully alert and able to swallow, I would give cyproheptadine early, since it blocks serotonin receptors. So far, the evidence for this med has only been case reports, and a recent review found them insufficient to show that it works. I wouldn't wait to see whether it does before doing the other steps.
3. When you call medics, ask for an ambulance, since the person can get worse fast, with seizures or dangerous overheating. It may seem faster to drive them to the ER yourself, but don't: paramedics can start treating them as soon as they arrive, and if they have a seizure in your car, you can't help them and drive at the same time. If you don't know what was mixed, poison control can help work that out once the ambulance is on its way, though they can't treat it.
4. Their twitching and tense muscles are making a lot of heat, and overheating is the most dangerous part of serotonin syndrome, so while you wait for the ambulance, I'd get them somewhere cool, take off their extra clothes, and set a fan blowing over cool damp cloths on their skin. If they still feel hot, ice packs wrapped in cloth help most at the neck, armpits and groin. A cold bath would cool them faster, but if they're confused, seizing or very weak, they could slip under or breathe in water, so don't put them in one. And don't count on fever reducers like Tylenol or ibuprofen, which do not help with heat generated by muscles.
5. Since they're already overstimulated, it helps to clear out the crowd and stop any arguing. While it's natural for people who are overstimulated to start pacing or exercising to "burn it off," the problem is that creates more body heat, and it raises the risk of their muscles breaking down into the blood, which hurts the kidneys (rhabdomyolysis). But if they feel a strong urge to keep moving (akathisia), I'd have them do some fidgeting (especially of the calf muscle, flexing the feet), which can soothe that urge without the over-activity risk.
6. Overstimulating also dehydrates them, but we can't give fluids unless they're fully alert, able to swallow and not vomiting, and then just small sips of an electrolyte drink (a hypertonic one, more concentrated than an ordinary one, may be required if they still have symptoms of lack of electrolytes).
7. The ER doctors will want to know exactly what they took, so before the ambulance comes, I'd gather their pill bottles and packages and jot down how much of each they took and when, street drugs and supplements included. If a dose went up lately, note that too, since a bigger dose alone can set off serotonin syndrome. And if you have a thermometer or a blood pressure cuff, write down the numbers with the time, which shows the doctors whether things are getting better or worse.
8. While you wait, you might think of this guide's other remedies, like niacin for ending a trip or DMSO for brain injuries, seizures and chest pain, but I wouldn't use either one here, and niacin is actually unsafe with serotonin syndrome. For MDMA-related jaw tension or muscle activity, though, I would consider oral magnesium chloride if they're fully alert and able to swallow (the amounts are in MDMA: Magnesium Alarm, above).
