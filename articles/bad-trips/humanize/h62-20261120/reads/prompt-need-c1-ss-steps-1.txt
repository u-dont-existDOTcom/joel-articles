You are reading one paragraph of an article for the person it is written for. Here is what the paragraph is for:

WHAT IT IS FOR: doing the first steps for a friend whose symptoms match a serotonin toxicity pattern, right after the guide says to call medics for any pattern

HEADING: Do this immediately when serotonin toxicity is reasonably suspected

PARAGRAPH:
S1: Stop all suspected serotonergic substances.
S2: No more SSRI, SNRI, MAOI, tramadol, MDMA, DXM cough syrup, 5-HTP, St John's wort, SAM-e, linezolid, stimulant redoses, or harmala/ayahuasca-type MAOI exposure.
S3: If the person is fully alert and able to swallow, I would give cyproheptadine early, since it blocks serotonin receptors.
S4: So far, the evidence for this med has only been case reports, and a recent review found them insufficient to show that it works.
S5: I wouldn't wait to see whether it does before doing the other steps.
S6: Call emergency help and arrange transport.
S7: Don't drive yourself.
S8: Poison control may help when the combination is unclear, but it is not the treatment.
S9: Cool the person without delaying transport.
S10: Move to a cool room, remove excess clothing, use a fan and cool damp cloths, and place ice packs wrapped in cloth at the neck, armpits, or groin when the person is hot.
S11: Don't put a confused, seizing, or very weak person in a bath.
S12: Fever reducers do not solve heat generated by extreme muscle activity.
S13: Keep stimulation low and prevent exertion.
S14: No pacing to "burn it off," exercise, crowds, or arguments.
S15: Akathisia can create a strong urge to move, but muscle activity can worsen heat and rhabdomyolysis risk.
S16: Hydrate only when fully alert and not vomiting.
S17: Small sips of an appropriate oral rehydration drink are reasonable.
S18: Don't force fluids, food, pills, or charcoal when someone is confused, vomiting, sleepy, or at seizure risk.
S19: Gather the evidence for the ER.
S20: Bring pill bottles and packages.
S21: Write down exact substances, dose, timing, last dose, recent increases, supplements, recreational drugs, temperature, pulse, and blood pressure when available.
S22: Hospitals may use benzodiazepines, external cooling, IV fluids, monitoring, and intensive supportive care.
S23: Niacin is unsafe for serotonin syndrome.
S24: DMSO is also not part of this branch.
S25: I would consider oral magnesium chloride when the person is fully alert, especially with MDMA-related jaw tension or muscle activity.

For each sentence, answer in one line: what does the reader need this sentence for, given what the paragraph is for?
Then list the sentences the reader doesn't need.

A sentence the reader doesn't need is one they could lose and still do what the paragraph is for, with nothing
missing. These are not reasons to keep a sentence: it is true; it has a source; it is interesting; it is a careful
hypothesis or a testable guess; it explains a disease or a case the reader doesn't have; it qualifies another claim the
reader didn't need either; it was already in the text. Read also for sense: a sentence whose words don't make sense
together, or that mixes pictures (a dial, a door, a floor and a leak for one thing), goes on the list too, with why.

Before you list a sentence for giving the reader nothing to do, ask what it means they should do. A caution, a risk
or a difference between people often implies an act the sentence doesn't say ("Sensitivity varies" means: start
with less): that sentence is needed, and its NEEDS line says the act it implies, so the writer can add it.

Answer in this shape:

NEEDS:
S1: [what the reader needs it for, or "nothing"]
S2: ...

UNNEEDED:
- S2: [the sentence, copied exactly] — [why the reader can lose it]
- (or "none")
