# His sentences beside mine (2026-11-08)

A fresh Sonnet reader was given three paragraphs of his Give the Session (A, Human High) and my AFT2 (B, AI High),
both plain advice built the same way, and asked how the sentences differ in how they're built and worded, quoting both,
without judging the advice. Its answer, strongest first:

1. **A qualifies inside the sentence; B states flat.** A: "ideally start earlier in the day", "roughly how much",
   "sometimes quite dramatically", "most importantly, sleep". B's only hedge is "tend to".
2. **A's concessions cut across what came before, with "though" and "although" mid-sentence; B's links resolve.** A:
   "If you're taking a psychoactive drug, though, ..."; "that's a good starting place ..., although people's
   sensitivities can vary". B: "since ...", "which means ...".
3. **A mixes long trailing sentences with short imperatives; B is even and medium**, with a clipped aphorism dropped in
   ("Sleep problems are their own thing.").
4. **A keeps going after its point** (a list, a ranking, a caveat: "food, some normal company and most importantly,
   sleep"); **B stops** once the point lands.
5. **A opens on the reader's situation ("If you're planning ...", "If you've never taken ...")**; B opens on a category
   and slots a remedy under it ("Leftover anxiety and fear ...", "Sleep problems ...").
6. **A's words are concrete and a bit odd** ("begin already depleted", "jumping into the deep end", "good cries"); **B's
   are category words** ("what kind they are", "something different").
7. **A's small words**: some, quite, roughly, most importantly, though, although, along with.
8. **B leans on balanced pairs** ("the fear or the bad nights", "fewer people and less noise"); A's lists are longer and
   lumpier.
9. **A is three paragraphs, each from the reader's situation and ending in a qualified clause**; B is one block of six
   short sentences, each doing one job.

Its summary: B would need qualifiers and "though/although" inside its claims, "If you ..." openers tied to the reader's
situation, plainer concrete words, some sentences that run on past their point with a tail, list or ranking, and varied
lengths, without the clipped aphorism and the neat "X, which means Y".
