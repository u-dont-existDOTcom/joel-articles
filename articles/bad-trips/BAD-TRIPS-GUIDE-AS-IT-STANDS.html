Quick Bad Trip Checklist

P1 Use this in order. Medical safety first. Then stop unsafe momentum, fix the room, help the body, find one calm human, protect sleep, and only then start interpreting anything.

1. Is this medically or physically dangerous?

P1 Trouble breathing, a prolonged or repeated seizure, collapse, inability to wake normally, dangerous overheating, severe or worsening chest pain, one-sided weakness, inability to speak normally, loss of bodily control, serious injury, repeated vomiting, a dangerous drug combination, or someone who cannot be kept physically safe?

P2 Get urgent help while giving basic first aid and any appropriate immediate rescue measures. Medical help and field rescue can happen in parallel. Please don’t spiritually process a body that is going under.

If someone says “my brain feels damaged”

P1 Take that seriously even if they are high. The phrase is not a diagnosis, but intoxication does not make it meaningless, and somebody with real neurological trouble may be unable to describe the problem better. Use DMSO immediately while checking whether they can stay awake, breathe normally, smile evenly, raise both arms, repeat a simple sentence, walk, control their bodily functions, and report seizure, severe headache, repeated vomiting, dangerous heat, chest pain, or vision changes. These checks include several recognized stroke warning signs.

P2 If this is a psychedelic and the concern is actual brain damage rather than a trip that is merely too intense, I am no longer trying to preserve the trip: DMSO comes first, then Ipomoea pes-caprae to stop the trip cold and fast. I do not yet have evidence that pes-caprae aborts MDMA; for MDMA, move from DMSO to the magnesium-and-electrolyte branch. Get medical help in parallel when objective danger signs appear or basic stability cannot be established.

P3 Note: I have found that smelling humic acid can fix an overdose experience in seconds, and tasting a few milligrams of it can produce hyperintelligence, but I’ve only noticed this with ayahuasca & loveyhuasca so far, have not yet tested it with others.

2. Is anyone about to do something unsafe?

P1 Stop the immediate momentum toward roads, balconies, water, stoves, knives, crowds, fights, strangers, driving, posting, texting, or leaving alone.

P2 Put away the car keys, the phone if posting or texting is becoming risky, weapons, sharp objects, and anything else that could turn a temporary state into a permanent consequence.

3. Is the room wrong?

P1 Check the obvious things: light, sound, temperature, privacy, bathroom access, music, crowding, and how many people are talking. Soothing meditative music, like that of Chantress Seba and others on my music page, can be very helpful. Music can also steer the state in the wrong direction, so use something familiar and keep the stop or change button within reach. Steady drumming may give a scattered body one rhythm to follow; airy harp-like or meditative music may feel more spiritually uplifting.

P2 Usually one calm sober person is the best main anchor. When that person can’t safely hold the situation alone, add one more calm adult or a crisis professional—not a crowd.

4. Does the body need stillness or movement?

P1 Don’t make this ideological. Try the safest option for two minutes and see what actually happens.

  • P2 If stillness helps, sit against a wall or lie under a blanket, with one hand on the heart and one on the belly.

  • P3 If movement helps, try slow pacing, gentle stretching, shaking out the arms and legs, wall push-ups, or one boring ordinary task you can finish.

  • P4 If staring inward is making you more stuck, keep part of your attention with the feeling while another part folds five towels, sorts a few objects, draws, or finishes another simple safe task.

  • P5 If movement makes you more impulsive, return to sitting or lying down.

P6 Avoid strenuous exercise with overheating, chest pain, faintness, severe confusion, stimulant use, unknown drug mixtures, or other medical instability.

5. Is there a human anchor?

P1 Contact one grounded person if you can. Say:

P2 I’m in an intense state and I’m scared. I don’t need you to solve my life. Please stay with me, speak slowly, remind me where I am, and help me not make any big decisions until tomorrow.

P3 If you took a substance:

P4 I took something. I am in [place]. It is [date/time]. This is intense, but it will change.

P5 If this came from meditation, breathwork, fasting, sleep loss, kundalini-style practice, or emotional overwhelm:

P6 I’m in an intense state. I am in [place]. It is [date/time]. It will change. I am stopping the practice and coming back to ordinary reality.

6. Are you alone at 3 a.m.?

P1 One lamp on. Blanket. Water nearby. Keys away. Put a familiar note, childhood toy, stuffed animal, or other object that already means safety beside you. Use the phone for support only, rather than posting. Write this where you can see it:

P2 This will change. No big actions until morning.

P3 If your body needs to move, do something safe and boring: pace slowly, fold clothes, wash one cup, or tidy one surface. If movement escalates you, sit against a wall or lie down. Protect sleep as soon as possible.

7. Is the trip merely too intense, with no brain-damage concern?

P1 Use this calming branch only after the quick neurological screen is reassuring and the person wants to preserve the trip. If they say their brain feels damaged, return to the DMSO-first branch instead.

P2 Try grounding before chemically or forcefully changing the state:

  • One hand on heart and one on belly.

  • P3 Longer-exhale breathing.

  • P4 Gentle Buteyko-style reduced breathing: breathe softly and slowly through the nose, taking in slightly less air than you want until there is only faint air hunger. No strain, no long breath-holds while intoxicated, and stop if it spikes fear.

  • P5 Silence or one calm track on repeat.

  • P6 Low light, a blanket, and simple reassurance.

  • P7 Slow pacing or gentle movement when that reduces pressure.

P8 Some people over-breathe during panic. The resulting low CO2 can produce tingling, lightheadedness, and feelings of unreality that make the trip seem even more threatening. Gentle breathing reduction is meant to soften that loop, rather than become a new breathing project. Source: Johns Hopkins on hyperventilation.

8. Is the person euphoric, grandiose, or cosmically certain?

P1 Some dangerous states feel wonderful. Bliss, certainty, and “I have a mission” can be more dangerous than fear because the person may resist grounding.

P2 The action-lock applies double: no driving, posting, confessing, confronting, redosing, disappearing, giving away possessions, testing theories, or proving anything tonight.

P3 If the revelation is true, it will survive sleep and breakfast.

9. Are visions, entities, memories, or cosmic conclusions giving commands?

P1 Don’t try to win a metaphysical argument. Don’t agree with harmful conclusions either. Reflect the feeling, then redirect toward safety, non-harm, sleep, and ordinary reality.

P2 A useful response sounds like this:

P3 I hear how vast and certain this feels. Many traditions say God is in everyone. That makes every body sacred, not disposable. Peace is the godly move tonight. No proof is needed. We protect life and sleep first.

P4 Get crisis or emergency help when there is a specific target, a weapon, movement toward action, escalating violence, or a situation the sitter cannot safely contain.

10. Is the gut the main problem?

P1 Nausea, diarrhea, a dirty or toxic feeling, or a parasite-die-off feeling may need body-level support. But don’t let “toxins” distract from repeated vomiting, severe abdominal pain, dehydration, blood, fever, confusion, fainting, respiratory depression, collapse, seizure, dangerous overheating, or another concrete sign of poisoning.

P2 My personal first thought here is ozonated zeolite rather than charcoal because charcoal can reduce the trip and bind many substances. That is a personal field note, rather than standard emergency medicine.

P3 Activated charcoal has legitimate toxicology uses after some recent oral ingestions, but it isn’t a universal “I took too much” remedy. It matters what was swallowed, how recently, whether charcoal binds it, and whether the person is fully alert and can protect the airway. It can be dangerous when someone is vomiting, sedated, seizing, or unable to swallow safely. Source: clinical review on activated charcoal.

11. Is nausea still the main problem after basic gut support?

P1 Use nausea smoothers that are unlikely to abort the trip when the person wants to preserve it. Ipomoea pes-caprae is not my ordinary first-line nausea remedy, because the difference between smoothing the nausea and stopping the trip can be narrow.

12. Do I want—or need—to stop a psychedelic trip?

P1 When the person wants the trip ended, or when a serious brain-damage feeling makes preserving it irrelevant, Ipomoea pes-caprae is my ultimate sledgehammer. For neurological concern, DMSO comes first and pes-caprae follows. Niacin is a backup trip killer. Charcoal belongs only in the separate recent-oral-ingestion branch. I do not yet have data showing that pes-caprae aborts MDMA.

P2 Charcoal may reduce absorption of some substances, but its use is situation-specific and it can be dangerous with vomiting or impaired consciousness. Source: Poison Control on activated charcoal.

13. Is this shadow work?

P1 Then use love, awareness, inner-child reparenting, somatic release, and gentle witnessing. Meaning still waits until the person is safe, hydrated, oriented, and unlikely to do anything rash.

14. Can I send love to the one suffering?

P1 When you can, do that. When you can’t, stabilize the room, body, breath, human contact, time orientation, and sleep. Then try again.

15. What support can I contact?

P1 Identify local crisis and peer-support resources before you need them. Coverage and hours vary by country.

  • P2 Fireside Project Psychedelic Support Line — U.S.-only, non-emergency psychedelic peer support.

  • P3 TripSit web chat — online peer chat for drug-related support.

  • P4 Find A Helpline — country-specific crisis lines, including phone, text, and online options where available.

  • P5 ICEERS Support Center — integration-oriented support after difficult psychedelic experiences; not emergency care.

  • P6 Challenging Psychedelic Experiences Project — education and peer-support resources for post-psychedelic difficulties.

P7 Bottom line: safety, room, body, human, no rash actions, sleep if possible, then meaning.

The Longer Triage: What Kind of Trouble Is This?

P1 When somebody is in trouble, the first question is simple: what could hurt them now? A body in danger, a person moving toward harm, and a frightened mind in a terrible room are different problems. Treating all three as “a difficult process” is how people end up trying to breathe through poisoning or calling an annoying song ancestral trauma.

P2 image 2

  1. P3 Is the body failing—or is neurological status still unknown?

P4 Breathing trouble, collapse, a seizure lasting five minutes or repeating without normal recovery, dangerous overheating, severe or worsening chest pain, one-sided weakness, loss of speech or bodily control, serious injury, signs of real poisoning, or a credible risk of harm to self or others means medical help while acting. This is the branch where time matters and presence alone is not the intervention. Don’t make somebody “sit with it” while a body is going under.

P5 A person saying “my brain feels damaged,” “my brain is broken,” or “I’m losing control of my body” also belongs in this first branch until the symptoms are actively checked. The phrase is not a diagnosis. It is also not permission to call the problem anxiety because the person is high. Someone experiencing real neurological dysfunction may be unable to explain it more clearly.

P6 Use DMSO immediately while asking what the symptoms actually are. Can the person stay awake, breathe normally, smile evenly, raise both arms, repeat a simple sentence, walk, and control their bodily functions? Is there seizure activity, one-sided weakness, speech loss, severe headache, repeated vomiting, dangerous heat, chest pain, or a vision change? If the person cannot answer reliably, basic stability remains unknown rather than reassuring.

P7 If objective danger signs appear, use DMSO while somebody arranges medical help. If the screen is reassuring but the brain-damage feeling remains, continue the DMSO-first route and reassess promptly. With a psychedelic, safety now means stopping the trip with Ipomoea pes-caprae rather than merely calming it. With MDMA, move to magnesium and the broader electrolyte branch; I do not yet know whether pes-caprae works for MDMA.

P8 A severe altered state that is only too intense belongs in a different branch. When the person is breathing normally, not dangerously hot, not seizing, has no neurological-injury concern, remains conscious enough to cooperate, and can be kept physically safe, calming and stabilizing the trip may be enough.

P9 The emergency room is not magic, and for a physiologically stable psychedelic crisis it may be a poor environment. A scared, psychotic-feeling person often receives a benzodiazepine; those drugs don’t reliably address psychosis and can cause paradoxical agitation or fear in some people. Antipsychotics may work better for psychosis, but they carry their own risks and interactions, often administered by someone who doesn’t know what was taken. Calling for help is not the same thing as receiving good care. That is why the decision belongs to the actual symptom pattern rather than the word “overdose.”

  1. P10 Is the room making everything worse?

P11 Too much light. Bad music. A hostile person. A sitter trying too hard—or doing nothing. Five people talking. A phone buzzing. The wrong temperature. No bathroom, privacy, or sense of safety.

P12 A huge amount of what gets called “deep psychedelic crisis” is a nervous system begging for a less irritating room. Dim the light, lower the sound, stop the chatter, clear out extra people, offer water without forcing it, adjust the temperature, open bathroom access, stop touching unless touch is wanted, speak slowly, and please don’t explain the universe at someone who is scared. None of this is glamorous. It’s mercy.

  1. P13 Is this real emotional material?

P14 Crying, grieving, remembering, or touching a genuine wound calls for loving awareness. Interrogation, analysis every thirty seconds, and “bro, you’re purging ancestral karma” while somebody white-knuckles the carpet are generally less helpful.

P15 Stay present. A useful phrase is: This feeling is allowed to move through. I don’t have to become it. I can love the part of me that feels it. Or simply: Can I send love to the one who’s suffering?

P16 Don’t drag somebody out of real grief. Don’t trap them inside a loop that is grinding them down either. Love isn’t stubbornness.

  1. P17 Is the body writing the horror story?

P18 Sometimes the mind isn’t in a metaphysical crisis. The body feels awful and the altered mind writes cosmic horror to explain it. Nausea, heat, exhaustion, dehydration, overstimulation, and sleep loss all become spiritual content very quickly.

P19 A horrible thought isn’t automatically a revelation. The body may need quiet, water, cooling, warmth, rest, or a calmer room. The body is part of the spiritual field.

  1. P20 Can love be reached yet?

P21 This may be the deepest question, but it usually isn’t the first move. Someone who is too overwhelmed, frightened, sick, hot, or scrambled may be unable to reach “send love.” Stabilize first. Then love.

Call medics while acting for:

  • Trouble breathing, blue or gray lips, or respiratory depression

  • P1 A convulsive seizure lasting about five minutes, repeated seizures without normal recovery, or a first or substantially different seizure

  • P2 Collapse, inability to wake normally, or rapidly worsening consciousness

  • P3 Dangerous overheating, severe rigidity, or clonus with fever

  • P4 Sudden one-sided weakness, loss of speech, inability to walk normally, or loss of control of bodily functions

  • P5 A report that the brain feels damaged when the person cannot complete a basic neurological check or is rapidly worsening

  • P6 Severe, worsening, radiating, or exertional chest pain—or chest pain with breathlessness, sweating, faintness, neurological changes, or collapse

  • P7 Serious injury, uncontrolled bleeding, or a possible head or spinal injury

  • P8 Repeated vomiting, severe dehydration, blood, or severe abdominal pain

  • P9 Violence, self-harm, or a situation the sitter cannot physically contain

  • P10 Concerning symptoms after an MAOI plus a serotonergic substance

P11 The remedies below can begin while help is being arranged. Calling medics does not require waiting passively, and using a fast field remedy does not require giving up a time-sensitive medical option.

Bring in mental-health support when:

  • The person may be unsafe to themselves or others.

  • P1 Psychosis-like symptoms outlast the acute state.

  • P2 Sleep won’t return.

  • P3 Mania or suicidal thinking appears.

  • P4 Panic won’t settle.

  • P5 The person can’t function afterward.

  • P6 The trauma material is bigger than the room can hold.

Acute Medical and Neurological Rescue

P1 An emergency means act now. It does not always mean that an ambulance is the first or only useful action. Some symptom patterns call for medics while you use an immediate remedy; some severe but physically stable altered states may respond faster to field rescue than to hours in an emergency room.

P2 image 4

If Someone Says “My Brain Feels Damaged”: DMSO + Humic Acid First

P1 Do not dismiss that report because the person is high or cannot explain it in clinical language. It may be anxiety or excessive psychedelic intensity. It may also be acute neurological dysfunction, and the person experiencing it may be the least able to describe it neatly. DMSO is my first immediate remedy while I ask what the symptoms actually are. I usually know within about a minute whether it is helping. Smelling humic acid or tasting a tiny bit can also help a lot, in my experience, but I’m still collecting data from others.

P2 While using DMSO, check wakefulness, breathing, temperature, speech, facial symmetry, both arms, balance, walking, bodily control, seizure activity, severe headache, repeated vomiting, chest pain, and vision. A person who cannot answer or cooperate has not passed the screen; their neurological status is still unknown.

P3 I believe DMSO works better for stroke and most acute brain damage than anything else readily available to ordinary people. That judgment is stronger than the clinical evidence, but the evidence is not empty: a preliminary human ischemic-stroke trial reported possible neurological benefit; a small human series used DMSO for intracranial hypertension; and animal stroke research found substantial infarct reduction even when treatment began after ischemia. DMSO also suppresses NMDA- and AMPA-mediated excitotoxicity.

P4 For objective stroke-like signs, I would use DMSO immediately while someone calls medics. DMSO cannot identify whether the problem is a clot, a hemorrhage, low oxygen, an electrolyte crisis, or something else. The larger adjacent risk is losing a treatment window or missing a bleed because the symptoms improved enough to feel reassuring.

P5 After DMSO, the second move depends on the drug. With a psychedelic, a serious brain-damage feeling changes the objective from calming the trip to stopping it: I would use Ipomoea pes-caprae as the next move. With MDMA, I would move instead to magnesium chloride and the broader electrolyte branch. I do not yet have evidence that pes-caprae aborts MDMA.

Active Seizure

P1 Time the seizure. Protect the person from injury, clear dangerous objects, loosen tight clothing, and place them on their side when practical. Don’t restrain them and don’t put anything in their mouth while they are convulsing or not fully conscious.

P2 If an appropriate rapid seizure-rescue benzodiazepine is available, use it by its intended route. Benzodiazepines are the established first-line drug class for status epilepticus. An ordinary pill is not the same thing as a nasal, buccal, rectal, intramuscular, or intravenous rescue preparation, and respiratory depressants in the mix change the risk.

P3 I would also use topical magnesium chloride and consider topical DMSO with a known CBD preparation as adjuncts. CBD has established antiseizure activity in several epilepsy syndromes, and transdermal CBD reaches systemic circulation, although it has not been validated as rapid seizure rescue. DMSO reduced seizure activity in a rat temporal-lobe epilepsy model and has several neuroprotective mechanisms, but it produced the opposite effect at some doses in an absence-epilepsy model. I treat it as a plausible experimental adjunct rather than something to wait on while a seizure continues.

P4 Call medics for a convulsive seizure lasting about five minutes, repeated seizures without normal recovery, trouble breathing, dangerous heat, serious injury, a first seizure, or a seizure substantially different from the person’s known pattern.

MDMA: Jaw Tension Is the Magnesium Alarm

P1 With MDMA, I treat meaningful jaw tension as the earliest warning that the body needs more magnesium. This comes from repeated field experience rather than a direct clinical trial. Everyone I know who has taken enough magnesium chloride to make the jaw tension fully release has avoided the other complications I have seen when people let that tension continue.

P2 A token dose may not be enough. On multiple occasions, I have dissolved Dead Sea magnesium chloride hexahydrate flakes in water and taken approximately one gram per minute orally for around twenty minutes before my jaw fully released. I have tolerated around twenty grams without diarrhea; for me, diarrhea has appeared only above roughly forty grams. Magnesium chloride is also generally less likely to cause diarrhea than several other replacement salts, including forms deliberately used as laxatives.

P3 This was not one exceptional episode. I have repeatedly needed large amounts when taking MDMA. My practical endpoint is the jaw releasing, rather than taking one conventional supplement dose and assuming the problem has been handled.

P4 Interestingly, magnesium taken before the MDMA peak also makes the peak less euphoric for me. That suggests it may both meet a rapidly increasing magnesium need and directly moderate part of MDMA’s excitatory effect. For harm reduction, slightly less euphoria is a reasonable price for a relaxed jaw and what appears in my experience to be a much lower risk of the state escalating.

P5 My second wife had severe convulsions after an MDMA overdose. At the hospital, her potassium was found to be critically low—described to me as essentially zero—and IV potassium was the only treatment they gave her. That experience is why I treat serious MDMA toxicity as a broader electrolyte emergency, even though magnesium is the first and most useful early intervention when jaw tension appears.

P6 Jaw tension should also mean no more MDMA, less exertion and heat, and closer monitoring. Worsening headache, confusion, vomiting, weakness, palpitations, collapse, or seizure raises concern about sodium, potassium, temperature, hydration, kidneys, and broader toxicity. MDMA is documented to cause acute kidney injury through several pathways, and controlled research confirms that hyponatremia can occur after a single dose.

P7 People with impaired kidneys do need to be more cautious with large, rapid magnesium doses because they may not clear magnesium normally. But they should be far more worried about the MDMA. MDMA can cause overheating, rhabdomyolysis, dangerous electrolyte shifts, and acute kidney injury. Kidney impairment is a strong reason not to take MDMA—not a reason to sit there with worsening jaw tension after taking it. I would not tell somebody with serious kidney impairment to copy my one-gram-per-minute approach blindly; I would tell them to treat the situation as higher-risk and get help sooner.

Chest Pain and Injury

P1 DMSO is also my first remedy for many injuries and some chest pain, with the decision about medics based on severity and the surrounding symptoms. After I overused erythritol, I developed angina that lasted for days. I took DMSO, the pain disappeared within about a minute, and it never returned.

P2 The cardiovascular science is preclinical but relevant. In rats, DMSO pretreatment reduced myocardial infarct size by about three quarters and sharply reduced cardiac injury markers. Other work found inhibition of tissue factor, platelet activation, and arterial thrombus formation. Myocardial-ischemia study; tissue-factor and thrombosis study; human platelet study.

P3 Mild, familiar, non-progressive discomfort in a fully alert person is different from new, severe, persistent, worsening, radiating, or exertional pain—or pain with breathlessness, sweating, faintness, neurological changes, or collapse. In the second branch, I would use DMSO while arranging medical evaluation. DMSO can also block pain signals, so pain relief alone does not prove that the underlying problem has ended.

Recent Oral Toxic Ingestion

P1 “Overdose” is too vague to choose a remedy. Ask what was swallowed, how much, when, and what the body is actually doing: respiratory depression, stimulant toxicity, serotonin toxicity, seizure, dangerous overheating, cardiovascular symptoms, severe agitation, objective neurological dysfunction, vomiting, diarrhea, or something else.

P2 Activated charcoal may help after some recent oral ingestions when the substance binds to it and the person is fully alert enough to swallow safely. It does not bind every toxin, can adsorb necessary medication, and creates aspiration risk in somebody who is vomiting, sedated, seizing, or unable to protect the airway. Clinical review.

P3 Poison control may be useful when the substance, combination, delayed risk, or value of charcoal is unclear. I do not treat it as a mandatory ritual; usefulness varies by country and by whoever answers. It is decision support, not first aid.

Suspected Serotonin Syndrome (SS)

P1 Serotonin syndrome overlaps with several symptoms above, but the rescue measures in the other branches do not necessarily treat it. It can follow serotonergic medication changes, combinations, or excessive exposure. Psychedelics, MDMA, DXM, many antidepressants, tramadol, linezolid, MAOIs, and some opioids can contribute, especially in combinations.

Recognizing serotonin toxicity

P1 Clinicians often use the Hunter criteria. One or more of the following patterns makes serotonin toxicity more likely:

  • Spontaneous clonus: involuntary rhythmic muscle contractions and relaxations occurring without stimulation.

  • P2 Inducible clonus plus agitation or sweating.

  • P3 Ocular clonus plus agitation or sweating: rapid rhythmic involuntary eye movements.

  • P4 Tremor plus hyperreflexia: shaking together with abnormally brisk reflexes.

  • P5 Hypertonia plus fever: rigidity with temperature above 38°C (100.4°F), together with ocular or inducible clonus.

P6 This is a recognition aid rather than a command to wait for every criterion before acting. Dangerous heat, rigidity, clonus, confusion, or rapid worsening deserves medical help.

Do this immediately when serotonin toxicity is reasonably suspected:

  1. Stop all suspected serotonergic substances. No more SSRI, SNRI, MAOI, tramadol, MDMA, DXM cough syrup, 5-HTP, St John’s wort, SAM-e, linezolid, stimulant redoses, or harmala/ayahuasca-type MAOI exposure.

  2. P1 If the person is fully alert and able to swallow, I would give cyproheptadine early. It antagonizes serotonin receptors. Case reports describe rapid improvement, including complete resolution within two hours in a small series, while a recent systematic review found the evidence for major outcomes inconclusive. Case series; systematic review. I would not wait to see whether it works before cooling, stopping serotonergic substances, and arranging help for moderate or severe signs.

  3. P2 Call emergency help and arrange transport for moderate or severe signs. Don’t drive yourself. Poison control may help when the combination is unclear, but it is not the treatment.

  4. P3 Cool the person without delaying transport. Move to a cool room, remove excess clothing, use a fan and cool damp cloths, and place ice packs wrapped in cloth at the neck, armpits, or groin when the person is hot. Don’t put a confused, seizing, or very weak person in a bath. Fever reducers do not solve heat generated by extreme muscle activity.

  5. P4 Keep stimulation low and prevent exertion. No pacing to “burn it off,” exercise, crowds, or arguments. Akathisia can create a strong urge to move, but muscle activity can worsen heat and rhabdomyolysis risk.

  6. P5 Hydrate only when fully alert and not vomiting. Small sips of an appropriate oral rehydration drink are reasonable. Don’t force fluids, food, pills, or charcoal when someone is confused, vomiting, sleepy, or at seizure risk.

  7. P6 Gather the evidence for the ER. Bring pill bottles and packages. Write down exact substances, dose, timing, last dose, recent increases, supplements, recreational drugs, temperature, pulse, and blood pressure when available.

  8. P7 Hospitals may use benzodiazepines, external cooling, IV fluids, monitoring, and intensive supportive care. Niacin is unsafe for serotonin syndrome. DMSO is also not part of this branch. I would consider oral magnesium chloride when the person is fully alert, especially with MDMA-related jaw tension or muscle activity, but it does not replace treatment of dangerous heat, clonus, rigidity, or instability.

My Acute Rescue Hierarchy

P1 This hierarchy is for acute situations, including emergencies—not merely uncomfortable trips. Some branches call for medics in parallel. Others describe severe but physiologically stable altered states where a fast field remedy may be more useful than waiting hours to be given a generic sedative.

P2 Psychedelic culture often treats every crisis as emotional or spiritual. Sometimes the person is nauseated, overheated, sleep-deprived, poisoned, dealing with an electrolyte problem, reacting to a bad combination, or feeling mentally destroyed because the body is in trouble.

P3 A trip with diarrhea, nausea, toxic heaviness, a dirty body-feeling, loss of bodily control, jaw tension, neurological disorganization, or the sense that the mind is going crazy because the gut or brain is going crazy belongs in a different branch from a mainly emotional crisis.

P4 The hierarchy appears before the mystical sections because love doesn’t require us to ignore the body. Medical danger may need medical help while field rescue begins. A bad room gets fixed. A gut-driven mental hell is not automatically shadow work. A dose or combination problem does not need to become a spiritual curriculum.

The Core Order When the Brain Feels Damaged

  1. P1 DMSO immediately. Smell humic acid, take a 5mg lick of it. Smell the Metta NMT sniffer (described in loveyhuasca guide).

  2. P2 Ask what the symptoms actually are while the DMSO is being used. Do not manufacture reassurance from missing information.

  3. P3 If this is a psychedelic, use Ipomoea pes-caprae to stop the trip. At this point safety matters more than preserving the experience.

  4. P4 If this is MDMA, move to magnesium chloride and the broader electrolyte branch instead. I do not yet have data that pes-caprae aborts MDMA.

  5. P5 Bring in medical help in parallel for objective neurological signs, dangerous heat, prolonged or repeated seizure, breathing trouble, severe chest pain, collapse, or when basic stability cannot be established.

P6 After medical danger, environment, dose, gut toxicity, electrolytes, and biochemical overload have been considered, we can return to the deeper question: is this sorrow, fear, shame, inner-child material, demons, jinn, shadow thoughts, or a mind learning to love what it previously exiled?

How I label the evidence

  • P1 Established acute treatment: standard seizure first aid, rapid rescue benzodiazepines, cooling dangerous hyperthermia, and treating documented electrolyte abnormalities.

  • P2 Limited human evidence: interventions with clinical reports or small trials but no strong modern outcome evidence.

  • P3 Mechanism or preclinical evidence: biologically plausible effects shown mainly in cells or animals.

  • P4 Personal field signal: what I have repeatedly observed in myself or other people. Repetition and a clear immediate endpoint make an anecdote more useful, but they do not turn it into a population rate.

P5 I use the strongest applicable evidence without pretending every useful rescue has already received the trial it deserves.

Gut-Toxin and Parasite-Die-Off Tools

P1 These are the first things I would use, even preventively on the come-up, because I want to address possible toxins before assigning the distress to something more mysterious.

Zeolite and Ferment in ZHoly3O

P1 When the trip feels like sick stomach, nausea, diarrhea, toxic heaviness, a dirty body-feeling, parasite die-off, or mental hell driven by gut hell, zeolite and the ZHoly3O ferment are often my first thoughts.

P2 Many ferments help to different degrees, including kombucha, kimchi, and apple vinegar. I prefer fermented beet juice made with apple vinegar and salt. Smelling the ferment helps a lot. Do

  • P1 Speak slowly and use fewer words.

  • P2 Ask simple questions: “Would less light help?” “Music or quiet?” “Closer or farther away?” “Water?”

  • P3 Reflect without arguing: “That sounds terrifying.” “I’m with you.” “Let’s breathe together.”

  • P5 Keep the room quiet, private, warm, and physically safe.

  • P6 Call for help early when safety is slipping.

P2 A bad trip does not prove that you are broken, punished, unspiritual, impure, or permanently damaged. It means the nervous system, meaning-making system, body, environment, memories, dose, or relationships became overwhelmed. The response is care rather than blame.

Spiritual ≠ “Getting Your Ass Kicked”

P1 The triage above is meant to make trips useful. Some people believe they deserve suffering because of karma or self-hatred. Suffering by itself fixes nobody. Suffering that yields insight requires enough clarity of mind and heart to learn from it.

P2 This is one side of the difference between spiritual bypassing and spiritual practice:

P3 Bypassing reaches for an ultimate view—“we are all One,” “I don’t exist,” “this is all karma,” “everything is Love”—while ignoring the person shaking in the corner.

P4 Practice says love is here, so we make the room safer, call for help when needed, stop pretending, and hold the experience without becoming it.

P5 That is tripsitting, shadow work, and the path becoming practical.

The Other Bypass: Feeling Good Forever

P1 A well-rounded trip should offer practical lessons and contact with suffering as well as freedom from suffering, lest we become “so spiritually high that we are no earthly good.” “Life is full of suffering” is Buddha’s First Noble Truth. Ignoring that side of life becomes dissociation or spiritual bypassing in the opposite direction, followed eventually by statements like “Everything is Love, including violence.”

P2 Many people meditate or take psychedelics to feel good. I am definitely one of them, and Buddha also said meditation should generally feel good. P5 Sometimes the real-world smackdown is unavoidable for deeper learning.

Give the Session a Beginning and an End

P1 image 13

P2 For planned altered-state work, think through the beginning, middle, and end before starting. A lot of prevention is boring: being rested, starting before everyone is exhausted, leaving time afterward, having food ready, and knowing how the session ends.

P3 When you have a choice:

  • Rest and timing: don’t begin already depleted. When possible, begin earlier in the day so there is still time for the experience, food, ordinary contact, and sleep afterward.

  • P4 Physical safety: know the substance and approximate dose, review combinations and medical concerns, prepare the room, and decide in advance what requires outside help.

  • P5 Intention without a script: know what you are open to exploring without demanding a particular revelation, memory, emotional arc, or spiritual result.

  • P6 Low-directivity support: the sitter protects safety and offers choices. They do not force music, touch, interpretation, confession, prayer, or insight.

  • P7 Closure: stop adding stimulation, return to the room and date, eat and hydrate when safe, write down the basic facts, and let the body understand that the session has ended.

  • P8 Integration time: leave the rest of the day and, when possible, the next morning free. Sleep and food come before turning the experience into a new life plan.

P9 Preparation → physical safety → intention → low-directivity support → closure → sleep and food → next-day integration.

P10 Use this sequence for planned sessions. Once somebody is already in trouble, return to the acute checklist instead of trying to improve the ceremony plan.

Dealing With Sorrow: Missing Versus Sending Love

P3 Relax, unwind, unbind, and unweave the identity illusion, mind, and unified experience. My pamphlet on already-perfect goes further into this.

After the Storm

P1 Don’t rush to turn a hard experience into content. Sleep. Eat simple food. Drink water. Walk. Journal. Pray and meditate gently. Talk with one grounded person, rather than twenty chaotic people. Don’t make huge decisions, diagnose yourself from one intense night, or assume every vision was literal and every fear was false. Let the system settle.

P2 image 15

Minimum Viable Integration

  1. P1 First 24 hours: sleep, food, water, warmth, ordinary contact, and no big moves.

  2. P2 Next few days: write the facts—substance, dose when known, setting, events, triggers, what helped, and what made things worse.

  3. P3 Then: consider what the experience may have meant emotionally, spiritually, relationally, or practically.

  4. P4 Last: make small behavioral changes that reduce suffering and increase love.

What Kind of Aftermath Is This?

P1 When symptoms linger, name the pattern rather than collapsing everything into “I’m broken.” Different patterns need different support.

  • P2 Anxiety and fear: grounding, routine, reduced stimulation, CBT-style reality testing, and therapy when it persists.

  • P3 Sleep disturbance and nightmares: sleep hygiene, no more substances, and professional help when severe or prolonged.

  • P4 Derealization and depersonalization: ordinary-world grounding, exercise, nature, outside attention, less rumination, peer or family support, and therapy when needed.

  • P5 Visual distortions or HPPD-like symptoms: reduce stress and substance use; seek professional support when symptoms interfere with life.

  • P6 Social disconnection and shame: one or two safe humans, peer support, and no social-media crowdsourcing while raw.

  • P7 Existential shock: write it down, slow down, and return to practical love before building a metaphysical system.

  • P8 Trauma material: trauma-informed therapy or integration support, without immediate confrontation while destabilized.

  • P9 Paranoia or psychosis-like beliefs: professional support quickly, especially when beliefs drive unsafe action.

  • P10 Somatic hyperarousal: gentle movement, breathing, grounding, and medical care for severe, unusual, or worrying symptoms.

P11 No “another ceremony will fix this” reflex.

P12 Don’t take more psychedelics, cannabis, ketamine, intense breathwork, fast, push kundalini, deprive yourself of sleep, meditate or stretch to extremes, or schedule “another ceremony to fix it” while destabilized. Stabilize through sleep, food, routine, sober support, ordinary body, and ordinary world. Deep practice can wait until the floor is back.

P13 Then ask what actually happened. What was medical or environmental? What was emotional? What was old trauma? What was fantasy? What was useful? What changed behavior? What needs therapy, an apology, rest, or plain practical action?

P14 The fruit of insight isn’t how cosmic the story sounds. It’s whether suffering goes down and love gets more available.
