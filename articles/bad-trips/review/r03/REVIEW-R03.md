**How it would read:**

> Serotonin syndrome overlaps with several symptoms above, but the rescue measures in the other branches do not necessarily treat it. It can follow serotonergic medication changes, combinations, or excessive exposure. Ayahuasca and other MAOI brews, MDMA, DXM, many antidepressants, tramadol, linezolid, other MAOIs, and some opioids can contribute, especially in combinations.

**Why:** One review's table lists LSD among the drugs tied to serotonin syndrome, with Syrian rue and harmine among the MAOIs (PubMed: [Scotton et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1178646919873925)). A review of psychedelics and serotonin toxicity says true toxicity typically comes from an overdose or an MAOI, and that serotonergic drugs without an MAOI "are low risk in combination with psychedelics that also do not contain MAOIs" (PubMed: [Malcolm & Thomas 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-021-05876-x)). So the psychedelics that carry the risk are the MAOI brews, and naming ayahuasca warns a reader who doesn't know it contains one.

**What stays:** The rest of the list and of the paragraph.

**For you to weigh:** "Psychedelics" isn't wrong, since LSD is on one review's list. It's broader than the risk.

**My advice:** Approve.

**What changes:** "somebody low in histamine-processing bacteria" becomes "somebody who breaks down histamine poorly".

**Now** (the words it changes in bold):

> Many ferments help to different degrees, including kombucha, kimchi, and apple vinegar. I prefer fermented beet juice made with apple vinegar and salt. Smelling the ferment helps a lot. Drinking a spoonful can also help, although it may cause itching in somebody **low in histamine-processing bacteria**, which then requires activated charcoal to reduce gut histamine.

**How it would read:**

> Many ferments help to different degrees, including kombucha, kimchi, and apple vinegar. I prefer fermented beet juice made with apple vinegar and salt. Smelling the ferment helps a lot. Drinking a spoonful can also help, although it may cause itching in somebody who breaks down histamine poorly, which then requires activated charcoal to reduce gut histamine.

**Why:** Ferments are high in histamine, and itching is one of the signs of histamine intolerance. But the review on it puts the intolerance down mainly to low levels of DAO, an enzyme of the gut lining: one study it cites found low DAO in 80% of 316 people with symptoms (PubMed: [Comas-Basté et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/biom10081181)). The bacteria it discusses are the ones that make histamine, in food. Charcoal took out 61% of the histamine in fish sauce (PubMed: [In et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2024.140169)); no study tests it in the gut, so that part stays as your experience.

**What stays:** Your ferments, smelling them first, and activated charcoal for the itching.

**For you to weigh:** Whether the bacteria were your own idea, one you'd rather keep as your view.

**My advice:** Approve.

## W-GLORY: A 2026 study on beach morning glory's safety (New study)

*Beach Morning Glory – Ipomoea pes-caprae, P5*

**What changes:** A sentence after "A whole leaf can easily end the trip and, in my experience, may prevent tripping for days afterward.": Daily use is another matter: in a 2026 study, a leaf tea given every day to mice with tumors, at a dose scaled from the traditional tea, caused skin sores and killed 7 of 10 within 15 days, though one large dose did healthy mice no immediate harm. It would link the study.

**Now** (the words it changes in bold):

> A whole leaf can easily end the trip and, in my experience, **may prevent tripping for days afterward.**

**How it would read:**

> A whole leaf can easily end the trip and, in my experience, may prevent tripping for days afterward. Daily use is another matter: in a 2026 study, a leaf tea given every day to mice with tumors, at a dose scaled from the traditional tea, caused skin sores and killed 7 of 10 within 15 days, though one large dose did healthy mice no immediate harm.

**Why:** PubMed: [Llerenas-Aguirre et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18142248). Mice carrying human breast tumors (bred without a working immune system, so the tumors take) drank a tea of Ipomoea pes-caprae leaves every day, at a dose scaled from the traditional Mexican tea of 12 to 14 dried leaves in a liter a day. By day 10, 4 of the 10 had blistering skin sores. By day 15, 7 of 10 had died, and the tea was stopped. None of the untreated mice had sores or died. A single large dose given to 7 healthy mice did no immediate harm. The authors warn that sick mice may be more vulnerable than people. It's the only study I found on the plant's safety, and the guide has nothing on it yet.

**What stays:** Your whole-leaf line and everything else on beach morning glory.

**For you to weigh:** Whether you want it at all: it's daily dosing in sick mice, and one leaf is about a thirteenth of that tea's daily leaves. If you do, you may want it in your words (Revise).

**My advice:** Approve, or Revise it into your words.

**How it would read:**

> Reduced sleep: DMSO reduces how long I sleep when I take too much, take it too late, or use it over too many consecutive days. It builds up past a threshold that seems to differ between people. In rats, higher doses made sleep lighter. Some people sleep better with DMSO because it reduces whatever symptom had been keeping them awake.

**Why:** The linked study gave 20 rats one injection of DMSO at the start of their sleep period and recorded the next 3 hours. It found lighter sleep, not shorter sleep, and it didn't test late doses or days in a row, so "proven" says more than it found.

**What stays:** Your lines on how DMSO shortens your sleep and builds up, and that some people sleep better with it.

**For you to weigh:** Nothing much: your experience stays as yours, and the rat line now says what the rats showed.

**Now** (the words it changes in bold):

> **Don't nebulize too much DMSO when your lungs are full of smoking-related tar, because it can carry those substances into the blood too quickly. Weakness is an early symptom.**

**How it would read:** this paragraph goes.

**Why:** I found no study of inhaled DMSO carrying tar or smoke residue into the blood, or of weakness from it. DMSO does dissolve the particles in cigarette smoke (labs use it to extract them, as in PubMed: [Huang et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1159/000350866)), so the idea isn't strange. ## W-NSAID: DMSO with corticosteroids and NSAIDs (Says more than the studies)

*DMSO, P9*

**What changes:** "including corticosteroids and NSAIDs" becomes ": DMSO can strengthen other medicines taken with it".

**Now** (the words it changes in bold):

> Don't take it with drugs that should not absorb rapidly, **including corticosteroids and NSAIDs**. With harmalas, don't put the harmala in the DMSO or take them at exactly the same time; taking them a bit apart is totally fine, and the point is to take DMSO if you've already absorbed the harmalas and the aya is too crazy.

**How it would read:**

> Don't take it with drugs that should not absorb rapidly: DMSO can strengthen other medicines taken with it. With harmalas, don't put the harmala in the DMSO or take them at exactly the same time; taking them a bit apart is totally fine, and the point is to take DMSO if you've already absorbed the harmalas and the aya is too crazy.

**Why:** Both drug classes it names are given with DMSO on purpose. An FDA-approved diclofenac (NSAID) solution for knee arthritis contains DMSO ([its FDA label](https://nctr-crs.fda.gov/fdalabel/services/spl/set-ids/609c5344-9c97-4fa8-ad53-d0f622ce64ec/spl-doc); PubMed: [Marren 2011](https://doi.org/10.3810/psm.2011.09.1923)), and DMSO with triamcinolone, a corticosteroid, is a bladder treatment tested in a randomized trial (PubMed: [Moss et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/nau.25142)). The rule itself holds: DMSO's US label says "Some data indicate that dimethyl sulfoxide potentiates other concomitantly administered medications" ([RIMSO-50 label](https://www.drugs.com/pro/rimso-50.html)).

**What stays:** The rule, and your harmala timing.

**For you to weigh:** If you want one named drug: the label of sulindac, an NSAID, says "Concurrent use of dimethyl sulfoxide and sulindac is not recommended". The pair lowers sulindac's active form, and nerve damage has been reported with it ([UK label](https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/8783/smpc/print), [US label](https://medlibrary.org/lib/rx/meds/sulindac/page/5/)). Revise to add it.

**My advice:** Approve.
