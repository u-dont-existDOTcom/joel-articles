## CBD-TRIAL: The skin-gel trial: what it found, and the 7-OH-CBD point (Says more than the study)

*Active Seizure, P4*

**What changes:** The end of your second CBD paragraph becomes: "... where they used a CBD skin gel, and it didn't do any better than placebo. The gel got CBD into the blood at about the levels of the starting oral dose, 5 to 10mg/kg. The approved oral doses go up to 20 or 25mg/kg a day, so the dosing may have been too low. Transdermal CBD also skips the liver's first pass, so less of it gets converted to 7-OH-CBD, a form that stopped seizures in mice about as well as CBD itself."

**Now** (the words it changes in bold):

> And topical CBD hasn't been well-studied. There was a short 12-week trial done, where they used a CBD skin gel, and **it did show that transdermal absorption works like oral CBD's but the dosing was too low**, and transdermal CBD also **doesn't get converted to the potentially more active form (7-OH-CBD) by the liver**.

**How it would read:**

> And topical CBD hasn't been well-studied. There was a short 12-week trial done, where they used a CBD skin gel, and it didn't do any better than placebo. The gel got CBD into the blood at about the levels of the starting oral dose, 5 to 10mg/kg. The approved oral doses go up to 20 or 25mg/kg a day, so the dosing may have been too low. Transdermal CBD also skips the liver's first pass, so less of it gets converted to 7-OH-CBD, a form that stopped seizures in mice about as well as CBD itself.

**Why:** Three things, from the trial's paper ([O'Brien et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9270696/)) and the [Epidiolex label](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2026/210365s024s029s030lbl.pdf). First, the sentence never says what the trial found, so a reader can't tell why the dose comes up: neither dose of the gel cut seizures more than placebo did. Second, the paper doesn't say the dose was too low. It says the gel reached blood levels that "overlap those reported with oral cannabidiol (5-10 mg/kg)", and that benefit had been seen with higher gel doses (500 to 1,000mg a day) in children with other epilepsies; the label's oral doses start at 5mg/kg a day and go up to 20 (Lennox-Gastaut, Dravet) or 25 (tuberous sclerosis). So "may have been too low" holds, and "was too low" says more than anyone has shown. Third, CBD through the skin still gets broken down by the liver once it's in the blood; what it skips is the first pass, where swallowed CBD gets converted before it reaches the rest of the body. And 7-OH-CBD stopped seizures in mice about as well as CBD did, not better ([Whalley et al., AES 2017](https://aesnet.org/abstractslisting/the-human-metabolite-of-cannabidiol--7-hydroxy-cannabidiol--but-not-7-carboxy-cannabidiol--is-anticonvulsant-in-the-maximal-electroshock-seizure-threshold-test-(mest)-in-mouse)), so "potentially more active" goes.

**What stays:** Your opening ("And topical CBD hasn't been well-studied. There was a short 12-week trial done, where they used a CBD skin gel"), your point that the dose may have been too low, and your 7-OH-CBD point, which still says why the skin route might do less.

## SEIZ-WARN: What can help before a seizure takes hold (from your note) (From your note)

*Active Seizure, P5*

**What changes:** A new paragraph after your DMSO paragraph, before the call-for-help line: "Some people feel a seizure coming before it takes hold, and in that warning a few things people report can still stop it for some of them, depending on what sets it off. If it's flashing or flickering light, cover one eye with a hand and turn away from the light, because closing the eyes doesn't block enough of it. Some people stop a seizure at the warning stage with a strong smell like jasmine, and some train themselves to link one smell with feeling calm and then sniff it when a seizure threatens. When overstimulation is what sets it off, a cool, quiet, dark and comfortable space can help."

**New line**: Some people feel a seizure coming before it takes hold, and in that warning a few things people report can still stop it for some of them, depending on what sets it off. If it's flashing or flickering light, cover one eye with a hand and turn away from the light, because closing the eyes doesn't block enough of it. Some people stop a seizure at the warning stage with a strong smell like jasmine, and some train themselves to link one smell with feeling calm and then sniff it when a seizure threatens. When overstimulation is what sets it off, a cool, quiet, dark and comfortable space can help.

**Why:** Your note listed what people report, the Epilepsy Foundation included, "but there's nothing that works for everyone of course". Checked one by one: covering one eye is the standard advice for light-triggered seizures, with a hand, and not closing the eyes ([Epilepsy Action](https://www.epilepsy.org.uk/info/seizure-triggers/photosensitive-epilepsy): "cover one eye with your hand and turn away. Do not close your eyes"). A strong smell at the warning stage stopped one patient's seizures in a classic case report ([Efron 1956](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13364083/)), and in Tim Betts's clinic people who learned to link an oil's smell (jasmine, ylang ylang, lavender, camomile) with feeling relaxed smelled it when a seizure threatened; a third were seizure-free a year later, in a study with no control group ([Epilepsy Society, July 2026](https://epilepsysociety.org.uk/sites/default/files/2026-07/ComplementarytherapiesJuly2026.pdf), which also warns that some oils can make epilepsy worse). The cool, quiet, dark and comfortable space is your experience; I found no study of it. I left out splashing water (nothing I found, and first-aid advice for a seizure doesn't include it) and music (its studies are of listening every day for weeks, not during a seizure). All of these are for the warning stage: once someone is convulsing, the section's first paragraph and the call-for-help line are what applies, which is why it goes just before that line.

**What stays:** Everything else in the section, in its order.
