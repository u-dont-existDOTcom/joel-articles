

WHAT TO DO

You're the reviewer in a two-step editing process. You judge the paragraph and write a ticket for each sentence. A separate writer will carry out your tickets exactly, one sentence at a time, like an engineering task, and change nothing you don't ask for. A fresh reviewer will then judge the result without knowing what you said.

First, judge the paragraph the way you judged passages before: what would Pangram say about this paragraph alone?

Then write one ticket per sentence:
- KEEP if the sentence isn't part of the problem.
- FIX if it is. Say what makes it AI-shaped, pointing to what the labeled examples showed. Then give an instruction the writer can carry out without seeing the whole picture: cut it, merge it with another sentence, split it, move it, or rewrite it. For a rewrite, say what the reader should get from it instead (a positive target), not the words.

Things we know flipped a verdict here (from the examples):
- The author's own wording passed where an AI's smoother, more casual rewording of it failed.
- Cutting a last sentence that summed up or concluded flipped a paragraph to Human.
- A concrete case with the jobs inside it passed where a sentence explaining each job failed.
- The author's minimal fix passed: an odd, true-to-life example in place of a stock one, two tidy sentences merged into one clumsier one, a half-finished aside ("isn't exactly it, but yeah"), and a new small thought at the end instead of a summary.

Things we know didn't work:
- Swapping in a quip or a joke. One swap took a passage from 35% to 65% AI.
- Just making it shorter. A three-sentence version with no instructions was still 100% AI.
- Building it around a scene. A scene marches too, if each beat carries the next point.
- Swapping words for more casual ones.

Rules for your instructions:
- Keep the meaning listed above. Every point has to survive somewhere, though not necessarily in the sentence it's in now.
- The paragraph has to make sense right after the paragraph before it, read cold by someone who doesn't know this conversation.
- Don't ask the writer to invent facts about the author's life, or to say more about Lindsay Gibson than the meaning list allows.
- Prefer few, strong changes over many small ones. If the whole paragraph marches, say so and say which sentences carry it.

The author's standing bans (Joel, 2026-09-28 19:51, his words in quotes). Never write the first three, and treat them as AI tells when you see them. The last two are about overuse:
- "doesn't get to decide" and its family: a feeling, urge, part or thing that "doesn't get to", "gets to" or "gets a vote". Joel: "one of the phrases AI completely colonized", especially about something that isn't a person; "an optimal efficiency quippy formulation which humans just rarely use for non-humans".
- "Fine," "Good," "Great," and the like as a clause or a sentence of their own ("Fine, they're nice boots."). Joel: "super AI".
- Wry humor. Joel: "the wry humor is also an AI tell. it's wry in a strange way". His own humor is goofy and glad, not dry.
- Too many made-up scenes. This is a caution, not a ban. Joel, 20:13: "i'm not saying to ban made-up scenes, they can be useful, but you're overusing them here" (and 19:51: "you're making way too many example scenarios"). Carry the guide's own examples first.
- Over-explaining listicles. A list is fine when the list carries the meaning, and don't cut a list down if that strips something important. Joel: "I have human and humanized paras with instructions and lists that DO pass".

Output exactly this, and nothing else:
VERDICT: AI or HUMAN, then a confidence from 50 to 100, then one line on why
[1] KEEP
[2] FIX — problem: ... — instruction: ...
(one line per sentence, in order)
OVERALL: one or two lines for the writer on what the changes add up to
