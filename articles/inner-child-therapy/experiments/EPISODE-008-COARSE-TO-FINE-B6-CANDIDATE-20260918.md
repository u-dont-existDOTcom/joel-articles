### Also Look Outward

After one terrible fight, it can feel like you've finally figured the other person out. Then they come back later and actually talk about what you raised. They may still disagree with you. The boundary that made you furious may still be there. Suddenly the first conversation is harder to use as a complete explanation of who they are.

You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.

Then another subject comes up and somehow you wind up back in the same place: your point has disappeared and you're taking care of their reaction. That kind of repetition is closer to what [Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) asks you to notice about emotional immaturity. You still may not know why it's happening.

And it gets harder to sort if you also really did hurt them. You owe repair for that. Maybe you apologize, and the apology is completely real, but they are still disappointed, so you keep going. A few sentences later you're backing away from the thing you originally came to say. What are you apologizing for now? The guilt can be real and still not answer that. It also doesn't tell you whether the other person is manipulating you.

Maybe they understand your point perfectly and still think you're wrong. Maybe your no still seems unfair to them. You explain it again. Then you hear yourself explaining why the last explanation didn't come out right. At some point there may simply be nothing else to add.
