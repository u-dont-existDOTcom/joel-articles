### Also Look Outward

One awful conversation can leave you pretty certain you've finally figured somebody out. Then they come back later and actually deal with what you raised. They may still disagree with you, and you may still hate the boundary they're holding. I wouldn't know what to make of the first fight after that except that it was awful. What I'd watch is whether the same kind of ending starts showing up in completely different conversations. [Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity is helpful there. Different subject, same ending: somehow your experience is the part that never gets much room.

You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.

If you really did hurt them, there is something to repair. An apology can get bigger than you meant it to. You meant to own what you actually did, and before long you're wondering whether you were allowed to have the complaint at all because they're still upset. Maybe you were wrong about more than you first thought. But if you're trying to work out how much of the argument was actually yours, feeling guilty is pretty poor evidence. It doesn't tell you they're manipulating you either.

They may still think your no is unfair after you've explained it as well as you know how. Maybe the first explanation really was bad, and the second one comes out clearer. But there are also times when they understood you the first time. They still don't agree. You don't have to keep producing new versions of the same no.
