If you want to kill yourself and you're about to act, or you can't stop yourself, or you're badly disoriented or intoxicated, or the means are right in front of you, first get another human being involved, and put distance between yourself and the means. That isn't my answer to whether life is worth living. It keeps the question open long enough for you to look into it.

Before getting philosophical, make sure there's an adult in the room. If the part that wants to die feels like all of you, use the Witness or the borrowed-adult move from earlier, and invite in any part that can watch what's happening, protect your body, or put off a decision that can't be undone. If you can already watch the suicidal state without being completely swallowed by it, you can skip this step.

Then ask: what is the self you want to kill? Is that self the same thing as your body? Why? Do you have the same body in dreams? What exactly do you expect your body's death to accomplish? If the problem is a state of mind, like self-hatred or despair, shame or loneliness, what makes you so sure that destroying the body ends it?

Buddhism doesn't teach that death is an off switch, or a free get-out-of-jail card back to the source. It teaches that repeated thought and intention shape the mind, that this keeps going through death and rebirth, that hell realms are possible, and that a human birth is a rare chance to understand suffering and end its causes. You don't have to believe that. But if you're about to bet your life on the opposite, look into it first. Near-death experiences and seriously studied rebirth reports can't give you reliable odds about death, but they can shake the certainty that destroying the body guarantees nothing comes after.

You can see that momentum within this life, too. Rumination feeds rumination. Isolation can deepen despair, and the more you hate yourself, the better your mind gets at it. Meanwhile physical life keeps handing you obstacles and redirections: other people, the world you can touch, responsibilities, practice, help. If the many traditions, and the evidence some researchers point to, are right that the mind's causal process goes on after the body dies, killing the body may take away some of those redirections without taking away the suffering.

And if losing everything is already on the table, what supposedly impossible change is still too frightening to try? A relationship? A job? A home? An identity, an obligation, a belief, a role? Try the changes you can take back before the one you can't. Look into the self, death, rebirth, love, and the life you've been afraid to change, while you still have a human body and other people around to help redirect the path.
