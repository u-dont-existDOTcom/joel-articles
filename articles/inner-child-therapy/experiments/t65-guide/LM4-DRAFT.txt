Part of love is wanting someone to be well, and how far that wish can reach depends partly on what kind of happiness you know is possible. If the happiness you know is food, safety, affection, friends, work that means something and some help when you need it, then wanting those things for somebody is love. Babies get some of this already. Practical love matters a lot.

But a big spiritual experience can show you a kind of peace, or a love, you didn't know existed, and then "I want you to be happy" suddenly means something much bigger. That's partly why loving-kindness meditation can sound almost comically simple to someone who's never been near what it's pointing at, and so powerful to someone who has. Saying "may you be happy" over and over isn't automatically love. It's one way of finding that love and growing it.

If you're curious about any of that, follow the curiosity instead of pretending to believe something. Read people from different traditions. Meet people whose lives make you wonder what they found, and look at the fruits. Does their path seem to make them more loving, honest and grounded, braver about suffering? Or mostly more sure they've got the right explanation?

Sometimes care isn't what's missing. The whole horizon of life feels too small, and a meal or a boundary or one kind sentence can help without touching the question underneath: "Why should I want this life at all?" Take that question seriously. What would have to be possible for being alive to feel worth discovering, not just enduring? Curiosity can be enough here, something like "People say they've felt a love I never have. I don't know if I buy their explanation, but what if it's possible for me?" What you've felt so far isn't the limit of what's possible.
