For many people, healing eventually includes some forgiveness, or at least getting out from under active resentment. You can't put it on a schedule, and you don't need it first to be safe or hold someone accountable. If you've been waiting to forgive before setting a boundary, you don't have to. Getting justice and recovering can come before it too. Active resentment can narrow what you see, though, and keep your little one organized around the injury. But pressure to forgive early gets you an enlightened-looking performance ("I'm at peace") while the wound goes underground.

What can help is seeing how a person's conditioning and history shaped the harmful thing they did. So did their pain, and how much they could handle. Given everything they'd become by then, it was the only move they had. Which can be hard to hear about someone who hurt you. Hurt people often pass on pain they never worked through. When understanding them stops being a "should" and you really see it, forgiveness may arise by itself. And the kind of rage where it's all their fault can soften into something more accurate.

Understanding them doesn't make what they did harmless, and in theory there were other options. It means punishment can't go back and create the awareness, support or ability they were short on. What's worth doing is repairing what can be repaired, and making a different response more available now. The harm is real, and they're still accountable. Boundaries and legal consequences can still be right, and so can moral judgment and distance, even permanent no-contact. You can understand them and hold them responsible. And yes, you can forgive someone you'll never speak to again. "I understand how you became this way" can sit right beside "I now understand why I shouldn't rely on you for this."

Forgiving yourself works the same way, and if that's hard, forgiving other people tends to be harder too. Own what you did, and only what was yours. Let the remorse register ("oh no, I really did that"). Repair what you can, by apologizing or making amends. Changing the behavior counts as repair too. Then you're allowed to stop holding yourself hostage, though you're still responsible. Some harms take years to forgive. Forgiving too soon is one trap, and living permanently inside resentment is the other. Don't push it. Over time, keep coming back to how the person became capable of it, until the understanding is real enough to change how you feel.
