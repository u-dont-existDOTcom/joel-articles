### Also Look Outward

You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.

One ugly conversation can be awful and still not establish emotional immaturity. The next time you talk, they may have had enough time to actually think about what you said. They can come back still disagreeing with you, including about a boundary you hate. Then some completely different issue comes up and halfway through you get that wait-a-minute feeling: weren't we talking about what happened to me? Somehow you're comforting them again and your own point has disappeared. [Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity pays attention to repeated relational behavior like that.

Then there are the fights where you really did hurt them. You apologize. They are still disappointed, and the apology keeps spreading until you're retracting something you still believe. What exactly are you apologizing for now? You can feel guilty through the whole conversation. Some of that guilt may belong to the harm you caused, but it still doesn't tell you how much of the rest of the conflict is yours. It doesn't tell you whether the other person is manipulating you either.

Maybe after all that they can repeat your point back accurately and still think your no is unfair. You look for the next explanation and there isn't one. The conversation ends with them still thinking you're wrong.
