If that got to you at all, good. That was half the idea. I used to listen to Suzanne Vega's The Queen and the Soldier every night because it reliably got the tears out.

The crying helped me a lot in terms of not going numb inside. It didn't help me develop insight, though. I could have kept crying forever. It was Buddhism and then inner child therapy which helped create a new perspective that was more healthy. But I'm really happy I did do the crying that was needed at the time.

Music can help with feeling love, not just sadness, although sadness is often the first step to open up. So if you sit down to try any of this and feel nothing, that's when I'd put a song on. Céline's right up there if you don't have one, and Rosey Grier's It's All Right to Cry is a little further down. My sister used to sing that one to me almost daily in such a silly way it was really sweet of her.

Some people, like another ex of mine, got so deep into crying that it became traumatizing. She actually had panic attacks from it. If you start getting scared of your own crying, turn the song off for tonight.

The Chicken-and-Egg Problem

Céline's song is called On ne change pas—"we don't change." I think that's both fortunately and unfortunately true for most of us. Fortunately in the sense that the sweet, innocent, curious, vivacious little one is still inside us, even if hiding. Unfortunate in the sense that we often don't truly develop the adult qualities that we might pretend to have, so we continue burdening that little one inside us with troubles they aren't made for handling. This shows up in how we may feel abandoned, helpless, impulsive, frightened, or desperate to be chosen.

And I realized that it doesn't make sense to ask the child to heal itself, which is really a disguised request for the child to abandon its own nature and turn into an adult. There was never anything wrong with the child's worth to begin with. Instead, we need to separately develop the qualities of a good inner parent for that rightly vulnerable child: the loving Nurturer, safe Protector, and wise Guide.

That’s one grown-up doing three jobs, not three more people moving in to help with the rent, and each job keeps the others in balance. Say a party gets to be too much for your little one. The Protector gets you both out of there, and on the way home the Nurturer tells them they did great, even though mostly they hid in the bathroom. And the Guide helps them think twice about the next weird party they might go to, avoiding another cycle of bathroom vacationing. 🙈

The Guide is the one people misunderstand the most. I left it out of my first version of this guide until my friend Key pointed it out, and without an inner Guide, it’s easy to go looking for one in a cult. Our lives can become small and like a never-ending therapy session when we focus only on caring for ourselves rather than giving ourselves a mission as well. That’s the Guide’s job. But on the other hand, some people over-rely on their discipline or their mission to avoid caring for themselves.

If the phrase “inner parent” calls up the wrong archetype for you, pick another one. Your future self might work, or the captain of a ship, so long as they’d do those three jobs.

But here’s the chicken-and-egg problem. What if you’re so far inside the child-state that you can’t become the adult? This is much easier when you can already reach one of those adult roles. But when you can't, it's like you sit there trying to be loving while your mind is becoming controlled again by the inherited critic voice, or you start going numb, throwing a fit, or panicking.

Sometimes somebody else may have to do one part of the adult role with you or for you first. You get to feel what that is like and watch how they do it. Maybe you copy one small thing you've seen them do, or imagine them there when they're not, and try one piece yourself while they're still helping with the rest. That's what I mean by borrowed adulthood. You may still need help with almost all of it for a while, but over time there are more and more of those little parts you can do yourself.

For now, I think of the whole path in five fairly predictable stages. You might be starting at any point here (and it may change even from hour to hour as well):

1. You're still fully identified as the child — use your childlike superpowers to come into the "Wow of Now" (have you seen the movie Waking Life?), and make sure there's enough safety around you to continue:
2. Begin to borrow adulthood — remember watching Mr. Rogers or Sesame Street? Those adult role models are what we're looking for at this stage. This can almost feel like playing house as a kid where you imagined how you'd take care of a baby doll etc. 
3. Practice adulting — do at least one part of caring for yourself while support is still there from the previous step.
4. Adult mode itself — after sufficient practice with reparenting, you become a natural at sending love to little you, protecting them, and directing them. 
5. Adult and child in relationship — the adult leads. The child isn't pushed away, but doesn't have to run the whole life alone. This can actually feel really weird if you've been stuck in adult mode for a while. Our goal is to go from weird to silly and playful. Lose self-consciousness while gaining self-awareness, if that makes sense!

Whichever stage you're at, saying the right thing when you sit down with your little one helps, but you also have to start actually doing some of it in the rest of your life. Your little one starts believing the grown-up you when they catch you lying down because you're wiped out, instead of pushing through one more chore.

Reparenting isn't an exercise you finish, after all. Feeling safe inside while you tell your boss no, or say what you really need, is the reparenting itself, whether or not a meditation ever made you cry. The sessions are practice for that.

My Journey

I used to preach Buddhist enlightenment a lot. But an old childhood wound could still hit and suddenly I was the same little kid again, even when the spiritual part of me felt completely free.

The way I picture it is almost stupidly simple. Imagine you're broke and starving, and your baby is starving too. Understandable: there is no food. Then a food bank opens nearby and suddenly you've got lots of nutritious munchies. But you're eating all of it yourself while baby you is still starving. Unforgivable.

Nobody leaves a baby starving on purpose. I just didn't believe there was a baby. Inner-child stuff sounded silly to me until I could actually feel him. Then there was this little me actually hurting, and I could send some of the love I'd already found his way.

I think most people who chase spiritual freedom are also trying to get away from something painful in themselves, and a lot of it goes back to childhood. That's spiritual bypassing. Plenty of seekers and gurus do it too; I've written about the Dalai Lama as one example. I did it too. It doesn't mean you're stupid or crazy.

Spending your whole life processing your wounded inner child isn't freedom either. Spirituality opened a much bigger horizon for me—love and happiness beyond the conditions of my life story. Maybe ordinary life already gives you enough love and meaning. Great. It didn't for me. Conditional love felt radically insufficient, so I kept looking for something deeper.

I was well-loved as a child, but from age twelve to twenty-six I was depressed and cried every night from feeling the pain of the world. Loving-kindness (metta) meditation healed that depression almost instantly for me.

I wasn't lacking love. My parents had given me a lot of it. I found more in Buddhist teachings—which, it turns out, also include inner-child self-love! and later in loveyhuasca. Metta gave all that love somewhere to land.

But it didn't reach everything. A lot of the child stuff around my attachment to women was still there. When I later tried self-directed inner-child therapy, it felt like I was making it up at first. I used San Pedro cactus and CBD tea with sublingual mapacho, and that cracked it open; structured sessions with an inner-child therapist deepened it.

I found the therapist, Daja, years later, randomly, and gave it a shot, although I didn't think I needed it. But she did actually help me see some things more clearly. For example, she had me write down how many people I could fully trust as a child (lots), then how many people I could fully trust as an adult (zero). That was difficult, and it's actually the opposite of what you might expect from inner-child therapy, since usually that's about people who had problems trusting as a child.

What it showed me is that I was ignoring a lot of things that were really important to look at, and that was one of them. I wouldn't have even thought to ask the question she asked me if I were doing it on my own. It's also an example of how therapy can sometimes make you feel worse than before, when you become more aware of problems. But personally I prefer to see things as they are, so I can figure out how to work with them, rather than just hide in delusions.

It also showed me that I really do need to count more on myself than I assumed. I need to be much better as an inner adult for my inner child, because my childhood was actually so nice that I kind of just assume I can trust everyone, and that gets me into trouble. So it got me curious to learn more about inner-child therapy in general and map it out completely, which is a process I'm still in the midst of as I test this guide with people.

Don’t Give the Inner Adult Away

If you still feel like a kid inside, somebody who seems grown-up can feel like salvation, and letting them do some of the grown-up jobs for you can actually help for a while. But they can know things you don't and still be wrong about you. The test is whether the relationship makes me more grounded, truthful, and free.

How do you check that? I'd keep whatever you feel around them, and look at their track record. Do they respect a no, or keep pushing on it, and what happens when you disagree? If something outside of them backs up what they're telling you, and it all adds up, that's a good sign.

Even Pema Chödrön, who inspired parts of this guide, comes from a tradition where devotion to a teacher is a big deal, and she still says a teacher should “wean us from dependency altogether” and help us grow up.

Say you grew up learning that what you wanted didn't matter. Now you're only just finding out what you actually think, maybe even learning to say no. Then somebody tells you there is no self. That might be freeing at another point in your life; right now it can sound a lot like the message you already know: what you want doesn't matter. Or you tell a therapist, “No, that's not right for me,” and they call it resistance. Maybe they're right. But maybe they're wrong about you, or taking you somewhere you don't want to go. You still have to be able to say so.

Some gurus really do demand surrender, and they're the opposite of the Guide: they point you at themselves (so, more or less, they'd like to be your God). And sometimes they never have to ask. Someone seems to get your little one like nobody ever has, or treats it like it's special, and it starts thinking, "They know me better than I know myself."

Some manipulative people are too smart to act perfect. They lead with their wounds, or with how brutally honest they are about their flaws (very disarming, which is sort of the point), and every warning you hear about them just becomes more proof of how deep they are. Your Protector has to catch the moment compassion starts explaining away what you've already seen. "I have honesty issues and I'm working on them" may be a perfectly honest thing to say. It's also telling you there's an honesty problem right now, and knowing about a problem isn't fixing it.

You can't spot love bombing by how much affection there is, so watch what it's doing to your head (are the red flags from last week starting to look kind of cute?). Praise and special attention (from one person, or from a whole group) can make the evidence vanish, and they can turn someone into the exception to rules you'd use with anybody else. Arguing with the feeling won't get you anywhere, so slow down instead, and check what's true before you decide anything.

Humor can sneak things past you too. "Ha, I'll just borrow your card, kidding... unless?" is a joke and a request at the same time. I'm not saying ban the jokes (where's the fun in that). Just take anything in there with real consequences and treat it like it was said plainly.

Borrow adulthood. Do not surrender it.

Before You Try to Go Deep

How much of the grown-up you is actually here right now? Try looking around the room, pausing before you act, making a choice and following through on it. If you can't do that because you're basically the frightened kid, leave the deeper conversation until later. You may need somebody steady there with you, or to do whatever helps you come back a bit before you try to talk to the child.

And if the grown-up you is there, and it's just how deep you're going that's too much, I'd go shallower. Like the flight attendants say, it's good to know where the exits are. 😅 So can you stop in the middle and come back to the room? If you can't, or going in at all keeps making it worse, I'd come all the way back out first. Some people try one technique after another instead, to prove to themselves they can take it. Your little one may have had to just take it back then, though. Now there's a grown-up around who can say, "Okay, that's enough for today," and try something gentler instead of giving up on them. Even just saying hi to them with your eyes open, before any imagery, counts.

A full break from your little one is a last resort. Sometimes it isn't up to you, though, like when something's actually unsafe. If you do need one, I'd jot down why, and how far you'd gotten before it got to be too much. A couple of lines on your phone is plenty. After a while away, it gets hard to remember. So once that reason has changed and you want to come back, the note tells you where it was still okay. I'd start there, or somewhere a bit gentler.

If being alone was a big part of what hurt in the first place, then reaching toward people is part of taking care of your little one too. Do you have to get yourself sorted out first, so you'll be better company? I really don't think so. If the hurt was being alone, waiting just means more of being alone. So I'd call somebody or show up somewhere, mess and all.

Your Body Might Need Some Love First

If you notice tension in your solar plexus or abdomen, or anywhere else that seems to be keeping you from relaxing into that heart chakra love, try some non-forced, slower exhales and a little gentle self-massage. I love using one foot to massage the other foot like a yin-yang symbol, but you can start with some nice circular or spiral massage right on the tense spot.

If that's not enough, there's deeper somatic stuff like Shaking, tapping (EFT), EMDR, Brainspotting, Somatic Experiencing, Water Therapy, Massage, Energy Healing, or some other bottom-up approach.

When your nervous system is flooded, your protective parts are busy with the flood, so they're not going to let your little one come out and talk much anyway. Whatever you try from that map, I wouldn't go just by how pleasant a session felt to tell whether it was too much. This kind of work can bring up grief and fear, and sometimes hard dreams, or stuff you'd rather never have met. And being present through all of it doesn't mean it wasn't too much. It's a bit like the gym, where sore and injured can feel pretty similar that evening, and it's the next few days that tell you which one it was.

So how were the next few days? How did you sleep, and could you get through your day okay? Do you want to go back toward what came up, or is some part of you saying no? (And was it hard but doable, or just too much? A lot of the time you just know.) If you honestly can't tell yet, I wouldn't rush to call it healing or harm. I'd just make the next one a bit easier (shorter, or shallower, at a better time of day, with your eyes open, a teddy bear standing in for your little one, or a friend in the next room), one change at a time, so you learn what made the difference.

Bring the challenge back gradually, and only when you want to and you're recovering well. Some people try to toughen themselves up in a cauldron, but what you're looking for is the edge where you can actually learn. And what about those hard dreams, or images that pop up during a session? They can show what feels important or frightening right now. Sometimes they show what feels missing, or what feels needed. They aren't evidence that what you saw in them happened, though, and shouldn't be used to confirm a memory or rebuild one. Or to suggest one.

You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue

If you don't hear a little kid talking in your head, don't invent one. Actually, this is one place where the usual inner-child prompt to “ask your little one what they need” can get confusing, because “ask” sounds like you're supposed to ask a question and then wait for a second voice to answer. Maybe that's what happens for some people, but you might not be some people (hehe).

You might barely have spontaneous words in your mind and yet still be able to think a sentence on purpose. Try it and see. If words are there, speak or write them. You might feel more comfortable that way than just staying in imagination land. And if that starts feeling like homework you're doing for a child who isn't answering, drop it. Maybe what's actually requiring your attention now is a knot in your stomach. Or perhaps you're in a place you want to leave. Sometimes nothing “answers” and you just know what would be nice to do for yourself.

And it might not be the same order all the time. You could have a feeling before thoughts, or thoughts might come first and provoke a feeling. They say hindsight is 20/20, but you actually might not even know which happened first. That's ok, too.

When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child

I've been writing this whole guide as if everybody who tries it wants to take care of the inner child. Some people get a little rush out of making somebody scared or small, though. For such folks, inner child therapy is clearly not the place to start. They need to first deconstruct the adult identity they've got.

When I talk with folks like that, I mostly want to know what the identity gets them and what it costs, and especially what it's really serving. Some of them even call themselves sovereign, and then somehow there's an urge they have to obey, which sounds more like slavery to me.

This part tends to scare the wrong people, like somebody who's horrified by their own thoughts. Everybody gets scary thoughts they'd rather not have sometimes, and they don't make you a bad person, but why would you bring your inner child into the middle of them? The nice thing about inner child therapy is that you can wait until you're calm and actually ready. A real parent doesn't get to do that, which is one reason nobody manages to be a perfect one.

And if somebody actually wonders whether they're malicious, they'd do better to meditate on it and find out why they're asking before they bring their inner child anywhere near it. Are they really feeling malicious, or is it some voice in their head saying so, like the inherited critic? If some of it turns out to be malice they actually want to act on, the inner child can wait.

In one of the Buddha's own accounts of how he woke up, ill will shows up in his mind and he sees it's hurting him. If he had to figure that out by watching, it's not obvious. Ill will can make you feel powerful. Being on fire would too, and people would definitely get out of your way, but unless you're the Human Torch from the Fantastic Four, you'd be the one burning. So it's worth noticing what it's doing to you. Has it ever left anybody at ease, with nothing to regret afterward?

The Buddha also told his son that loving-kindness is what gets rid of ill will, and compassion does the same for cruelty. I'm biased on loving-kindness, since it got me out of my depression. For these folks it starts with the grown-up they are now, though, and their little one stays out of it.

As for when their little one gets a turn, I'd say once they've had some bad days where scaring somebody would've felt good, and they didn't do it. Until then it helps a lot to have a real person in their life who likes them and won't laugh along, since loving-kindness is easier to pick up from someone who already has some.

Borrow the Adult Before You Can Be the Adult

Noticing Counts

"Huh, somebody in here is really scared." That might be the most adult thing you've got right now, and it's plenty to start with. Whoever's noticing isn't all the way inside the scared one.

It can help to use your own name. For me, instead of "I'm terrified," that'd be "Little Joel is terrified. What does Little Joel need right now?" You'll sound a bit like Elmo :) In lab studies, though, people who talked to themselves this way reacted less to upsetting pictures and memories, and their brains didn't seem to be working any harder to do it.

If you can tell your parts apart pretty easily, you've probably got plenty of noticing already. Don't keep drilling it just because the love isn't coming yet. A lot of meditators are great at watching. I was, and baby me was still starving.

Borrow One Competency at a Time

A complete ideal parent may be impossible to imagine (unless you saw The Brady Bunch). And imagination is pretty hard for adults anyway since it kinda gets stamped out of kids at an early age, but it's not just a foolish waste of time. It's actually a genius mode unlock. So since the adult is actually a baby when it comes to power to imagine, start small: Borrow one function at a time from a figure who embodies it. Perhaps a therapist or your grandmother really cared for you. Perhaps your teacher at some age was a great guide. Maybe an older sibling protected you well. And there's always your future healed self if you can imagine that.

If you weren't loved well as a child, "love your inner child" can sound like nonsense. Love them how? Maybe you really want the best for your little one. Maybe you hug them in your imagination, or miss them. And you can still feel none of the warmth people mean when they say love. That doesn't close the door, though, it just means you start smaller.

Maybe just imagining a caring grown-up makes you feel more threatened, or lonelier. Lonelier is a strange one, with somebody right there. Then go smaller still, to the least cruel response a decent adult could offer your little one. It's scaffolding, and it isn't a test of whether you were loved enough.

Taking the hostility out makes room for warmth. What people most often say helped is letting their defenses down. I mean by choice, after the part guarding you has been heard. If you've never been safe being loved, your ego can act like a rigid security guard that won't let the feeling in, and being vulnerable is the opening the feeling needs. (This guard is only about letting the feeling in. People still have to earn your trust.) Sometimes the warmth is there, with a cold part standing in front of it, guarding something. That part tends to step aside once it's been heard (see the section Hear the Protective Part First).

If the love still isn't coming, you can at least stop judging your little one. Obviously no insults. Helpful advice is great, we all need advice. Let them absorb it at their pace. And don't expect a thank-you for the session, like they booked it. That's enough to start being the adult, even if it isn't enough yet for them to trust you.

Most of us wouldn't open up to somebody who'd only just stopped being mean, or let them take care of us. That takes warmth, and if there isn't any yet, borrow some. Think of someone you remember fondly. How would they sit beside your little one? Just make sure it's someone who liked you the way you were.

For protecting, ask what a minimally competent adult would do here. Maybe something simple, like actually smelling that old food you're about to eat and then trashing it if it smells a little weird. If you're about to send an angry text, hit pause. If the room itself triggers your spidey sense, leave, or lock the door and get help. Even if this still feels like the child acting like the adult, for now, that's ok. "Fake it til you make it" isn't exactly it, but yeah. Afterward, you can tell your little one, "I did that for us. That was the Protector." If it sounds funny, laugh!

The Guide can be you too, just a calmer you. That's a lot easier to imagine than your future healed self. Think back to what (and how) you decided about something like this the last time you were calm. Or ask what the wise person you respect would have you practice today. A wise tradition (like "What would Jesus do?" or "What would Buddha do?") can give some direction, too, until you develop your own internal compass.

The thank-you you weren't waiting for might not come anyway. 😜 More like "Oh, so NOW you care?" Which, fair enough, you did take a while. Your first instinct might be "But I'm trying!", and then it's just two six-year-olds arguing. Some days, just hearing it out like a decent adult is the only thing you'll manage to borrow, and your little one might still roll their eyes at you. 🙄

Borrow Hindsight

I sometimes ask: "How would I respond to this once I understood it more clearly, in balance and with compassion?" It's a question borrowed from your future self. It can change how the whole thing looks. It can also just make you feel a bit more like that person, or less alone (sometimes all at once). Keep in mind it's only their best guess. No perfect Higher Self is handing down answers.

That future you also knows why you can't pull this off perfectly today. What would they do for you, if they were here? Maybe they'd be kind about it, or maybe they'd just make you some food. If you can't picture them at all, make the food yourself (a "no" or a pause counts as food here), and skip pretending to feel some way you don't.

You can borrow a wiser future version of whoever else is involved, too, as long as you don't assume they'll end up on your side, or sorry. What the current one keeps doing is still what you go by, however good the picture looks (the future one has never called you back).

Remember When You Already Did It

My father often suggested folks remember a similar-level (or even harder) challenge that they were able to handle. Maybe you survived a car crash and surgery a few years ago, but now you're having trouble with emotional eating. You can draw strength from the past. As my sister, a historian, likes to say, "The past didn't go anywhere." Your own challenge doesn't need to be as dramatic as a car crash, as long as it really happened. So who or what helped you get through it? Is one piece of that still within reach? If you catch yourself thinking "I survived a car crash, so why can't I stop eating when I'm upset?", that's the past turned into a stick to hit yourself with. Maybe you're more worn out now, or missing whatever helped back then. (No example? Try another doorway.)

Notice the Part That Came Here

Some people hear all this about love and answer, "No. I hate myself." If that's what you find, it deserves an honest hearing. Just listen to it, without arguing back. But if you opened this article, something in you wanted things to be different, whatever the rest of you was saying. The same goes if you came to therapy or asked someone for help. Or maybe you'd already decided you were hopeless, and then for some reason you tried again.

What was it? It might have been hope, or desperation. Maybe you were curious, or scared. Some people come here out of duty. Or it was more like, "I'm not going to keep suffering like this." And maybe it was love. You don't need to decide which. Ask that part what it hoped might happen when it brought you here, and follow that thread.

Borrow Love—or Borrow the Perspective of Care

Sometimes it's not that you can't love in general. Your dog can chew up your good shoes and be forgiven before you've found the other one. But maybe you direct that love to your little one and the attempt goes flat, or starts to feel weirdly unsafe. Just feel that love you can feel for others for a minute, in your body, before you try to redirect anything. Then as you relax into that and enjoy it more, imagine it flowing downhill, like an overflowing river of delicious chocolate. Now imagine how happy your inner child is at the bottom of the hill to play in that chocolate love that rolled down!

Watch how it lands, though. "I care about you" can be patient, or it can be "I care about you, now hurry up and feel better." Getting close can be too much, too, the way kids squirm away from an aunt they barely know who goes in for the big hug, and then climb all over the uncle who's just sitting there. And if nothing moves at all, don't push it. Knowing that love is in you somewhere counts for a lot, and you're borrowing something you already know how to do.

Or put somebody you care about into your exact life, your family, your bank account and all, and then step outside it and ask what you'd tell them. It's a cheap trick for becoming the outsider to your own mess. The quest for self-knowledge is a life's journey, but you don't have to wait for your life to finish to figure out your best next move. And the gap it gets around even has a name, Solomon's paradox, after the king everybody brought their disputes to, who then wrecked his own life.

Then take a small piece of whatever you'd tell them and hand it to yourself. If the advice was "get more sleep," then for now just try brushing your teeth before midnight instead of at two. That's how you learn as a kid, and it works now, too. The technical term for this is "scaffolding."

If the only time the Nurturer shows up is when something's wrong, your little one can start to feel like they're your job. Sometimes show up just to say "I'm glad you're here," and then enjoy them for a minute, the way you'd enjoy a little kid who wants to show you a rock they found.

A Smaller Doorway: Goodwill

Sometimes "I love you" is more than you can honestly say, especially to somebody who's treated you badly. Even the usual whittled-down loving-kindness prayer, "May you be happy," can feel like rewarding them. That's why I go with Buddha's overall wish (paraphrasing): "May you be loving, peaceful, and free," since if they were all of those, they wouldn't have done what they did, and then they actually would be lovable.

This goodwill isn't just for the target of it, though. You may find it bounces back and opens up a little warmth in your heart. And if "I love you" goes flat when you aim it at yourself or your little one, the same wish works there, too. 🫶🏿

Ask God for a Loan

The dog kind of love is real love. Some people also know a love that's a whole different size, like God's, or whatever opened up for them in prayer or on a long retreat, and I don't want to lump that in with the dog. If you've been there, don't shrink it down to a thought experiment about a friend.

If you haven’t been there yet (and even if you have), I have good news for you!

Your Loan is PRE-APPROVED!

One practical door anyone can use to slowly experience divine love is gratitude (even after the Turkey’s done & Thanksgiving is over!). Genuinely cultivated gratitude (not just empty/forced ritual) is one way to experience divine love for the first time, or to get it back when the feeling fades. I think it’s essential if we want to live in that love instead of occasionally visiting it.

This is a bit tricky to put into words, but let me try anyway… You may be suffering now, but you could be suffering more. That’s something to be grateful for (although please don’t fall into the common trap of being grateful that you have what others don’t have, as that’s more like selfishness). I’m not talking just about changing your “glass half empty” perception to a glass “half full perception.” I’m not pushing “toxic positivity.” Being thankful shouldn’t be pushed on people who just need to grieve for the moment. On the other hand, I’ve noticed that I can sometimes completely flip a bad psychedelic trip into a good one with gratitude alone.

And I’m certainly not preaching “prosperity philosophy,” even though it can seem to start at that. We still need to remember that if we can only be thankful for the good things we have, we can fall into the trap of thinking that what we have is happiness itself. I’m ultimately reaching for something much deeper.

If you have a moment right now to breathe, that’s something to be grateful for. I learned this many times the hard way. For example, when I got tuberculosis from a cave in Mexico at age 20, and coughed for six months straight, eventually literally unable to breathe, leading to a near death experience. And then in Senegal, I had a suspected bilharzia infection in 2021, and could only painfully pee blood, I promised to always be thankful if I could ever pee normally again. Miraculously, I then found some obscure ethnobotanical research (on Google Scholar, before AI) which led me to drink roselle tea. Within an hour, I was cured. I don’t always remember to say “thank you” when I pee now, but I try.

That may sound like a silly, quirky waste of time to some folks. But what I notice is that saying thank you for every little thing, whenever I remember to, is not about imposing some kind of “spiritual discipline.” It is directly reconnecting me to something pure and sacred. And even (or especially) when I am suffering, I try to remember to be grateful for that also, because if not for suffering, I would not even think about how much I have enjoyed not suffering as much, and how much it will help me to develop more compassion, wisdom, and gratitude in future. It connects me with the plight of the countless other beings suffering at that very moment, authentically bringing up a wellspring of compassion in my heart for all of us. And I see clearly that it is my inner child who is feeling this pain, and what an honor it is that I can be there for him.

Of course, that’s not to say you should not try to suffer less, nor does gratitude for difficulties justify staying with an abuser. The point of life, according to every spiritual tradition and religion, I think, is to suffer less, which first requires us to understand the cause of suffering, and what real suffering and real happiness are. If you, like Biggie Smalls, have come to understand that having money is not real happiness, that’s a big deal! That’s something to be grateful for. Because getting what you want is an endless loop, and it doesn’t lead to being content with less. When you can see that less is more, at a deeper level, that’s where the real gratitude flourishes.

Notice how, when you offer thanks (to the universe, to life, to God, whatever you may believe in), a space in your heart opens up. It might not feel immediately like you suddenly know the real meaning of Divine Love, but it’s a little spark of … something.... There’s a tiny bit of freedom, of peace, stillness, softness. And as that softness deepens each time, you start to understand what I mean by Divine Love. Many people have written books on this topic (like A. Helwa’s book, Secrets of Divine Love, for example), so I won’t belabor the point further here, but I hope you are able to experience this when you try it out for a while, and I’d really love to know how it works for you!

Once you start to feel that lovely softness of the heart, invite your little one in with you as your spiritual integration practice. They might go numb, or call BS on that. If so, they might just need to see if you're actually humble enough, and grateful enough for them, to listen to their rejection and be patient. If you instead retreat back into your cozy corner, it's the food bank feast all over again: You can have an enormous spiritual feast upstairs while the child is still starving downstairs.

When the Spiritual Relationship Hurts

If a spiritual teacher you trusted betrayed you, even sitting down to meditate can feel like it belongs to them now. Kids quit piano over one bad teacher, and this is way worse. But if you let an abuser control your appreciation of life, then you've given them an unearned win.

Being the grown-up doesn't mean controlling everything yourself, though. After a betrayal like that, part of you might want to. Spiritual support can help you act where it's yours to act, and let go of outcomes and other people's choices where it isn't. The Serenity Prayer is the example I'd give, if prayer still feels like yours.

Become the Adult Apprentice

Say a friend sits with you while you fall apart. Most of us feel better and call them again the next time, and they get a little better at it every time while we don't. So steal some of it. Usually what helped was how they just sat there without trying to fix you. Ten seconds of sitting with yourself like that is good enough for a start, so try it before you call next time.
