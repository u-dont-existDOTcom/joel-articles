Building Trust With Your Little One

Not Every Hero Wears A Cape

Set one boundary. "What's a 'boundary'?", I might be asking. That looks like saying no to something that's costing you too much (money, time, whatever it is). Handle that task you've been putting off, or the tax man, or your DUIs in Maryland, even if all you do today is open the envelope. Keep one small promise to your little one. Clean your room! Maybe your little one had grown-ups who said sweet things and then did nothing, and maybe you've done it too, so this is how they start to believe you.

You can protect your little one from yourself too, like by repairing after an internal attack. "What's an 'internal attack'?" Calling yourself an idiot for losing your keys again, for one. Your little one was listening, so you go back and apologize to them. If they're sarcastic about it, you hear it out instead of snapping back. They don't trust you yet, and you don't ask them to. They complain about something, and if they're right, you say so. All of that shows as clearly to them as the clean room did. It's evidence you're safe to be around and that you accept them. Love needs both, but without warmth they can still feel like they're only being put up with.

Plenty of people are still at the putting-up-with part. Even with little or no warmth for your little one yet, the relationship can start with the Protector's plain acts and the warmth can come after, which feels backwards, because you'd think you have to feel it first before you could do anything for them. But yeah, bonding starts somewhere. And this is not to say you've never done anything to care for yourself. If not, you'd be dead right now. But we're making additional acts of care intentional now as a base for love to grow.

Intentional, meaning you pick one of those acts and decide when you'll do it, and what the smallest first step is (like with the envelope). Some of them are only one step anyway. If it needs money or somebody's help, sort that out before the day, and if you'll have to tell someone something they won't like, say it out loud to yourself ahead of time. Then put yourself in their shoes to see how it sounds, and go back & forth like that a couple times until both sides feel good about it, or good enough, as long as what you needed to say is still in there.

After the day, look at what actually happened. If you did the Protector action(s), not feeling better right away is information to learn from. It doesn't mean failure. Healing is nonlinear, and sometimes standing up for yourself might make you feel worse at first. And you can ask yourself, "What is this practice supposed to help, and how would I know if it's helping?" Not doing it is harder to figure out. Fear is the first guess, but maybe you didn't know how or why. Being worn out can stop you too, and sometimes whoever said they'd help couldn't make it. Hey, we've all been there. 🤗

Love Doesn't Have to Wait for Trust ❤️

Sometimes the love is there for your little one and still feels unsafe to them, or safe for a minute and then not. Love and trust don't have to come as a pair, but they got tied together, as if one had to vouch for the other. So your love can sound like you're asking them to trust you. It doesn't need their trust, but it does need you to be honest with them, which isn't the same as perfect.

The love can go first, though, before their trust is done going over your track record (they've heard what you still call yourself when you mess up, sometimes out loud). Your little one says "I don't trust you." You answer, "I believe you. And I love you."

I was about five when my mom told me it was time for bed (or something equally outrageous to a five-year-old). I cried and screamed "I HATE YOU!" 😡 She answered, "But I love you!" 🥰

That completely threw me. Fights don't usually go like that. Anger is a way to get more power when you feel a lack of justice or agency. You puff yourself up so you seem frightening or dangerous, or you demand to be heard. My anger was there to get me heard, and her answer showed me I didn't need it for that. Whatever injustice I thought there was wasn't coming from a lack of love, either. So the anger seemed stupid. I saw for myself that it was only hurting me, without helping me see clearly or get what I wanted. Still, what I screamed was true right then, and what she said back was true too, so I could hate somebody who loved me and she'd still be my mom the whole time.

When it's your little one yelling at you, though, try not to turn "But I love you" into a line you say on cue, because then it can brush the anger off, and what it's really saying is "so stop being angry." I think you can mean it and still be doing that. Does the love stay afterward and take whatever's true in the anger seriously?

The love can stay, and your little one might still come back with "Big fuckity whoopty doo. I don't believe you." 🙄 You answer, "Okay. I believe that you don't believe me. I still love you. 🤗 You don't have to love me back. Tell me what feels unsafe."

Your little one might say it's you. You don't have to accept every conclusion they reach about you, but do listen for the concrete complaint without fighting the yelling and swearing around it. If it's something serious, answer it seriously. The complaint might only be one sentence, once the swearing runs out. Like the anger at bedtime, the swearing is there to get them heard, so once you're listening instead of fighting, they don't need it anymore.

You can see the same move in a dramatized scene from the movie A Beautiful Day in the Neighborhood. Lloyd, a reporter, tells Mr. Rogers, "I can't imagine it was easy… growing up with you as a father." He doesn't collapse under that or fight it, but he does answer what's worth answering in it. He tells Lloyd that until recently his oldest son never told people about his dad, and his youngest "genuinely tested" him. Eventually he and his youngest found their way, and now he's very proud of both his sons. Then he says, "But you are right, Lloyd. It couldn't have been easy on them. Thank you. Thank you for that perspective." Like the two truths at bedtime, a true complaint and real love don't cancel each other out, so you can take the true part without giving up your love or defending yourself.

Also notice what else in you answers your little one, besides what you choose to say. When they say "I don't believe you," something in you might fire back an accusation, and then they can learn your love is only there as long as they take it the right way. The resentment can be purely visceral: being doubted after you've tried feels unfair, but there's nothing unfair in them going over your track record. The part that resents not being believed might be the same one that said "I love you" in the first place, or a different one, or a bit of both. Both talk in your grown-up voice, so to your little one they're the same. Be careful not to take the accusation as proof your love really is conditional, when it could just be another part talking.

Your little one might not be the only one getting accused. When life has gone badly, part of you may try to get some control back by finding someone to blame, like "If you'd gotten it together at fifteen, you could have saved everybody." If it's somebody's fault, it didn't have to happen, and that feels like control. But figuring out which age of you is to blame doesn't give you what it would have taken back then. That part may start picking on a different age.

Whichever age it picks on, saving everybody was never your job. At fifteen you probably didn't even have your own money, or anywhere else to go. And actually, not to beat a dead horse here, but every age of you was stuck with whatever it had then, even the adult you were last year. Hindsight being 20/20 doesn't change that. You still did what you did, though, and if it hurt people or damaged a life, that may need repair.

So instead of asking which age of you deserves the blame, I'd ask these:

1. What was that age of you trying to get, or get away from?
2. What did it understand back then?
3. What options could it actually see? Could it stand to take any of them?
4. What happened because of the choice it made?
5. What was missing? Some ability, or help/protection from someone else?
6. What can you learn or repair now, and how would you act differently in such situations?

Whatever was missing back then, you can start giving your little one now.

Your little one has their own version of that last question: "Big fuckity whoopty doo. What are you gonna do for me?" They actually want to know, though I think the first answer may be that you keep loving them, even while they don't believe you. What you do for them takes days to add up, but the moment they doubt you, you can show them right away that the love stays and doesn't turn into an accusation. Then their trust can grow from the stuff back in "Not Every Hero Wears A Cape," like hearing out the sarcasm, or finally opening that envelope.

If your stomach tightens while your little one waits for an answer, slower exhales may help you stay with them. Feeling calmer doesn't mean their complaint is wrong, though, and it won't make them trust you on its own.

Make a Simple Vow

If your promises still mean something to your little one, maybe because you've kept a few small ones, a quiet vow can help too. As many real-life parents know, your heart really explodes in such an unexpected and wonderful way when you hold your newborn baby for the first time (happened to me!). And if you're not yet a parent, it can really help to imagine holding a baby in your arms to make the vow feel real. Imagine you just had a baby, how would you feel? Would you look at him/her with adoration and joy? Carry that into visualizing yourself with your own inner child:

"I know you're in there. I love you. You don't have to love me back or trust me yet. I can't promise that nothing painful will happen. Or that I'll always know what to do, or never react badly. But I can promise to tell you the truth and listen when you're angry. I'll make our life together safer where I can, and take the next step I can see to protect you. When I fail, I'll come back to make it right. You don't have to believe these words. Watch what I do."

Notice it only promises what you can keep, so your little one can actually watch you keep it.

Only say the "I love you" part if you can feel it. If you don't feel the love yet, say you care (with the goodwill prayer: "May you be loving, peaceful, and free"). That's a first step. Or even before that, just promise your little one you won't be cruel to them on purpose. Borrow the Nurturer to improve this.

Whichever you choose, your little one may still answer with "Big fuckity whoopty doo." That's allowed, and one beautiful session wouldn't convince me either. Pick one thing to do that's small enough to repeat. A big gesture you later drop would only prove them right to doubt you, but a small thing you keep doing can change their mind.

Whenever your little one shows up looking for their parent, even if they don't know that's what they are doing, you go back to being the one they can rely on, and you're kind to them while you do it. If they show up to have some innocent fun, don't "put them in their place" as the adult. But sometimes they show up as the one who doesn't want to go to bed. Now you're in my mom's place: you still go to bed, and staying kind about it shows them bedtime isn't a lack of love.

Remind Yourself

The vow can live somewhere you'll see it, too, like on a stuffed animal from when you were little, a photo of yourself as a child, or just a sticky note on the bathroom mirror. Whatever helps you feel connected to the Nurturer and Protector you've been practicing. The Teddy bear note may remind you to think, "When I see you, I remember to stop attacking myself and go to bed on time."

A real-life child of yours, or a calm pet, can also act as a companion that brings out the relationship with your own inner child. But those are also their own beings with their own needs as well. Let them come and go without making them responsible for the session.

When Healing Turns Into Checking

You can get hooked on healing too. The wound can be completely real while you're checking it for the hundredth time to see whether you've finally grieved it correctly.

Going back can still matter, even when nothing new turns up. But returning to the same wound can also become a search for certainty in an inherently uncertain existence. Spending all day asking, "Did I process it properly? Did I grieve it enough?" is clearly not what we're after here.

And the checking doesn't always stay in your head. A body scan can turn into one, or a confession. Even, going on a date, or checking your dating profile matches, can be a version of this if what you're really after is proof that you're good and lovable. It might calm things down for a while, and then the next little wobble matters even more.

If you're feeling guilty because you didn't put your seat belt on, just put it on. If you still want to investigate it later, fine. Perhaps now and then you feel bad about that time you refused to put it on, broke your collar bone in a crash, and then got blamed for not wearing it. That's natural, but doesn't mean there's something new to look at. Then the background checker goes, “But are you really listening?” There it is. Seat belt's on. 😇 Where were we?

Don't be afraid to ask your therapist/yourself "What is this practice supposed to help, and how would I know if it's helping?" This sovereign, gently curious stance should apply to everything in life. Feeling calmer or having deeper insights or explanations for the trauma response are some possible signs of initial progress, but if that's the endgame, it's just a cope. Real healing is ultimately measured by reduced need for the therapy. It's like real parenting: you might start out reading parenting books and thinking hard about what to say or do, but after a while you get into the groove and a lot of it becomes natural. Inner parenting can be like that too. You don't need to keep checking this guide or planning every conversation with your little one; the nurturing, protecting and guiding become more automatic. You might still turn to therapy when you need it, and life will keep offering chances to grow wiser and more compassionate. And if the therapy is actually working, it should improve your daily life, and especially the health of the inner child-inner adult relationship. Going at it harder isn't as useful as going at it with more wisdom and compassion. Take the parts that work, and innovate on the rest.

If these exercises start feeling like a ritual you have to complete before you're allowed to live, stop there.

Simple Practices

Catch the Hook Before the Story Takes Over

Pema Chödrön calls the tightening and urge you get before the story has finished forming "shenpa," and she says "hooked" is a more descriptive translation than the usual "attachment." You might notice the hook before you have any clue what part of you is doing the hooking. Or you might notice it embarrassingly late. The jaw and stomach are already tight, and/or half the text is written, and then suddenly it dawns on you: you're supposed to be observing yourself. 😀 Maybe forget the parts detective work for a minute. You can figure that out later.

When you catch it, look around and just take a moment to enjoy some free honesty: "Oh! I'm hooked again! That's awesome I could notice it!" If nobody is in danger, take a breath or three before you finish whatever you were doing. Sometimes human beings need to be being, not always doing. 🙃 Maybe then you'll find there's enough adult around to be nice to your little boo for a second. Or enough Protector to stop something you'd regret later.

Here's where I want to clearly depart from the new-age wizdumb that all your problems are self-created: Somebody may really have crossed a boundary. That's not just because they are your karmic partner mirroring what God wants to show you in yourself, even if that also applies sometimes. Your reaction is what you can control the most, though. If what they did hit something old in you, notice how that feels in your body. And notice how your feelings can also amplify or distort your perceptions of others. Just because your stomach tightened up doesn't mean their boundary crossing indicates “They're evil.” You can defer that judgement for when you're in your happy place.

Feelings about yourself work the same way. "I feel attracted" or "I feel ashamed" is real as a feeling, but it doesn't settle who you are, or who you love. Making too much out of something momentary is one side of the human psyche's pendulum. Give yourself time to see what patterns form more durably, and what feels authentically yours vs something planted or manipulated into being there from society, influencers, family, friends, religion, etc., etc. You may find an exceptional freedom in exactly that in-between place where you don't have to decide what your pronouns are, or whether you believe in this or that. If you stay in flux forever, though, it can seem like there's nothing solid about you. In that case, sometimes it helps to meditate or talk with a good unbiased therapist who can help you see the pattern.

The opposite extreme of our pendulum, throwing everything transient away, as some people do with "thought-noting" practice, also isn't healthy, except as a meditation exercise. Transient feelings and thoughts can be meaningful signals. For example, if you don't want to be touched, say no now and work out what it means later. Same if something might be dangerous right now, or might be a medical warning sign: step away or get checked first.

A Heart-to-Child Loop

With a little warmth to work with, rest one hand at the center of your chest (the heart chakra) and feel for love, and the other on the solar plexus, just below, and feel for your little one. The solar plexus is the most vulnerable part of the torso. One punch there hurts a lot, as I sadly learned while getting beaten up every day at kindergarten recess by a gang organized by my best friend. Can you believe it? So it makes sense to look for your little one there.

Picture the love traveling from your heart down to your little one. If some comes back up, let it, and send it on down again, so it goes around like a loop. That part's a gift from them, and they don't owe it to you, so if nothing comes back, just keep sending yours. No warmth today? Then rest your hands there and notice what you find, no faking required. Non-judgement is a place to start. It can blossom into love over time.

Journal With Each Hand

Lucia Capacchione, an art therapist, wrote a whole book on this one, The Power of Your Other Hand. As titles go, it's pretty literal. Your other hand, the clumsy one, writes like a first grader anyway, so it gets the scared or hurt feelings (nothing made up). The usual hand answers, as the grown-up. Some people who get nothing but silence from their little one are surprised by what the other hand comes up with. A protective part might grab the pen first, and then it gets that hand until it's had its say.

And what if all that writing leaves you more confused than when you started? Then just keep track for a while, and save the patterns for later, if they turn out to matter at all. A memory aid doesn't have to explain your life. It could be a dated note of what happened, or of what helped. A reminder or a next step works too. Or you can leave yourself a voice note.

Just Like Me

This one works best on a steadier day, when thinking about other people won't pull you out of your own shoes. Name the feeling plainly. Lonely, say. Then think of how many people are feeling that exact thing right now, and wanting it to stop, just like you. With that many people feeling the same thing, the theory that you're the defective one gets kind of hard to keep up. Some of them make it worse trying to get away from it. Just like you, sometimes. 😅 Wish all of you, you first, enough support to stay with it, so the pain stops with you.

If you've tried Pema Chödrön's tonglen, this is a gentler cousin, and normal breathing is fine. Whoever hurt you might come to mind, too. Forgiving them isn't part of it, and neither is picturing their childhood. Getting in touch is a separate decision, for a much calmer day, if it ever comes.

“And So That’s the Way It Happened!”

My ex, Xandria, taught me to append “And so that’s the way it happened!” to a difficult memory. Say it the way a grandpa would finish a story on the porch. ("Once upon a time…" helps if the start is where you get stuck.) Somehow it moves the whole thing out of the courtroom, where the case was still being argued, and into your life story. Pick a memory you can handle for the first few tries.

Does saying it make the memory fiction? Well, no, it's still what happened, as far as you remember it, even if some details turn out fuzzy. And nobody's off the hook (you included, if you were the one who did the harm): whatever repair is owed is still owed. If the phrase does the trick, leave it at that, no need to narrate the whole thing.

Do keep an ear on who's narrating, though. If the voice starts sneering, like it's making fun of the younger you, stop right there (that's the courtroom creeping back in). Same if you feel more ashamed, or kind of floaty and unreal, since more distance won't help with that. And when it goes well, it's a good time to check in with the younger you in that story. What do they need from you now?

One Intention in the Morning, One Review at Night

In the morning, begin the day intentionally, not just because the sun chose to come up once more, since we can't count on that 🫣. Here's one way you might pl/orkfully start off:

“Today I will neither be Peter Pan nor Captain Hook. I will notice a hook or 2 which lead to unconscious behaviors.”

Then at night, pay yourself to be your own therapist so as not to break the bank. Equipoisedly look into how the practice went for you today:

"Where did I notice the hook?" At dinner with the in-laws? Doom scrolling TikTok nonsense? Watching animé?

"Where did it still seem to control me despite noticing it?" (That's the most frustrating part, when you know better, and you still act the same.)

Then get practical so it doesn't become another OCD loop: "What small repair or protective act shall I bring in for a proper response to that?"

And don't forget to appreciate the good! Moments of warm, beneficial connection (whether internally from child to adult, socially, spiritually, or undefined) are all to be celebrated. Were you maybe firm like an elephant today, or courageous like a lion? Adopt the entire spirit animal zoo if need be! Turtles are good if you need impulse control.

Count recognition as progress, regardless whether it came up a bit late, as the goal is to bring the awareness closer and closer to the present. All or nothing from the get go would be cool, but we normally have to learn step by step. If/when you interrupted the pattern, pat yourself on the back, or even kiss yourself on the shoulder. If you made a mistake, you can see how to repair that. You can renew your intention for tomorrow, getting better & better, every day, in every way. And enjoy your well-earned rest tonight.

Use Childhood Photographs Gently

Maybe you've tried picturing your little one and thought, "This feels fake. I'm just making it up." A real photo of you as a child can help with that, since there's no face or posture to invent. Yes, yes, it might feel a bit strange, seeing your own face from back then. But look at it gently and just notice what comes up.

While you look, notice what your body does and what feelings or urges show up. Is there something that younger you seems to want you to understand? Some of it you'll remember yourself, and some of it you might even glean from talking to family/friends from then ("You hated that coat"). It might just be an inference you're guessing from the picture, or perhaps it triggers an image or a dream. Or just nothing comes up, which is also completely OK. Ask what your little one needs from you now, and do a bit of it.

We don't need to ask it leading questions, like "Who abused me & how?" or "What memory is my body hiding?" Grilling one photo until a story shows up is a good way to get a story, but stories aren't necessarily the truth, and even if they are accurate, the feelings beneath them are still more important. Memory is an amorphous thing, easily influenced by guided imagery and suggestions, so we gotta be careful about that. Look at this study, for example, where people were shown a doctored photo of a childhood hot-air balloon ride they never took. Half of them actually started sorta remembering it. I'm sure you can relate.

A photo is just one moment that somebody picked and framed... not necessarily representative. Flipping thru an album may give different jolts in your body.

If you start to suspect someone of something serious, like abusing you, please don't confront or accuse anyone based only on what came up here. You might be looking at the photo thinking, "That would explain so much." Journal what came up and how it came up, and see if you can get support from someone level-headed who won't lead you toward an answer.

Put the photo back in its drawer when you're finished. If it starts tugging at you, so you keep going back to search it or you need to know for sure, or you're getting overwhelmed, take a break from it right then. Settle yourself in the present to close out properly. Re-evaluation counseling always ends on this "Present Time" reminder. Can be as simple and silly as "Oh, Today is Monday, my favorite day of the week!"

Close the Session and Return to Life

That "Present Time" ending is worth stealing for every session, photos or no photos. Remember the checking from When Healing Turns Into Checking? Without an ending, the processing can slide right back into that. So when you're done, look around and say where you are. Feel your feet on the floor, or the chair under you, and unclench your jaw and hands, since they've probably been at it this whole time.

And what's next for you two? Maybe it's making food, or just going to bed. Whatever it is, tell your little one, "That's enough for today. We're going back to the life we're building." Nothing has to be resolved first, and whatever you're still feeling can come along. Going back to your life is part of the healing, too.

When the Adult Voice Feels Fake

A lot of people get stuck right here. You call up the Nurturer and try to talk to your little one, and you get silence or a numb feeling, and all of a sudden your phone or the laundry feels urgent. Or some voice says this is silly, and it's hard to tell which part of you is saying it.

My friend Scott said:

"Half the time when I try to connect to baby me, I feel fake and it seems crazy."

I used to be all personal trainer and teach persistence on this as the remedy. Keeping at it does help, since a little one who learned to expect being dropped won't trust you right away. But I don't think that explains the adult feeling fully. More importantly, I think it could even be like unintentional gaslighting to the little one to ask to connect with them when you don't believe they're even real. Like when my mom pretended to listen to me when I was young, but even less excusable (from the inner child's view) because she was not asking for my time when she did that. So, it's worth meditating first on why it feels fake.

Sometimes kindness feels scary more than fake. One study's title has a word for that: "compassion-threat." People in it with anxious or avoidant attachment reacted to compassion imagery like a threat, and a ten-minute exercise in feeling secure helped with the next try. Another found a first try at imagery like that "can be frightening for people who have a tendency to be self-critical." So feeling fake could mean you need a bridge, more than a bigger performance of love.

And picturing one ideal parent seems to help some people. There's a whole method built on it, called the Ideal Parent Figure method. In a small pilot, seventeen adults with complex PTSD from childhood trauma did five weeks of it and were still doing better eight months later. Seventeen people with nobody to compare them to won't prove the whole method, but it's a start.

For some people it only stops feeling fake in an altered state, like on MDMA or deep in meditation. All of a sudden your little one is right there! Oh my gosh! And the love or grief that sounded like therapy talk is just obvious. Old memories might come up too. They can feel totally real, but that alone doesn't prove they happened. I'd still hang on to what you felt, even once it's gone, and try to bring a little of it into your normal life. You've felt that your little one is real now, so talking to them afterward isn't just pretending.

The Parent You Inherited

I'd look at the parent "script" you inherited: how you learned to treat yourself from how people treated you growing up, long before you started building your Nurturer, Protector and Guide. It can be warm, but it's often more like a critic, or a warden with rules. The rules can even sound reasonable, like "You can come back to the table when you're done with your embarrassing crying fit." If yours was like that, your little one learned bit by bit to hide that part of their authentic self which wasn't allowed, so they could keep belonging. They might still be doing it.

If it's a critic, the parent you inherited also measures whatever you show the world against an impossible ideal, and then attacks you from both sides: you're not good enough, and anything that does look good is fake anyway. That second one means nothing you do could ever count, and it's one place feeling fake can come from. When a new grown-up voice shows up saying "Trust me," of course your little one doesn't answer.

But with the borrowed adulthood trick, the new grown-up can start outside your head, as someone who likes you the way you are, or as something you do to protect your little one, like letting the phone ring when you know how that call goes. You don't have to pretend to feel anything to let a phone ring. Your little one can watch you do it many times before trusting you, while the parent you inherited calls it fake.

Start With Whatever Showed Up

Your little one wants to play, too, which is why I call the deeper therapy pl/ork, play + work. A lot of the best discoveries come when you're both just messing around, kind of like how kids tell you what's really going on from the back seat, when nobody's looking right at them. Play not only paves the way for therapy, but is a part of how successful therapy should be measured.

A lot of what adults tend to lose, your little one still has, like the silliness and the irreverence, the rawness, the creativity, all that, and maybe a connection to the Great Mystery from before any path gave it a name. That's the spark of life they bring, and you get to draw on it and give back, so it's more communion than command. For one example of the spark, there's my post Treasuring the Inner Child's Gift.

You might go looking for your little one and get mad instead, or realize you've been staring at the rug. I count that as pl/ork too, even if it feels like it's in the way. Before you decide where it's coming from, ask it what it's trying to stop, or what it wants. Maybe it just has something to say. It could be your little one, mad that you took so long to come back, or a part of you that's trying to protect you. Maybe it's that parent you inherited, or something from earlier today, or a bit of each.

How you picture all that is up to you, as long as it helps, and the simpler the better. I think a fear that keeps turning up to tell you something is worth talking to as a part of you. If it sounds like your little one, though, hear it out the first time it shows up. Let a stray thought go as just thinking, and come back to the room you're in. A worry that just keeps asking to be reassured can go the same way, unless it's your little one asking.

I wouldn't spend long deciding what to call each part, though. Every part of you learns from how the others treat it, your little one included. A part of you that's trying to protect you might warn you about something, and when you hear it out, it doesn't have to get any louder.

You can tell that part something like, "I see you. I'm not going to force my way past you. What's going on?" Or however you'd actually say that. Then wait and listen patiently. If nothing comes, that's fine for now. No need to answer for that part just because you'd set this up as a healing session. Even without the answer, you can notice what sets the reaction off, and you can understand what the reaction is propelling you toward, and away from.

Or if you do get an answer, it could come in different forms, like as a voice or just a hunch. It's often more like a feeling. Maybe grief or anger. If so just listen and be present for it if that's what it wants. If it tells you to do something, I'd only do it if it's loving and leaves you free. It should make sense, too. Even then, anything big or hard to undo can wait until you're calm and you've checked it against what you actually know (if you're in danger, get safe first).

Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein came at intuition from two camps of research that were supposed to be at odds, and the paper they wrote together is called Conditions for Intuitive Expertise: A Failure to Disagree. They start from intuition being "sometimes marvelous and sometimes flawed." Yours too, probably. So which kind is a given hunch? How sure it feels won't tell you. Their answer, roughly, is to ask whether the situation is predictable at all, and whether you've had a real chance to learn it. A hunch can pick up on a pattern before your head can explain it, so hear it out, and then let your head have a look. Neither one should decide alone, though. Your head can get tired of not knowing and be glad to hand the whole thing over, to a hunch or to somebody who sounds sure.

Anxiety deserves the same kind of listening. A lot of the time it's a part of you that's trying to protect you, making a guess about what's coming: that you'll get overwhelmed, maybe, or that people will laugh at you. It can be right! It's probably been wrong before too, and you know some of those times. So when it's safe to find out, I'd hear it out and then go see. Maybe nobody laughs. (Or they do, and you survive it.) If it might be real danger, though, I'd get some distance first, and find out how right it was later. Same with something a doctor should look at.

Some of it I wouldn't test at all, even when it sounds like a prediction. If a part hesitates about being touched or about sex, that's enough to stop. And if it says no to going deeper in this work, or to an altered state, I'd stop there too. It might not be able to tell you why.

Two Common Protective Patterns

Two kinds of reaction really are trying to protect you, more often than not. The first one is on duty all the time, and it can sound like a skeptic: "You're just making this up." It may have spent years keeping you from being humiliated again, or left again. That's a long time to stay on guard. It might be tired by now, but it's experienced and good at what it does. It has probably watched you get excited about other healing things, then drop them.

The other one shows up when you get close to something painful, as a sudden pull to do anything but feel it (even a yawn can come with an "I'm so done with this" feeling). It might believe there's no grown-up around who could hold what's coming up. I'd want out too. That yawn might actually be the opposite of checking out, though. Re-evaluation Counseling, a kind of peer counseling, counts yawning as emotional discharge, a feeling on its way out. The skeptic and the pull both started out trying to prevent something that really hurt, and they can keep at it even when what they do now hurts you. If you treat either one like an enemy, or like proof that something's wrong with you, it'll probably just think "See? I knew it," and stay in charge. I wonder what it would do if it didn't have to.

Hear the Protective Part First

Whichever protective part showed up, try talking to it in the same Nurturer voice you meant for your little one. A harder voice can seem more fitting for something that's been in your way. I'd still keep it the same. If that voice isn't there in you yet, borrow it from someone who's been safe for you. At some point you can ask that part, "What are you afraid would happen if you stepped back?" It might say this will just be one more performance, and you'll fool yourself again.

That one can sting, because there could be something to it. Maybe you've fooled yourself before, and that part remembers. "Tell me what you remember, I'm listening." works well here. It puts you into the role of the Nurturer, which alleviates the instinct to argue back. Usually your little one only comes forward after that part feels heard. Peek-a-boo! If you push past it, you might get exactly the performance it was afraid of. Even if you didn't win an Emmy last year, that can look convincing, with the right words and maybe even tears, while nothing underneath feels any safer.

A new roommate doesn't win you over with a speech on move-in day (hopefully, right?). The rent just has to keep showing up. Your Protector, the grown-up you, earns that protective part's trust about the same way, mostly out in ordinary life. The things you do can be small, like keeping a promise to yourself nobody else knew about. Or saying no to someone, and not texting an hour later to take it back. That second one can be really hard.

That part doesn't have to move out. It's been living there way longer than the grown-up you. It can keep watch while you take one small step toward your little one, and I think watching is what it wants anyway, to find out if there's a grown-up here now who could call it off if things got to be too much. If it says no, I'd listen. When it lets you, keep an eye on that part too, for any change at all. If it's been calling all this stupid, it might turn out to be scared of getting its hopes up. I wouldn't want to get mine up either. I'd tell it something like, "You don't have to hope yet, I'll keep coming back even when I mess up." Sometimes it doesn't say anything back for a while.

When to Change the Strategy

Weeks in and nothing much is changing? I'd want to quit too, or else keep doing the exact same thing forever so I never had to find out. Before either one, I'd look for what exactly is in the way. Sometimes those weeks were mostly reading about it and getting ready, which can feel a lot like doing it (this article included). Or you did sit down with your little one, and fear got there first, or a part of you that's trying to protect you. It can be as plain as never having the place to yourself. Whatever it turns out to be, I'd work on that one thing instead of giving up on everything.

Maybe you've been trying to comfort your little one for weeks, and the words are all there, but the loving grown-up behind them isn't, not yet. Some people at that point might try saying sweeter and sweeter things, and then upon that not working, perhaps just "throw the baby out with the bathwater," and decide reparenting is useless. I'd borrow that grown-up first. Then try again, and see if your little one can tell the difference.

One more thing about telling whether it helped. A few hours of feeling lighter, with the old feeling back the same day, is a few hours of relief. I'd take that! But I wouldn't decide from it that the problem's over, or even that the exercise did it, especially if you also slept well for once. Write down what changed and how long it lasted if you want, and then leave it alone.

How many tries is enough? I really don't know, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who promises you a number, including you, since some things shift in a day and some take about as long as learning guitar. If it's making things worse, or you just can't do it right now, I'd change course sooner (stopping counts). Not wanting to do it anymore is a reason too, but if that "nope" sounds like a part of you that's trying to protect you, I'd hear it out first. If it still says no, that's your answer. And if you keep following the reparenting steps correctly without getting the expected results, consider if you have a unique case these steps don't match yet. Maybe you'll find something that works better for you, or you can ask me what I'd do for this case, or ask a therapist you work with. Either way, I'd like to hear about it!

Sometimes the session itself goes really well. You feel real care for your little one and say exactly what they needed to hear. And a few days later you're skipping lunch and saying yes to everything again. A lot of people's first guess is that they didn't go deep enough. I don't think that's it, though, and I don't think this one failed either. Maybe the grown-up just hasn't come out of the exercise with you yet. Whatever you did for your little one in there, I'd try a tiny bit of it out here, like texting a friend that you're having a rough day instead of saying you're fine. That can honestly be harder than the meditation was. One small thing like that can teach your little one more than ten more good meditations. It doesn't have to happen after every session.

When the Urge to Escape Arrives

Sometimes the urge to get away from what’s coming up emotionally gets a lot bigger. All of a sudden you need a drink, or that ex doesn’t seem so bad after all. Some people start a whole new business. If that’s you, it might mean you got close to something that really hurts, and that can be progress, honestly! I’d thank whatever part of you came up with the escape plan, and slow down. If it’s more than you can hold right now, I’d get someone steady with you.

So why not just blast right past that part, with intensive meditation or psychedelics? Both can get you past the guards before there’s a grown-up big enough to hold whatever comes through, so I’d hold off until there’s enough Nurturer and Protector around. How much is enough depends partly on the medicine and what therapy is done with it.

Like MDMA, San Pedro is more protective and nurturing than many other medicines, especially at lower doses, so you don’t need as much Nurturer and Protector built up for those, though you still need some. Some people benefit more from solo therapy, while others benefit more from having a trained therapist guide the experience.
