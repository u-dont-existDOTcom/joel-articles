# Before You Try to Go Deep

Sometimes you are already too deep. If you feel so much like the scared kid that you can't pause long enough to choose what happens next, don't make yourself go looking for more. Get somebody steady around you, or do what helps you come back a little. The child can wait until there's enough adult you around to make a choice and actually follow it.

## You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue

If you don't have much inner monologue, don't try to fake one for this exercise. You might still be able to make a sentence on purpose even if words almost never show up by themselves. “Ask the child” can be literal if words come, but it doesn't have to be. Say it out loud, write something down, put on a song, notice the knot in your stomach or the urge to move away; maybe a picture or drawing gets closer. Sometimes the answer is just the caring thing you do next.

You don't have to replace missing words with a movie in your head, either. Sometimes quiet is just quiet; that isn't resistance by itself. Don't worry about whether the feeling or the words came first. It changes from moment to moment. If a feeling is still wordless, leave it that way until words help you understand what happened or choose what to do. Thinking later isn't cheating, and a feeling doesn't become final truth just because it got there before the explanation.

## Regulation May Come Before Dialogue

And sometimes there's no shortage of words; your body is just clenched up.

If your solar plexus, abdomen, or somewhere else feels clenched and it's hard to relax into that heart-chakra love, try a slower, non-forced exhale or some gentle massage. I love using one foot to massage the other like a Yin/Yang symbol. Circular or spiral massage over the tense area can be nice too.

Sometimes you need more bottom-up help than that: Shaking, tapping (Emotional Freedom Technique), EMDR, Brainspotting, Somatic Experiencing, Water Therapy, Massage, Energy Healing, or another approach that works from the body upward.

If talking still isn't working, don't talk harder. Sometimes body first, talking later; that's what the sequence in the roadmap below is for.

If imagery starts carrying you so far away that the actual room is getting fuzzy, open your eyes. Look at what's in front of you, remember where you are and what time it is, and feel your body here. If you come out of a session more disoriented, more compelled, or less able to function, make the next one smaller. Or stop and get some grounded support.

### Catch the Hook Before You Interpret It

You can often feel the hook before you know what it belongs to. Your jaw tightens, your belly contracts, heat comes up, or you just feel yourself pulling away. Maybe you only notice because you're already halfway through a text or reaching for a drink. Another time you've started scrolling, overexplaining, attacking, disappearing, or promising something just to make the feeling stop. And sometimes you jump all the way to what this means for the whole relationship. [Pema Chödrön](https://pemachodronfoundation.org/product/taking-the-leap-book/) uses the Tibetan word *shenpa* for that tightening and urge before the whole story has finished assembling itself.

At that point, don't solve the relationship. Look around and say, “I'm hooked.” If nobody needs protection right now, buy yourself one to three breaths. Feel the charge. Do the smallest adult thing you can actually do: give the child a little warmth, stop something that needs stopping, or choose one next step. If even that is too much, just don't act yet. Whether your story is right can wait. For ten minutes, focus on not making anything worse: leave the text unsent, put the drink down, let the thing you want to blurt out stay unsaid, and don't make the promise or the big relationship decision yet.

When the charge has dropped, look again. Somebody may really have crossed a line and also woken up something old; a younger state or protector can be there with a real grievance. Maybe what comes next is a boundary, grief, something to repair, or some care you never got. Start with what happened before deciding what it means. “My stomach tightened” is something you noticed. “That person is evil” is a verdict. What did they actually do? What supports that conclusion, what doesn't, and what else could fit? If literal words were in your head at the time, keep them as part of what happened. Words you find later can help too, but they're words you found later. One breath still counts, and if turning inward starts making you less oriented, less able to function, or more compelled, stop and get support from outside yourself.

### Also Look Outward

Don't use inner-child work to explain the other person out of the picture. Your old wound can be involved and there can still be a real problem with what they're doing. What happens when you disagree? Can they look at what they did, own their part when they need to, and make room for your side, or do the hard conversations end with you taking care of their feelings while your own position disappears? [Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity keeps the focus on what somebody repeatedly does instead of endless guessing about motives. Sometimes you've already explained enough.

One awful conversation is still one awful conversation. People can disagree with you, need time, or have a boundary of their own. Look at the pattern. You may never know whether they can't respond differently, won't, or just couldn't that day, but you can still know what response isn't available to rely on today. If their behavior changes, change your assessment. Another promise isn't the same as changed behavior, and you don't need another injury just to feel certain enough to protect yourself. Guilt doesn't decide it either: you may really have hurt somebody and owe a repair while you're also giving up your own position because you can't stand their disappointment.

Before the next conversation, decide what counts as enough. Maybe mutual understanding isn't available. Maybe the whole job is getting one thing across, saying no, or getting out without forty minutes of defending yourself. You don't have to make somebody understand before you act. Let the Witness notice what's happening inside while the Protector watches what is actually happening between you and the other person, then let the Guide choose the next move.

### Keep the Draft. Edit the Response. Care for the Writer.

Write the response you actually want to send. Don't send it yet.

Read it again as if the person who wrote it is somebody you're taking care of, not somebody you're trying to make behave. Fix what would make the message unfair or useless, but don't erase a real protest, request, or boundary just because it sounds angry. If the responsibility really is yours, keep that too. “Better” doesn't have to mean nicer. Maybe the message gets shorter. Maybe you wait or don't answer. If you say you'll come back tomorrow, come back tomorrow. If protection is needed now, act now.

Then stop editing and read the first draft again. What was this person trying so hard to get somebody to understand? Did they need protection, reassurance, or actual help? The angry draft isn't automatically childish. Sometimes the mature protest showed up there first.

### When More Processing Becomes the Hook

You can get hooked on healing too. The wound can be real and you can still be checking it for the hundredth time, trying to find out whether you've finally grieved it correctly. Take care of the safety issue or practical thing first. Then, before another round, ask what actually changed last time. Maybe you finally felt something you had only been thinking about, or saw something new enough to change a decision. If you're back at the same question because the last answer didn't feel certain enough, that's different. Grief can come back to the same ground without every return being a new request from the child.

If it's checking, let the thought stay unfinished and go back to the person, activity, or life you chose. You don't have to debate it, make it disappear, or wait until it feels solved. Before doing the exercise again, know what it's supposed to help and what would actually look different if it were working. You can leave calmer and full of insight while the original problem hasn't moved. If the method keeps giving you no useful contact—or mostly makes you monitor yourself more while daily life gets worse—don't answer that by doing it harder. Keep what helps and try another way of meeting the same need. Caring for the child doesn't require a meeting every time the mind says something, and if the exercise starts feeling like a ritual you have to finish before you're allowed to get on with your life, stop there.
