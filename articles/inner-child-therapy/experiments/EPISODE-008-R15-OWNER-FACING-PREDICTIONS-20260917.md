## Prediction A — repeated disappearance as the governing social fact

### Look at What Actually Keeps Happening

One ugly conversation doesn't tell you much. People get defensive. They need time. Sometimes they're just saying no to you, which they're allowed to do. But if you keep bringing somebody something that hurt and your part of the conversation somehow keeps evaporating, that's different. [Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity is useful here because eventually you have to stop being a detective and notice what keeps happening.

You may never know whether they can't meet you there, won't, or just aren't going to today. Fine. You still have to live with the version of them that's actually showing up. If that changes, great—you can change your mind too. Promises are lovely, but they aren't the change. And you don't have to get hurt one more time just to make the pattern scientifically respectable.

Guilt can make this harder to see. You may owe an apology without owing the rest of your position. Sometimes the conversation still ends without agreement. That doesn't mean you have to keep explaining forever.

And if there's still a whole speech bouncing around inside you, there's somewhere safer to put it first.

## Prediction B — reliability rather than diagnosis as the governing question

### Look at What Actually Keeps Happening

You don't actually have to decide whether somebody is emotionally immature. That's a pretty big verdict anyway. What matters sooner is much more practical: what can you count on from this person when something between you gets uncomfortable? One bad conversation doesn't answer that. Neither does disagreement, needing time, or somebody having a boundary. Repeated behavior starts answering it whether you like the answer or not. [Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) is helpful to me for exactly that reason.

Maybe you never work out whether they couldn't hear you, wouldn't hear you, or would have heard you next Thursday. You can leave that mystery unsolved. If the behavior changes, update your opinion. Until then, a promise is still just a promise, and protecting yourself doesn't require one more demonstration.

The annoying part is guilt. You can genuinely have something to repair and still notice yourself giving away the rest of your position because somebody is disappointed in you. Feeling guilty doesn't magically tell you which part belongs to you.

Sometimes you've said enough. The other person still thinks you're wrong. Life continues.

If the rest of the argument is still bouncing around in your head, write it somewhere private before you do anything with it.

## Prediction C — the ninth explanation as the governing movement

### Look at What Actually Keeps Happening

There is a point in an argument where you realize you're explaining yourself for the ninth time. Maybe this explanation is going to be the magical one. 🙂 Or maybe the useful information is that you keep ending up here.

That doesn't mean one messy conversation proves anything dramatic about the other person. People need time, they disagree, and sometimes the answer really is just no. But over time you learn whether your experience can stay in the room at all, or whether every difficult conversation somehow turns into you abandoning it. [Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity is helpful when you're tempted to spend forever solving the other person's motives instead of noticing the pattern.

You may owe them an apology somewhere in the middle of all this. Apologize for what is yours. Just don't assume their disappointment automatically owns everything else too. You may never know whether they can't meet you differently or simply won't. You can still stop the conversation before you know.

They might never agree with your version. The ninth explanation probably won't become sacred text.

And if there is still a whole speech bouncing around inside you, there is somewhere safer to put it first.

## Current recommendation

**Prediction C is the strongest architecture for the next pass**, not because its wording should be copied, but because its governing movement is genuinely different from R14's assessment sequence. It begins inside the social pressure to keep explaining, lets repeated behavior become the discovery, lets guilt interrupt that same exchange, and naturally lands in the private-draft practice.

Prediction A is the safest fidelity route and may be easier to preserve. Prediction B is the cleanest conceptual compression but has the highest risk of becoming another polished advice mini-essay.
