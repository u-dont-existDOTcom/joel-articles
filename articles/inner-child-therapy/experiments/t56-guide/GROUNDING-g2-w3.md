[4] FLAG MISFIRES: "It's had enough, so why put it through this?" — This is a NEW example of the trap's voice. It echoes something the article already taught readers to do ("Okay, that's enough for today," and try something gentler instead of giving up on them; "If it says no, I'd listen") and then calls it the masquerade. It gives no way to tell a grown-up who wants to skip the hard part from a little one who really is at its limit. So a reader whose little one has really had enough, or a reader already stuck in forced adulthood, may push past a real limit. — The reader should get that stopping for now and coming back gentler is still care. The masquerade is using "it's had enough" to drop every hard thing for good.
QUESTIONS:
Q: "You told me to say 'that's enough for today,' so when my little one has had enough, is stopping the trap?" — answered: earlier, in another setting ("Now there's a grown-up around who can say, 'Okay, that's enough for today,' and try something gentler instead of giving up on them"; and just above, "you ask hard things of it so it can grow, at its own pace"). But [4] puts nearly the same words inside the trap, so at this point the reader takes the earlier advice as overruled. Later in the guide it is covered only for readers without enough stability ("Direction requires enough stability for the child to be guided rather than spiritually overruled"), which doesn't help them tell the two apart in the moment. — MUST — Smallest answer: stopping for now and coming back gentler is the Guide working at its pace, and the trap is "had enough" used as the reason never to come back to anything hard. Size: a clause.
