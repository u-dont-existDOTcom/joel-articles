I think this is closer to what the Buddha taught, too. A king who'd had his own father killed once confessed it to him, and the Buddha said that seeing a fault as a fault, making it right and holding back from then on is how you grow in the practice. He never asked for a mind where nothing bad shows up. He said kamma is about what you intend, and he gave monks several ways to handle a nasty thought once it's there. So it's about seeing clearly and repairing things, and nobody's asking you to prove you've got a pure mind. Feeling bad about something you chose to do can sometimes mean something needs looking at. Feeling bad about a jingle for just showing up is another thing.
