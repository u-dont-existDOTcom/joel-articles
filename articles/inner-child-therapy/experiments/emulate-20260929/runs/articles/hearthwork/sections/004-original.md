# What RC gave me—and what it left out

I spent years as a young adult inside [Re-evaluation Counseling](http://rc.org/), one of the older peer-counseling traditions. It has been operating since the 1950s. Switching between client and counselor, giving equal time, offering attention without interpretation, and allowing the body’s natural discharge processes were the most important psychological methods I had encountered. RC taught me to listen. It gave me somewhere to put the weight of my own life. It cost nothing and depended on no professional. I am genuinely grateful for it.

I’ve also come to think RC underdeveloped several things: somatic awareness and the body’s window of tolerance, parts work and [inner child reparenting](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/), and a clear account of what discharge is actually *for*. RC treats crying, trembling, and laughing as the main engine of healing. I’ve come to believe that discharge heals only when reparenting accompanies it. *Discharge without reparenting is the original wound repeating itself.*

When somebody remains identified with the inner child, the adult isn’t guiding a child through pain. The child is screaming alone again. When no adult function is accessible, the pair should lend steadiness before inviting a larger release: orient to the room, slow the pace, offer warmth and boundaries, and let the person choose one small step. The release can turn into existential despair, stories of being unworthy or unloved, and states the body can’t hold. A steady parent wouldn’t keep escalating a child into that place. The parent would allow the feeling, prevent the story from multiplying around it, and help the child return. Big releases without that frame can retraumatize. The same bodily process, held by an inner adult or a carefully borrowed adult function, can become integration. Hearthwork brings the parenting alongside the discharge so they reinforce one another.

RC also concentrated authority around its founder and his lineage in ways that caused real harm. That shadow is well documented. Some descendant communities, especially Co-Counseling International, split off precisely to remove that centralization while keeping the method.

RC is profoundly political. Systemic critique is already one of its main hallmarks. It understands distress as something installed by oppression, runs wide-world projects on racism, classism, and environmentalism, trains counselors as activists working for collective change, and has decades of constituency-based practice among women, men, Black liberation, working-class people, Jewish liberation, young people, and many others. Some material becomes speakable only among people who share the relevant suppression. Anyone who has encountered RC without feeling that political weight probably hasn’t gone very far inside it.

My question is more specific: *can modern society, even at its best, hold psychological life at all?* RC’s theory assumes yes: heal, organize, change the institutions we have, and build a better version of the same general kind of society. I’m making a different argument. Industrial modernity dissolved the places that used to carry psychological life: extended kin, religious community, mutual-aid societies, intergenerational households, and the slow web of being known for years. Activism inside the institutions that replaced the village can improve those institutions without necessarily rebuilding what disappeared.

RC’s path may also be right. The two may be complementary. Organizing can continue while people deliberately rebuild small groups capable of holding a life. The village can’t be won through activism alone, because the activism is happening inside the institutions that replaced it.
