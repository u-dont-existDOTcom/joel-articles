# On possession, oppression, and the inner adult

[image 4](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!WZVg!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7a7c23e2-273a-4464-a5d8-4eacd08c00c0_1122x1402.png)

Westerners usually ask me about access, training, and credentials. Africans and people from other traditional cultures ask different questions. Does reparenting address what their cultures call possession, witchcraft attack, ancestor business, or entities that find purchase in vulnerable people? Can it help under the weight of patriarchy, caste, religious orthodoxy, and clan obligation—forces that Western therapy often files under “social context” even when they dominate the whole life?

My answer is yes, through different mechanisms. Hearthwork takes seriously something mainstream Western therapy generally avoids: *evil exists as an active force in the world*. It can appear through malice and deliberate cruelty, or through systems built to dominate. You don’t need my metaphysics to recognize that. Most cultures have always understood that some forces in human life are trying to harm us. Healing has to face that reality rather than collapsing everything into the sentence, “Only your inner experience is real.”

## On possession and unbound entities

A few years ago, a friend I’ll call H. went through something Western therapy would probably call a severe dissociative or anxiety episode. I sat with her through it. From where I sat, it was a spiritual attack: an entity arriving through somebody in her circle who held strong malice toward both of us. H. had never experienced anything like it. Even with my prior experience, the first few seconds were terrifying.

Exorcism-style resistance often makes these moments worse. Hostility meets hostility and feeds the same field from both sides. What I knew, and what I slowly guided H. toward over several hours, was that *love is the actual remedy*. Love is the highest power. Entities flee from it or transform inside it, no matter how overwhelming they seemed a moment earlier.

The difficulty is that most people cannot reach genuine love in that state. They can say loving words, perform a technique, or imagine sending light. The entity is operating deeper than the performance. *To love powerfully outward, you have to first have developed deep love for your own inner child.* Reparenting builds the capacity to love in a form real enough to dissolve hostile energy.

H. already had some self-love practice, so my redirection wasn’t entirely foreign. It still took hours because the foundation wasn’t fully developed. Somebody with no reparenting practice might understand the instruction perfectly and have no access to the state it requires.

Traditional remedies such as exorcism, sacred recitation, and ritual cleansing can work in the moment and still fail to hold. They may address the entity while leaving the vulnerability untouched. *The door stays open.* An unparented inner child seeks attachment, including attachments to forces that do not care for it. Reparenting closes the door without denying that the entity was real. It gives the child a safer bond with its own inner adult and builds the kind of love hostile forces cannot tolerate.

## The discernment between shadow and entity

Some experiences that feel like attack are disowned shadow: your own rage, destructive impulse, or fear placed outside yourself. Some experiences that look like shadow remain clearly foreign. Often both are tangled together.

The useful question is: *what happens when I bring the inner adult and its love into relationship with this?* Material that softens and integrates belongs more to shadow. Material that keeps a foreign agenda and cannot bear the love is closer to entity territory. Practice improves the discernment. Beginners misclassify in both directions, which is one reason faster judgment is usually less useful than staying in relationship with the material longer.

## On crushing social norms—and why frontal assault often doesn’t work

Reparenting also addresses suffering created by patriarchy, caste, violent religious orthodoxy, rigid clan duty, and other unjust social arrangements. It doesn’t make those arrangements acceptable and does not replace political action. It restores the *capacity to act* that oppressive systems deliberately weaken.

I don’t believe oppressive systems come from nowhere or consist of pure arbitrary evil. Patriarchy exists partly because women in an unhealed state are often emotionally volatile, socially chaotic, and unconsciously wield sexual and mystical power in ways that frighten men and destabilize the wider community. None of that justifies patriarchy. It makes patriarchy a harmful answer to a real underlying tension. Caste, religious orthodoxy, authoritarian family systems, and elite distrust of democracy also respond to something real and respond badly.

This changes how we think about transformation. A frontal attack on the social arrangement can remove the outer restraint while leaving the original tension untouched. Legal equality does not automatically produce flourishing women. Women can remain ashamed of their own power and unstable in relation to it after the law changes. The external system was oppressive, but it was also containing something that now has to be held internally.

Reparenting builds that internal steadiness. As enough women develop stability, self-knowledge, and a mature relationship with their own power, the *felt need* for patriarchy can dissolve. The new pillars—internal stability, real self-knowledge, and a mature relationship to one’s own power—take the weight, and the old ones can go. I think the same logic applies more broadly. Elite distrust of real democracy isn’t entirely paranoid; it reflects an accurate perception that most people, in their current state, can’t govern themselves wisely. Reparented people can. Once enough people can carry that responsibility, real democracy becomes possible. The old arrangements can fall away or transform because they’re no longer needed.

Direct confrontation still matters under acute injustice. Long-term transformation reaches deeper through reparenting. Some battles even strengthen the system by confirming the tension that produced it while leaving the tension intact.

## A note on forgiveness

Healing eventually requires forgiveness of the people who hurt the inner child and forgiveness of oneself. Anger held indefinitely keeps the wound active and begins organizing the person around it. Forced forgiveness creates spiritual bypassing. Inquiry works better: *how did this harm-doer become someone who could do this? What was their inner child carrying? What wound was being passed through them when they hurt me?*

Understanding does not erase responsibility. Boundaries remain. Legal and relational consequences remain. The person who caused harm is still accountable. The inquiry allows you to see that hurt people hurt people and that, given the totality of conditioning, their action was what their nature was going to produce. When that understanding lands deeply enough, the frame that made forgiveness feel like a betrayal of the wounded self dissolves. Forgiveness arrives without being commanded.

People who cannot extend this understanding outward usually struggle to extend it inward. Self-forgiveness follows the same path, with four practical steps: responsibility, remorse, rectify, release. Name what you did and its impact. Let the violation of your own values register. Repair what can still be repaired and prevent repetition where possible. Then stop holding yourself hostage after the responsibility has been faced.

Premature forgiveness is one trap. Permanent resentment is the other. Stay with the inquiry and let the seeing develop. When it truly lands, the forgiveness has already happened.

## On the inner adult itself

The inner adult needs other people. It was raised by the same parents who failed the inner child, so a parent with only their own parenting history to learn from becomes a closed system. Solo reparenting often stalls for exactly this reason: the adult can give only what it already knows.

External input breaks the loop—elders, teachers, friends from differently parented families, lineages, and genuine peer counseling. Nervous systems also learn regulation through other nervous systems. A child develops self-regulation through repeated contact with attuned adults. People who did not receive enough of that in childhood may hit limits trying to rebuild all of it alone. Pair practice and community therefore belong inside the inner pl/ork rather than sitting on top as optional extras.

This is where borrowed adulthood belongs. The person receives a narrow adult function, observes it, participates in it, initiates part of it, and gradually internalizes it. The helper lends capacity without gaining authority over the meaning of the experience. Every useful borrowing ends with a handback: “What did you receive, and what can you carry yourself now?”

The Buddha used the term *kalyāṇa-mittatā*, admirable friendship, for something close to “the whole of the holy life.” He did not quite mean that literally. But close.

My friend [Key](https://keygoddess11.substack.com/), whose ongoing inner-child therapy practice is unusually public on her Substack—a rare degree of transparency about wounded-healer practice—helped me sharpen another point. The inner adult has three roles. The Nurturer receives pain with warmth. The Protector makes that warmth believable through boundaries, competence, and ordinary action. The Guru/Leader gives direction and connects the child with nature and universe rather than making family, religion, a group, or one relationship the only source of belonging. Without the Protector, soothing can remain words. Without the Guru/Leader, reparenting can become permissive.

The adult also isn’t supposed to dominate or exile the child. The healed child brings intrinsic worth, silliness, creativity, innocent love and trust, intuition, and a connection to the Great Mystery before any chosen path gives it a name. The healed adult draws from the child and gives back in a continuing loop of love and learning; the relationship is communion rather than command. I show that side more directly in [*Treasuring the Inner Child’s Gift*](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/treasuring-the-inner-childs-gift).

The Protector has to become visible outside the session. Set one boundary. Handle the avoided task or financial mess. Leave the relationship that keeps eroding safety. Keep one small promise. These acts accumulate into trust because internal soothing without external competence can sound like the same unreliability that wounded the child.

The child may not be the first part who answers. Silence, numbness, cynicism, anger, the urge to scroll, or a sudden need to fix something can be protective parts standing between the person and pain they consider dangerous. They belong inside the pl/ork. Pushing past them only confirms that the adult still doesn’t listen when part of the person says no. Start with whoever showed up: notice the guard, ask what it fears would happen if it stepped back, and wait. A Hearthwork session may need to stay with breath, shaking, silence, or felt sense before direct inner-child dialogue becomes useful.

One protective voice deserves its own name: the *inherited parent*—the internalized caregiver voice formed from how the child was treated. I use *Inner Adult* for the Nurturer, Protector, and Guru you deliberately build, so the distinction matters. For many people, its first form is a critic or warden: don’t cry, don’t need too much, don’t get angry, don’t embarrass us, and don’t become too independent.

The inherited parent can compare the outward persona with an impossible ideal, then attack from both sides: you aren’t good enough, and whatever looks good is fake anyway. No wonder the child doesn’t immediately trust a new adult voice saying, “I’m here now.”

The three roles translate outward too. A good peer can lend Nurturer through presence and warmth and Protector through confidentiality, time boundaries, and respect for “no.” The peer does not supply the client’s Guide or decide what the experience means. A carefully timed question can help the client’s own Guide become audible without interpretation, advice, or authority over the answer. Then the roles switch.

Some people face a parallel identity-building task. In controlling families, rigid religions, or relationships where belonging required performing somebody else, the authentic child-self may never have had enough room to develop. When childhood is organized around avoiding pain and preserving attachment, much of the person’s attention goes into reading danger and meeting expectations. There is less room for ordinary experiments that answer, “What do I like? What do I want to try? Who am I when nobody is grading me?”

Reparenting and identity-building can proceed together. Private journaling without imagining a reader, temporary intentional distance from overpowering social cues, recovering buried preferences, and noticing where belonging is being purchased with self-betrayal can help the child-self emerge while the Nurturer and Protector are already learning to care for it.

One version of the performed self is the *human doer*: worth has to be earned through beauty, intellect, status, spirituality, achievement, sexual desirability, or becoming indispensable to everybody else’s problems. These can all be real gifts. They become belonging bargains when failing in the arena feels like losing the self.

A practical antidote is experimental play. Try painting, dancing, gardening, a language, a sport, volunteering, music, or something else that has no obvious value to your résumé or social position. Let the child be bad at it. The adult’s job is to protect the beginner from the inherited parent’s instant verdict: “We are finding out whether we enjoy this, not proving our worth.”

The four ages of the inner child, attachment styles, the Window of Tolerance and polyvagal states, memory reconsolidation, protective parts, the inherited parent, self-differentiation, the human-doer pattern, learning to feel love when warmth is not yet available, forgiveness and self-forgiveness, bilateral journaling, experimental play, and the relationship between reparenting and Internal Family Systems are covered across [my full inner child guide](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/inner-child-self-love-tips-and-guided) and the Hearthwork manuals. All of this places Hearthwork closer to liberation psychology, traditional healing, and some religious frameworks than to mainstream Western therapy. The work is internal repair *as preparation for living in a world where harm is real and ongoing*. The inner adult develops enough strength to face external forces—and, when the love is steady enough, sometimes dissolve them.
