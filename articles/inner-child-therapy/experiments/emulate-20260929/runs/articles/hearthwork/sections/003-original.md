# When somebody quietly turns you into their therapist

You’re at a dinner. A friend of a friend corners you in the hallway and starts telling you about their breakup, their boss, and the weight that fell on them last week. You listen. You nod. Twenty minutes pass. You’ve contributed a few small affirming sounds. You’re tired, you never agreed to this, and you can’t see a way out that doesn’t feel cruel. They clearly need somebody. You’re standing there. What kind of person walks away from someone in distress?

If this happens to you often, you have something people can feel. A steady nervous system attracts it. So do contemplative practice, social grace, helping-profession training, and the ability to stay present while another person falls apart. Women get a disproportionate share, especially eldest daughters and anybody read as warm and available. So do clergy, therapists outside working hours, recovering alcoholics with time in the program, and anyone whose presence signals a capacity to hold.

I’m going to call this *clienting*: somebody makes you their counselor without asking, usually without realizing it. Friendship is mutual. Therapy has an agreed frame, training, time limits, and fees that keep both people aware of what they’re doing. Clienting borrows the intimacy of both and gives the listener none of the protections. It drains people who are good at listening, slowly damages friendships, and often doesn’t help the person unloading very much either, because the care has no agreed time, role, or boundary.

Most of us respond in one of three ways. We people-please, stay available, get exhausted, and begin resenting the person. We become colder so fewer people come near us. Or we refuse once and feel guilty for the next three weeks. None of these responses gives either person what they actually wanted.

## Give the care an actual container

Now imagine that you and the person who keeps clienting on you agree to do this deliberately. You meet on a schedule, the roles are explicit, and each of you gets the same amount of time. Outside that hour, you’re friends again, and you can say no without wondering whether you’ve abandoned somebody in crisis. The care becomes mutual, and the listening usually gets better because both people know what the hour is for.

This is peer counseling. I’ve been calling my synthesis Hearthwork: the home fire, the slow gathering place, the warm center where people know one another across time. Hearth is older than therapy and older than the state. Most cultures have had some version of it. Hearthwork is my attempt to rebuild that capacity deliberately, using methods available now and without pretending we still live in the village that once supplied it automatically.

The basic premise is: Equal time. Attention without interpretation, advice, or fixing. You speak. I listen. Then we switch. Presence, Warmth, Discipline, Confidentiality. The pair meets regularly, often across years, and the client uses the time however serves them. What one person says while clienting doesn’t get used against them after the switch. It was heard in the client role; now the other person receives the same fresh attention.

*It is the most powerful psychological tool I have ever encountered, and almost no one has heard of it.*

You can use this material at three different depths. Most readers won’t take up the full methodology, which is fine. Start where your life actually is.

## Three ways into Hearthwork

*1. Literate friendship.* Learn to notice clienting and the asymmetry it creates. Learn the difference between circling the same complaint and processing something that may actually move. Instead of absorbing a crisis in a hallway, say, “I’d love to hold this with you. Can we make time tomorrow when I can be more present?” Suggest a therapist, group, healer, or another friend when something keeps repeating beyond what friendship can carry. You don’t need sessions, roles, or a manual. You become a more truthful friend and let your friends become more truthful with you. For many people, that is enough. Friendship can also model care that was missing. By itself, though, it usually lacks the deliberate repetition and handback that turn borrowed care into adult capacity.

*2. Reparenting as a personal practice.* The deepest psychological task many adults face is building a real relationship between the wounded child inside and the adult who can finally care for that child. Some people can already occupy the adult position. Others hear only the inherited critic, go blank, or feel like the child pretending to be grown. They may need to borrow one adult function before they can carry it. The aim remains an internal adult who stays present without asking another person to become the missing parent forever.

Reparenting needs deliberate practice: bilateral journaling, the baby-self practice, somatic work, written vows to the inner child, and self-compassion performed as an actual internal action rather than a pleasant concept. Some of it can be done alone. Some moves much better with a witness who can lend warmth or a boundary without taking over the parenting. I call the bridge *borrowed adulthood*: receive care, observe it, participate, initiate, and gradually internalize it. This is an author-developed synthesis of familiar practices, not a validated treatment sequence on its own. The pairs manual gives enough to begin.

*3. Full Hearthwork.* Weekly pair counseling over time, then small circles, coordinators, and the rest of the methodology. This level is for people who want an enduring practice and a community around it, alongside their own repair.

Reparenting is the hinge in all three. Friendship can offer pieces of it. Therapy can sometimes guide it very well, but expensively, intermittently, and inside a relationship that ends when the appointment ends. Hearthwork makes reciprocal practice more available. *The relationship that most needs healing is the one between your adult self and your inner child. Other people can lend care, model a capacity, and help you practice it; the relationship becomes durable as that capacity returns to you.*
