# Therapy can’t rebuild the village

Mental-health numbers keep getting worse despite the enormous expansion of professional services. Loneliness kills at rates compared with smoking. Anxiety and depression are rising across age groups. Suicide has not declined in countries with the most developed therapy infrastructures. Something is producing distress faster than the professional system can absorb it, and the source is larger than any individual patient.

Industrial modernity dissolved the places that once held ordinary psychological life. Extended families scattered. Weekly religious communities weakened. Mutual-aid societies and rooted neighborhoods thinned out. Intergenerational households became harder to maintain. Capitalism needs labor to move, and moving breaks ties. Suburban housing separates people. When two parents work full-time, neither has the hours that older forms of community required from at least one full-time adult. Religion shrank, and for most people nothing comparable took its place.

The platforms came afterward. They simulate connection, fragment attention, and feed us one another in doses nobody can take in. This is a business model. It works better when you stay alone and scrolling than when you spend the evening with people in a room.

Professional therapy grew inside the same modernity. It began selling pieces of the lost human container back to us, one hour at a time, by appointment, with copays, and a healthy dose of sexual abuse as icing on the cake… A truly good therapist can be very useful for specific kinds of work, assuming you can figure out how to vet one. Therapy still can’t provide what most people are starving for: being known across years by people they trust, inside reciprocal relationships that continue after the clock runs out. The therapist won’t see you in your ordinary life, and you won’t see them in theirs. The relationship is paid and asymmetric by design. That is what makes it clinical. It can do certain things well. It cannot replace the village.

*It takes a village to raise a child. We have replaced the village with a single overworked parent, a Department of Child and Family Services, and a CCTV camera.*

The state usually answers family suffering with a report, a social worker, a file, a placement, or surveillance. When a child is hurting, when a family is failing, or when somebody becomes mentally ill in ways the household can’t contain, the state rarely gathers a village around them. It puts them into a system. The result is often surveillance plus harm.

Most therapists—aside from [MAPS-trained dark empaths](https://substack.com/@udontexist/note/c-258839445?utm_source=notes-share-action&r=5vdc6m)—are trying their best inside an inadequate arrangement. Many social workers are heroic. The practical point is simple: if you keep treating a social collapse as an individual problem, individual solutions will keep failing you.

## The access story doesn’t add up

Our culture describes the mental-health crisis as a problem of *access*. Give everybody a therapist and the crisis will recede. That explanation fails twice.

First, there will never be enough therapists. The training pipeline is long, the labor is expensive, geography is uneven, and many of the people with the greatest need are too poor or too rural to receive regular care. The mismatch survives better policy because this labor-intensive form of care cannot match the size of the need.

Second, therapy was never designed to supply an entire psychological ecology. It is usually one paid person meeting one client for fifty minutes in private, once a week. Even an excellent version reaches only a small part of what a healthy life requires. The daily reciprocity of being known, the weekly rhythm of community, the seasonal movement through celebration and grief, and the multigenerational holding of birth and death have to come from somewhere else.

For much of human history, those things came from the village. Some people still receive them from religious communities, working-class urban neighborhoods that haven’t fully dissolved, intentional communities, or extended families that survived the pressure. Most people in industrial modernity receive them from nowhere and notice only after they’ve been hungry for years.

AI mental-health tools are arriving, and some will be genuinely useful. They will also almost certainly be used to replace human care that was already missing. Insurance companies will pay for a chatbot instead of a therapist when they can. Employers will prescribe wellness apps instead of improving working conditions when they can. The technology can be useful; the political economy decides what it replaces.

## The old answer is still available

Humans already know the basic method. Sit down. Pay attention. Listen without interrupting. Take your turn being heard. Meet again next week. Keep meeting with the same people long enough that they know who you were before the current crisis.

Quaker clearness committees, twelve-step rooms, Buddhist sanghas with practice discussions, nineteenth-century working-class friendly societies in England, restorative circles in Brazilian favelas, and the Forum practices of intentional communities in Germany and Portugal all refine parts of this. The details differ. Equal turns, disciplined attention, ongoing relationship, and some ritual marking the time as held appear again and again.

Our moment also allows a specific synthesis. We can take the basic peer-counseling form from RC, add inner-child and reparenting practices from contemporary depth psychology, add somatic awareness from Somatic Experiencing and polyvagal theory, use Gendlin Focusing for the preverbal felt sense, borrow Quaker clearness for discernment, use restorative circles for conflict and harm, and build safeguards against the failures of older traditions: concentrated authority, sexual misconduct, financial exploitation, and penalties for leaving. The resulting methodology can hold more than any one of those sources held alone.

That synthesis is what I’ve been developing. There are two manuals, one longer version for groups and one shorter version for pairs who don’t yet have a circle. Both are free. Both are adaptable. They are starting points rather than sacred documents.

The methodology is still the smaller project. Two people in a room practicing disciplined mutual care are doing something powerful, and they remain two people in a room. The longer task is rebuilding community itself.
