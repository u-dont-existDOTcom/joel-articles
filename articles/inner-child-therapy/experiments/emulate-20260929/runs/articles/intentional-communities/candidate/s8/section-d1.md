The review from the now grown child matters more than founders usually want it to. The adults can talk forever about what they intended. The person who actually lived through the system may eventually describe something else.

## The Mother Is Primary. The Village Is Real. Neither Replaces the Other.

I'm in the camp that says moms should raise their own kids, but in a community where the care is really shared, so it isn't all on them. The village can handle meals, supervision, teaching, protection, playing, and a lot of the fathering, but it should never take the kids from the moms to "liberate" the moms.

A big part of why I think community matters for kids is Jean Liedloff's [*The Continuum Concept*](https://www.arvindguptatoys.com/arvindgupta/conconcept.pdf). Keep in mind that it's an outsider's view of the Ye'kuana, and she may be idealizing them. Still, it's a powerful picture. She notes that they carry their babies with them wherever they go and bring them into daily adult life. Children are around people of several ages and learn by taking part, and no one exhausted parent is expected to be a child's whole social world. While a nuclear household can recreate some of this, a real village can make it normal.

Experiments with kids sleeping and living apart from their parents should be approached with special caution. Research on Israeli kibbutzim found that [the practice of children sleeping in a communal setting eventually came to an end, due to a combination of developmental findings, parental preference and social change](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12395568/). Kids can benefit from high-quality group care and from contact with several different loving adults, but replacing a secure attachment to their parents is a different matter.

That being said, Tamera's Children's Place deserves a direct look, not a caricature. Personally, I'm still biased toward systems that open up a kid's circle without making the bond between parent and child ideologically inconvenient.

## Children Need Freedom Inside Real Protection

[caption] African kids enjoying their childhood!

Kids also need their own territory, a space to create their own society in, invent games and work out ordinary disputes, without always being under the management of adults.

I remember that from my time at a Unitarian youth camp, and it's still one of the freest things I've experienced in my life. ZEGG's *Kinderhaus* works from a similar idea. The adults take care of safety on the outside, but the kids construct their own world.

Because this can be badly misunderstood, the safeguards have to be spelled out. A children's territory can't become a place where adults stop paying attention. It can't be a place where older kids dominate younger kids without the adults noticing, or where secrecy gets treated as a sign of independence.

I also don't buy the suburban paranoia that says kids shouldn't have warm relationships with grownups who aren't related to them. It's good for a child to know and be known by lots of grownups who enjoy them, and who would notice and act on any kind of problem that might come up. That kind of fear won't stop the bad things from happening, but it can destroy the good things.

The protection has several parts:

Give children real agency. Their "no" matters. No grown-up has a right to a child's affection, physical contact, obedience beyond what legitimate safety needs, or time alone with them.

Have a no-secrets rule. No grown-up may ever ask a child to keep a gift, a game, a touch, a conversation, or a relationship secret from the child's mother or another trusted adult. Surprises are different, because there's a time when the surprise will be revealed. But secrets meant to isolate the child aren't allowed.

Teach kids to be truthful before there's a crisis. They should know that what they say is powerful and will be taken seriously. That teaching must never turn into an interrogation, punishment for not being sure, or a reason to dismiss what a child reports. A scared or confused child may not tell the story perfectly, and still need protecting.

Hear the child even when the person accused is loved by others in the community. That's where many communities fail, because the adult may be charismatic, useful, rich, spiritually important, or just everybody's friend. You have to know what you're going to do ahead of time, before anyone's face is attached to it. First, put the child in immediate safety. Then see that a competent and fair investigation is done, and don't allow anyone to hide behind their status in the community.

Make sure kids have relationships and a reporting route outside the community. Grandparents, cousins, friends from down the street, teachers and other trusted adults give them perspective on their life, and someone to talk to. Make sure they have at least one trusted adult, advocate or reporting route that doesn't go through the leadership of the community. In 1984, Vermont authorities [raided Island Pond and seized 112 children](https://vtdigger.org/2024/06/21/40-years-later-island-pond-has-little-interest-in-revisiting-its-historic-raid/), and a court returned them after rejecting the state's blanket request. State intervention can fail spectacularly. Trying to enclose children completely within a community can also fail. Children need more than one world.

I also searched [the research corpus](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/) for a well-documented case of a child in an intentional community who continued to be seriously dangerous to other people, where the record follows the whole sequence of events: the conduct, the assessment, the intervention, the review, and what happened later. I didn't find one. That may be because the records are private, or because the cases were sent elsewhere, but it means I can't claim that communities already know how to handle the hardest version on their own. The child, the family, and the people at risk all need access to competent help and review that the home community doesn't control.

## The Door Must Open Outward at Adulthood

Amish young people decide for themselves, as adults, whether or not to join the church, and [roughly 85 percent or more eventually do](https://groups.etown.edu/amishstudies/social-organization/population-growth/). Popular culture has blown *Rumspringa* out of proportion, as if every teenager went wild, but adult baptism still matters, because it makes belonging a commitment instead of a fact imposed at birth.

If all the children stay, how would anyone know whether they'd come back if they were free to leave? Let them study, travel and make friends. Let them encounter other ways of life, and disagree with the founders without being treated as traitors.

Every childhood narrows the future. Most ordinary parents aren't just going to give their kid a bunch of money at 18 and say, "Go see the world." They'll try to get them ready for some next step that's going to actually work. A poor or money-free community may have even less cash to offer, and food, medical care and basic survival may rightly come first.

The realistic standard isn't unlimited freedom. It's that leaving shouldn't be made artificially impossible. Kids who grow up without using money especially need some education about how the money world works, so they aren't helpless in it: how to get a job, pay rent, sign contracts, deal with a bank, avoid scams, get ID, get around, and ask for help when they need it. They need skills and an education they can take with them, relationships outside the community, access to their records and documents, and at least one plausible next step, whether that's school, an apprenticeship, work, family or another community. If the community can help out with temporary housing, travel, tools, or a small reserve for the transition, great. But even if it can't, it can at least not punish people for leaving, or withhold the knowledge and records they need to function somewhere else. No upbringing gives perfect freedom. The point isn't to erase path dependence. It's to keep belonging from turning into a trap.

Some may leave and stay away. Others may start their own communities with different values, instead of waiting decades to inherit the original one. Parents may feel that as a loss, even when it's the movement evolving.

### Discuss Child-Rearing Values Before Children Are Caught Between Them

Couples who share a home, a culture and a bed still split over screens, vaccines, [schooling](https://www.instagram.com/p/DcDGotaFCeZ/?utm_source=ig_web_copy_link&igsh=MzRlODBiNWFlZA==), religion, food, discipline and medical care. Now multiply that by twenty adults with twenty different childhoods.

Goodwill isn't enough to answer those questions once there are children in the middle of the disagreement. Communities need enough shared principles about raising children to offer coherence, and enough room for parents to still be parents.
