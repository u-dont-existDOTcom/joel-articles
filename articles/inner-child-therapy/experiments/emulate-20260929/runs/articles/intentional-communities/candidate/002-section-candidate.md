# The New Age May Dawn Suddenly

I just put a movie my father made in 1988 up on the Internet for the first time: [*Where's Utopia?*](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/wheres-utopia-click-read-more-my) It was the result of years of work, traveling to all kinds of communes and communal experiments around the country, trying to answer a question that for him was primary: How do we live together without recreating all the loneliness, domination, and stupidity we were supposedly escaping?

He had already spent 17 years in the [Bruderhof](https://www.bruderhof.com/), a Christian community that doesn’t use money internally. He had no personal money there: income went into the common purse and the community provided what members needed. So when I talk later about moving beyond internal money, I’m not proposing something nobody has ever lived. My father spent most of two decades doing it. They also accepted him as a Jewish atheist, making him a remarkably long-running doctrinal exception. In meditation there, he came to his own understanding that “God is the Universe, and the Universe is Love.”

He might have stayed, but the U.S. antiwar movement during Vietnam needed somebody who knew how to set up printing presses. He eventually founded the Aquarian Research Foundation—not a commune, but a nonprofit devoted to researching how the planet could have a positive future and publishing what it found. His conclusion was simple and enormous: human beings need small tribal communities again, because [Civilization Is A Hoax,](https://government-scam.blogspot.com/2020/06/everything-you-were-taught-is-lie.html) as I blogged about at the beginning of the Covid plandemic.

This is the ***escuelita*** referenced in my title. The term “escuelita,” meaning “little school,” comes from the Zapatistas in Mexico. Their [*escuelitas*](https://schoolsforchiapas.org/advances/schools/la-escuelita/) are places where people come and live for a while to learn how the Zapatistas practice autonomy in their communities. In the same way, people would live in these healing communities to heal themselves and to learn how to practice the methods they’re using, so that some of them could leave and set up another healing community somewhere else. Otherwise, we’ve built a lovely refuge for whoever got there first.

I grew up on shoulders, visiting every community we could at the time ([Twin Oaks](https://twinoaks.org/), [Acorn](https://www.ic.org/directory/acorn-community-farm/), [East Wind](http://eastwind.org/), [Padanaram](https://www.facebook.com/p/Padanaram-100064853281734/), [Zendik](https://highlandscurrent.org/2017/05/09/leaving-zendik-farm/), and more). This was boring as hell at the time, because I was just a visitor’s kid, close enough to see how people lived but not part of any tribe, but it gave me a great sample to compare against.

From 1999 to 2001, my family actually lived with the [Love Israel family](https://uwapress.uw.edu/book/9780295988856/the-love-israel-family/) in Washington while escaping [Y2K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_2000_problem), that brief historical period when otherwise reasonable adults prepared for civilization to be defeated by a two-digit date. We lived in a yurt. There were wild berries of every kind, and I loved being in nature. I was also lonely, bored, irritated by the cult programming, and missing my friends in Philadelphia.

I wanted to befriend the children there, but they treated me as an outsider. My mom never liked the place. The leader was also named Love Israel, and to me he seemed like the fakest person I’d ever met, complete with the expected God complex.

We spent a lot of time on our knees in the community garden growing food they could sell in town. The money was opaque and seemed to travel mostly upward toward Love. Meanwhile everybody sang, “Here in the garden, there’s nothing left to do but eat sleep and keep clean.” Again and again. 🌱

My father eventually gave the community his airplane. They sold it instead of using it. He felt tricked, began exposing what was happening, and then we were asked to leave.

The [East Wind](http://eastwind.org/) commune was almost the opposite way. There were maybe eighty people when I was there, enough to be diverse, small enough to actually make friends. I was very happy there. I especially liked showering in a big communal shower with both sexes present. I wonder if that still goes on at East Wind? It's the sort of thing that doesn't make it into the history books.

Even at East Wind, though, there was a power issue. They had a nut butter factory, which was providing a lot of their income, and the woman who was running the factory was very hard to question. Otherwise I thought it had a great social life, a nice way of distributing labor, and no obvious guru.

There were like eight people at the [Acorn](https://www.ic.org/directory/acorn-community-farm/) commune when I was there, and no kids. I didn't feel like it was really a commune I could join so much as a group of people who were already friends, and who happened to live on some really beautiful land and be good at building cool stuff. It would have been hard to join; it would have been more like asking whether their group chat had an opening.

[Twin Oaks](https://twinoaks.org/) was big, like two or three hundred people, and almost institutional. It was friendly enough and interesting enough, but I didn't feel like I was in a tribe there. It was more like a tiny hippie town, with a really complicated labor credit system.

One thing that struck me as odd about all of them, basically, was that they didn't have a communal therapy practice. All the usual human pain and suffering came into the commune with them, and it all got translated into politics.

Which meant weekly meetings, run according to Robert's Rules of Order, with enormous agendas, where most of the things you wanted to talk about couldn't be talked about, and you generally felt like you'd rather be sleeping. (Some people solved this problem by just going to sleep in the meeting and openly snoring.)

[Zendik](https://highlandscurrent.org/2017/05/09/leaving-zendik-farm/) went in the other direction. They held a brutal community meeting that I’m still recovering from 35 years later. 🤣 Five-year-old me was put in the center and told I was bad because I hadn’t volunteered to help adults with whatever jobs they were doing. Apparently kindergarten had already failed to teach me labor consciousness.

The secular communities often buried hurt under procedure. The spiritual communities often put one person in charge of everybody’s inner life. Most of the rest of this article is my attempt to describe the missing third option.
