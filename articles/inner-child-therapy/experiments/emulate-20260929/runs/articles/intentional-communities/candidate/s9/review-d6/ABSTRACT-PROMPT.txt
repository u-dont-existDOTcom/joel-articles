[caption] A Zapatista Schoolhouse. It reads: Worthy Rage (Digna Rabia)

In 2005, a few college buddies and I went down to Oventik, one of the Zapatista caracoles in Chiapas. We almost died several times getting there. The mountain road ran along cliffs, and our rental car was on its last legs, which is a great combination when you're on your way to visit a revolution.

In San Cristóbal de las Casas, the only white man we met sat us down in a café and grilled us so directly that he seemed like a CIA agent. Why were we there? Who were we working with? What were our plans? When were we leaving?

It made us feel important for a minute. We were just college kids in a dying rental car, but apparently the empire had questions for us. 😂

Oventik itself was like something out of a dream. We played basketball outside with the Zapatista youth, and people gave us talks about their history and philosophy, which I translated for my friends because my Spanish was better. Visitors were coming from all around the world to learn, and to make the communities less easy to attack in silence.

I felt a deep spirituality from the Zapatistas. I also suspect there may be a private relationship with ayahuasca, but nobody told me that, and it's only my impression, not something to take as fact.

What impressed me the most was how they do authority. It's mandar obedeciendo, to lead by obeying: the representatives carry the decisions people made in the assemblies, and they have to answer to the people who gave them the mandate.

The Zapatistas are also the closest thing to a whole example that I found, because their autonomy goes beyond how they vote. They've set up their own education and health care, and their own justice, and they run their own collective production and political formation. On top of that they've worked out ways of teaching outsiders, all while coordinating beyond a single village.

The Fire and the Word describes the movement growing from family to village, village to region, and region to larger structures. It also gives one of the most useful Zapatista principles for community design: build the practice first and develop the theory afterward. The autonomous municipalities came first; an association of them became a rehearsal for the Good Government Boards. When different municipalities encountered different problems and uneven progress, that experience generated another structure rather than being forced to fit the original plan.

After a long internal review, in 2023, the Zapatistas announced they were dissolving their previous federated governmental structure, which was split into Rebel Zapatista Autonomous Municipalities and the Good Government Juntas. The original idea was to have power vested in the local communities, but it had crept up and consolidated itself at higher levels over time for practical reasons. Their new arrangement moved authority downward toward a Local Autonomous Government in each community.

Institutions get more and more layers and offices as they age. We see this same pattern happen repeatedly, everywhere in history, except usually it goes one way without reversal, unless there's a revolution.

But there is a special reason the Zapatistas were successfully able to reverse this degeneration. Zapatista autonomy grew out of Indigenous communities with long histories. They share language and family ties. They've fought land struggles together, and defended themselves together for decades. A new ecovillage can't copy the diagram everyone can see and get the trust underneath it right away.

And that's not me saying that. Despite actually trying to train people in their model, the Zapatistas themselves have been clear that people shouldn't expect to directly or easily import/export their philosophy (Zapatismo) as something ready-made, shake & bake. The useful thing isn't their diagram. It's how they learn: they build things and ask questions, and when they find out where the practice isn't working, they change the structure.

Now, the Zapatistas also don't prove my whole economic path, from unpriced internal necessities, to a common purse, to getting rid of outside money. Do their communities use collective work to support their schools and clinics, and their autonomous government and resistance? Yes. But they also sell products and trade, and they get outside support. So what do they show? That major necessities and public functions can come out of ordinary market purchase and be governed collectively instead. Outside help can make a community more capable, sure. But if the specialist or the donor or the organization stays indispensable, it can also create gatekeepers and uneven dependence.

image 15

[caption] Escuelita Zapatista — The Zapatista school to teach outsiders their way of life

In celebration of the 10th anniversary of their autonomous governments, the Zapatistas opened up the Escuelita, where outsiders lived with families and learned autonomy by taking part in day to day life. Around 1,500 students came to the first sessions, and thousands more came to later ones.

One way an escuelita could fail is if it becomes a business, where residents spend their lives performing community for paying outsiders, and success gets measured by how many people come instead of how many daughter communities get started. A week of feeling transformed isn't what it's for. It's for teaching enough of the social and material system that some people can build it somewhere else.

In the context of my research I have also looked for evidence of what happened after the Escuelita. While I found information on alumni networks, people bringing practices back with them, and organizing later, I couldn't find a clear chain of somebody having been to the Escuelita, then a new communal institution being set up, and then evidence about how its members and their children actually did over time. Zapatista influence was already spreading abroad before the Escuelita, so if some institution resembles a Zapatista one later, that alone doesn't prove the Escuelita was responsible. I'm borrowing the Zapatistas' teaching method and their ambition to replicate, not claiming they already proved my whole model.

Sociocracy has a very different history, yet some similar features have emerged there as well: circles, decision-making by consent, distributed authority, and linking between the levels. Does this prove there's a universal formula? No. But it means that people building institutions of this sort have another serious system to study, instead of inventing their governance during their first dispute.
