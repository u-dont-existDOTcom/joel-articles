# Stance check: section 11 rewrite ("Founderism: The Four Questions"), a3

## Conflicts in the rewrite

**1. A rule ("Medicine is optional") becomes a right to refuse someone's offer**

- Rewrite: "If someone offers you medicine, you can always say no."
- Essay: "Peer-led never means casual. Screen for medical and psychiatric contraindications." and "The medicine stays behind the other pl/ork." ("My Ceremony Principles"); "Screening, sober sitters, emergency plans, integration, and clear boundaries all have to exist before the first ceremony." ("3. Medicine pl/ork"). The published sentence it replaces is "Medicine is optional."
- What a reader would wrongly believe: that in Joel's model medicine reaches members when someone offers it to them personally, and refusing is the safeguard. In the essay it comes through screened ceremonies with prerequisites, and nobody is expected to join them.

No other sentence in the rewrite changes a position the essay states. The other differences are only wording.

## Contradictions in the published text

**1. The "boundary" vs. the ceremony prerequisite (section 11 vs. "My Ceremony Principles")**
Section 11 names the risk, "Medicine prerequisites can create gatekeepers with power over people at their most open," and then answers it: "the principles are commitments I choose for my own practice, never powers the community gains over me." But "My Ceremony Principles" makes the prerequisite a gate the community applies to other people: "This prerequisite also filters out experience collectors." The prerequisite can't be both a commitment each person chooses for their own practice and a filter on others. The section never says which one it is, so the gatekeeper risk it raises is never answered. The rules that follow ("Medicine is optional. Abstaining cannot reduce membership rights.") protect people from pressure to take medicine. They don't cover people being refused it.
Rewrite: still has it ("when things are required as pre-reqs for people to get medicine" … "These aren't principles to give the community power over me").

**2. Joel's own land vs. the model's admission guarantee (section 11 vs. "The Freeloader Problem" and "Resources, Land, and Exit Terms")**
Section 11 lists "No individual or permanent clique controls admission" as one of the model's guarantees. The same section pictures people who "end up living on land I buy," which matches the plan stated earlier: "I do plan to buy land, and people may naturally end up living around me" (The Freeloader Problem). But the essay also says "Whoever owns the land holds enormous power over the community" and "Choose the legal form … before the purchase" (Resources, Land, and Exit Terms). If one person buys the land first and residents arrive later, that person decides in practice who may live there. The section flags this ("Ask them about me") but never says the land would leave his individual ownership. As written, the guarantee and the plan can't both hold.
Rewrite: still has it ("ask them about me, if people end up living on land I buy"; "No single person … will have the power to decide who gets into the community").

**3. One warning sign proves nothing, yet one sign means leave (within section 11; a weaker case)**
About the daily signs, which include "disagreement is treated as failure," the section says: "None of these proves abuse by itself. Several together deserve attention." Later in the same section it says: "When a community begins diagnosing dissent instead of responding to it, leave." Diagnosing dissent is very close to treating disagreement as failure. So one sign first deserves attention only when others come with it, and later it is reason to leave at once. Leaving doesn't require proof of abuse, so this is conflicting advice rather than a strict logical contradiction. Still, a reader can't tell whether to wait for several signs or leave the first time dissent is diagnosed.
Rewrite: still has it ("Presence of one of these alone isn't proof of abuse" … "Once you notice a community diagnosing dissent like this instead of answering it, leave.").

Conflicts in the rewrite: 1. Contradictions in the published text: 3.
