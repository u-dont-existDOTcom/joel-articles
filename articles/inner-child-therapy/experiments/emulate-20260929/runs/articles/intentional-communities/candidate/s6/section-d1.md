# Which Connections to the Technosphere Do You Keep?

Loneliness is only one reason this feels urgent. I wrote separately about the wider polycrisis:

I just read this post from Jamie Wheal about the “polycrisis,” which I suppose needs no explanation, but it’s basically a word for summarizing that every kind of shit possible is hitting the fan which was already half broken:

I don’t know exactly how much of the future fear is accurate. AI may make human life enormously better, suck millions of people out of their jobs, put itself between us and nearly everything as a sort of digital gatekeeper, or do all three before Chantress Seba gets through her commune applications.

Investing in becoming more independent as a human feels like a better idea to me than waiting for certainty. I'd rather have community and discover I didn't need an exit than need an exit after spending ten years optimizing my feed.

So in designing a community, which connections to the technosphere are useful for you to keep? Which ones do you limit?

New friends are silver, but old friends are gold

Things like medical care, banking, phones, vehicles, schools, supply chains, government ID, internet and energy are each a separate decision. If you opt out of all of them completely, it can cause needless suffering. If you opt into all of them, the outside system can reach directly into every essential function.

The Amish have spent generations weighing in as communities on how to use (or not use) certain technologies, depending on what they do to community life. Different groups come up with different answers. A tool might be OK at work but not at home, or shared instead of everyone owning one, or held off until it's clearer what it does to people. Whatever else you think of the Amish, they get that adopting something is a group decision, which an automatic software update isn't.

Medicine is one of the hardest couplings to work out. On the one hand, a community might want to work with as “natural” a form of medicine as possible, but on the other hand, what happens when someone needs trauma surgery, antibiotics, dentistry, emergency obstetrics, or some other specialist care? It’s important to think about these things ahead of time and work out how members of the community are going to access care, what the community will guarantee from common resources, what might need outside money or insurance, and how the community will treat people who want a different level of care.

No one should have to go through an ideological hoop while they're frightened or sick. When someone in the community gets catastrophically ill, the agreements should be in place to support whatever that person chooses, including access to common or outside money when it's needed, transportation and privacy, and they should allow for the possibility that the community's preferred approach won't be enough.

At the same time, the Surgeon General’s advisory showed that social connection has a measurable impact on several health outcomes. While community is not a panacea, it can change the day-to-day conditions in which people get sick, heal, age, and die.
