SECTION: Founderism: The Four Questions

PUBLISHED:

Every community begins with somebody’s initiative. A person finds the land, writes the first documents, teaches the method, attracts the first members, or keeps the whole thing alive when everybody else loses interest.

That contribution creates real influence. Pretending it doesn’t exist only makes the influence harder to examine.

I know the tension personally because many methods in this article are mine. I wrote the materials. That means I can easily become the person who decides whether everyone else “understood” them correctly. Anti-authoritarian language does not immunize an author against enjoying authorship.

A few things help. Judge the method by outcomes people can observe: are members flourishing and safe; can conflicts be resolved; can people disagree, leave, and later tell a different story; are children doing well when they grow up; does the community remain materially viable; can it correct a bad prediction; can it replace the founder; and, if it is meant to spread, do later projects actually emerge and last? Longevity, occupancy, consensus, and member retention are not enough by themselves. A group can have high retention because people love it—or because leaving costs too much. Do not judge the method by fidelity to the founder’s preferred interpretation.

Allow parallel implementations. Fork the method. A community that changes Hearthwork and gets better results has taught me something. If every adaptation still needs the founder’s approval, the parallel versions aren’t really parallel.

Here are the four questions:

Can the community respectfully override the founder? Can it replace the founder’s role? Can it revise the founder’s vision? Can it survive the founder’s absence?

Then follow the boring power. Whose name is on the deed? Who controls housing, food stores, vehicles, tools, and any outside bank account? Who keeps the records, sets admissions, chooses reviewers, decides access to children or medicine, and can make a complaint disappear? The org chart can say “horizontal” while the keys, passwords, titles, and reputation all point to one person.

An override is real only if it can operate against the person being challenged. If the founder chooses the reviewer, holds the records, owns the land, and can remove the complainant, the community cannot meaningfully override the founder no matter how many circles it holds.

Ask these about any community, teacher, or method. Ask them about me if people end up living on land I buy.

Also watch the daily signs: the founder becomes more central over time; disagreement is treated as failure; every bad result is blamed on poor implementation; insider knowledge grows while public accountability shrinks; outcomes are never measured; former members become morally contaminated the moment they leave.

None of these proves abuse by itself. Several together deserve attention before the founder explains why this situation is spiritually unique.

Every practice in this article can be turned against the people using it.

“Nothing suppressed, everything processed” can become compulsory disclosure. I’ve seen the risk in communities using [ZEGG Forum](https://www.theartofrelating.com.au/zegg-forum.html). A group begins by inviting honesty and ends by treating privacy as resistance.

Peer counseling can become surveillance delivered in a warm tone. Medicine prerequisites can create gatekeepers with power over people at their most open. “Willing to be seen” can become a demand that everybody reveal whatever the group wants to know.

The boundary is simple: the principles are commitments I choose for my own practice, never powers the community gains over me.

“Nothing suppressed” describes my relationship with my material. It does not authorize anyone to extract that material. Members can decline to share without producing a therapeutic explanation for the refusal.

Medicine is optional. Abstaining cannot reduce membership rights. No individual or permanent clique controls admission. Disagreement has to be answered as disagreement.

“Your resistance is your wound talking” is one of the most efficient sentences ever invented for avoiding an argument. When a community begins diagnosing dissent instead of responding to it, leave before they schedule a group process about your leaving.

There is also a floor the majority cannot vote away: bodily safety, enough food and sleep, independent medical care, private thought, contact with people outside the community, a way to report harm without asking the accused person’s friends for permission, and a usable way to leave. A majority vote does not make deprivation consensual.

While founderism is a problem in every community, it's especially so in communities which form naturally over time as I'm describing may happen in my case. In such cases, there's no formal community and no formal rules until people decide to come together to make them. It doesn't make sense to do that at first, if, say, I'm the only one there and I don't know who else will be there that I could trust. If there's one other person who comes to visit, it also doesn't make sense, so there's a critical mass point at which people decide "we were just living together like friends, but now we want to formalize the community."

If I could find a suitable commune to join, that would basically reverse this problem. I would then be the one on probation. But if we are all starting together from scratch, then we would all be at an equal probationary level with each other.

It's a bit like the process of getting married. At first, you visit your girlfriend or boyfriend when schedules align and each of you agrees. Over time as you get closer to marriage (which hopefully is not a fully unconscious process), you start making decisions together and considering sharing more and more resources. Once married, if you decide to divorce, you split up the resources, although that's also contentious depending on how long the marriage was for, whether one person feels tricked, etc,  and unfortunately often ends up in court.  Leaving a commune is the divorce half of it, where the shared resources have to get split up. When joining or forming a commune, we can hopefully be more conscious about the process and less romantic.

Friends who come to visit don't get written terms, though. That's not how anything works, and at first they don't even know if they'll stick around for a day or a week. There are implied terms, like be respectful and be honest, and those shouldn't need to be in writing. If someone is offered something like an internship or a probationary period, that would require written terms. Or if it naturally evolves and they bring up wanting something like that, it could be done.

## How My Own Model Could Become a Cult

[caption] I could totally start a cult. Trust me.

Every practice in this article can be turned against the people using it.

“Nothing suppressed, everything processed” can become a situation where someone in the group must tell all, even if they don’t want to. I have seen that risk in groups that use the [ZEGG Forum](https://www.theartofrelating.com.au/zegg-forum.html) practice. A group starts out inviting honesty and ends up treating privacy as resistance. But privacy also can be resistance to the communal spirit, so there has to be a dynamic, living, always re-assessed balance between what you and the community owe each other, and what remains your own domain. In general, people can have their own private practices, of course. But some practices go too far against the communal spirit. If someone starts worshipping Satan, say, they get booted.

Peer counseling can become peer surveillance, even if the tone is warm. And when communal therapy processes are pre-reqs for people to get medicine or to be respected members, it can feel like the community is gatekeeping. Well, it is gatekeeping. Some people simply should not be let in the gate, at least not yet. And some people will resent that, and choose to leave, and that's fine. Some communities will be stricter gatekeepers, and others will be more lax. There are pros and cons to each extreme, and again, a living balance must be struck.

It should go without saying that nobody has to take medicine to have equal respect in the community. But medicine is an optional part of the overall therapy process, and if a member is avoiding the therapy process and becoming hard to deal with, they will naturally not belong at a certain point. If it gets to the point of being impossible to live with, they will be kicked out. But nearly always, people who don't fit in leave way before that.

They should not be demonized for not trying to fit in. They should be offered compassion, but ultimately it's up to them if they want to fit in and follow the communal processes. And in some cases, the community may make exceptions. If someone is actually sick and needs care, kicking them out could be like a death sentence. It depends on so many factors that I can't decide any of this ahead of time. It's up to the people involved.

No single person or permanent group of people should handle grievances and have the power to decide who gets into or stays in the community. Each person should have a usable avenue for exit. Dissent is important for growth. That doesn't mean everything everyone says is equally valid, though. Sometimes it really is their wounded ego projection, and that should be acknowledged. But again, balance, because if complaints are too often or too broadly painted as projection, that's as much a failure, if not more so.

There are some basic human rights that can’t be voted away from you by the majority in a community, unless it's a question of resource availability as a whole. If safety and the conditions for health can be provided, it should be provided. People should be able to maintain relationships with others outside the community, as long as those relationships don't interfere with the community as a whole (for example, you can't bring your boyfriend to live with you if he's not only not a member of the community, but also antagonistic towards it).

It can be tricky to spell everything out, though. Let's say a grandmother is demonizing the commune. Cutting off contact with her could make things way worse. When it's tricky and dangerous for everyone like that, I think the commune needs to step in and help people decide how to communicate with outsiders. Nobody can give that advice ahead of time, including me. It has to be worked out in the moment, context by context.

If your human rights are unfairly stripped from you, even by a majority vote, then you can vote with your feet.
