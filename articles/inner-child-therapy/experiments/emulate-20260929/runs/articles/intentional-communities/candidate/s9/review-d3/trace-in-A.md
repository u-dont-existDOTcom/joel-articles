## P6 → P6g

CONTEXT BEFORE: P5 (published, kept): The Zapatistas felt deeply spiritual to me. I also suspect there may be a private relationship with ayahuasca, but nobody told me that, and I present it only as my impression—not as fact.

PUBLISHED P6:
What impressed me most was their form of authority: *mandar obedeciendo*, leading by obeying. Representatives carry assembly decisions and remain answerable to the people who gave the mandate.

REWRITE P6g:
What impressed me the most was how they do authority. It's mandar obedeciendo, to lead by obeying: the representatives carry out the decisions people made in the assemblies, and they have to answer to the people who gave them the mandate.

CONTEXT AFTER: P7 (rewrite): The Zapatistas are also the closest thing to a whole example that I found, because their autonomy goes beyond how they vote. They've set up their own education and health care, and their own justice. They run their own collective production and political training. They've also worked out their own ways of teaching outsiders, all while coordinating beyond a single village.

## P10 → P10r

CONTEXT BEFORE: P9 (rewrite): After a long internal review, in 2023, the Zapatistas announced they were dissolving the Rebel Zapatista Autonomous Municipalities and the Good Government Juntas. Their new arrangement moved authority toward a Local Autonomous Government in each community. Those local governments can coordinate through regional collectives, but the coordinating levels are explicitly described as depending on the communities instead of sitting above them.

PUBLISHED P10:
Most institutions accumulate layers and offices as they age. The Zapatistas reviewed theirs and pushed authority downward. They did not issue a leadership book and open a certification program. They changed the system they were actually living in.

REWRITE P10r:
Most institutions get more and more layers and offices as they age. The Zapatistas did a review of theirs, and then pushed authority down. Did they put out a book on leadership and open a certification program? Nope. They just went and changed the system they were actually living in.

CONTEXT AFTER: P11 (rewrite): This is also where I have to rein in my enthusiasm. Zapatista autonomy grew out of Indigenous communities with long histories. They share language and family ties. They've fought land struggles together, and defended themselves together for decades. A new ecovillage can't copy the diagram everyone can see and get the trust underneath it right away.

REWRITE P12d:
One thing the Zapatistas have been clear about is that people shouldn't import or export Zapatismo as a ready-made model. The useful thing isn't their diagram, though. It's how they learn: they build things and ask questions, and when they find out where the practice isn't working, they change the structure.

CONTEXT AFTER: P13 (published; its rewrite isn't settled): They are also not proof of my complete economic path from unpriced internal necessities, to a common purse, to eliminating outside money. Zapatista communities use collective work to support schools, clinics, autonomous government, and resistance; they also sell products, trade, and receive outside support. What they demonstrate is that major necessities and public functions can be taken out of ordinary market purchase and governed collectively. Outside help can build capability, but it can also create gatekeepers and uneven dependence when the specialist, donor, or organization remains indispensable.
