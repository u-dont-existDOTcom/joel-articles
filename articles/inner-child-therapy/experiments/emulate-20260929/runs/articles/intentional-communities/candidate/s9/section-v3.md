# The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas

[caption] A Zapatista Schoolhouse. It reads: Worthy Rage (Digna Rabia)

In 2005, a few college buddies and I went down to Oventik, one of the Zapatista *caracoles* in Chiapas. We almost died several times getting there. The mountain road ran along cliffs, and our rental car was on its last legs, which is a great combination when you're on your way to visit a revolution.

In San Cristóbal de las Casas, the only white man we met sat us down in a café and grilled us so directly that he seemed like a CIA agent. Why were we there? Who were we working with? What were our plans? When were we leaving?

It made us feel important for a minute. We were just college kids in a dying rental car, but apparently the empire had questions for us. 😂

Oventik itself felt like a real dream. We played basketball with Zapatista youth on an outdoor court. People gave us talks about their history and philosophy, and I translated for my friends because my Spanish was better. Visitors from around the world were coming to learn and to make the communities less easy to attack in silence.

The Zapatistas felt deeply spiritual to me. I also suspect there may be a private relationship with ayahuasca, but nobody told me that, and I present it only as my impression—not as fact.

What impressed me the most was how they do authority. It's *mandar obedeciendo*, to lead by obeying: the representatives carry the decisions people made in the assemblies, and they have to answer to the people who gave them the mandate.

The Zapatistas are also the closest thing to a whole example that I found, because their autonomy goes beyond how they vote. They've set up their own education and health care, and their own justice. They run their own collective production and political training. They've also worked out their own ways of teaching outsiders, all while coordinating beyond a single village.

[*The Fire and the Word*](https://www.amazon.com/Fire-Word-History-Zapatista-Movement/dp/087286488X) describes the movement growing from family to village, village to region, and region to larger structures. It also gives one of the most useful Zapatista principles for community design: build the practice first and develop the theory afterward. The autonomous municipalities came first; an association of them became a rehearsal for the Good Government Boards. When different municipalities encountered different problems and uneven progress, that experience generated another structure rather than being forced to fit the original plan.

After a long internal review, in 2023, the Zapatistas announced they were dissolving the Rebel Zapatista Autonomous Municipalities and the Good Government Juntas. Their [new arrangement](https://enlacezapatista.ezln.org.mx/2023/11/13/ninth-part-the-new-structure-of-zapastista-autonomy/) moved authority toward a Local Autonomous Government in each community. Those local governments can coordinate through regional collectives, but the coordinating levels are explicitly described as depending on the communities instead of sitting above them.

Most institutions get more and more layers and offices as they age. The Zapatistas did a review of theirs, and then pushed authority down. Did they put out a book on leadership and open a certification program? Nope. They just went and changed the system they were actually living in.

This is also where I have to rein in my enthusiasm. Zapatista autonomy grew out of Indigenous communities with long histories. They share language and family ties. They've fought land struggles together, and defended themselves together for decades. A new ecovillage can't copy the diagram everyone can see and get the trust underneath it right away.

One thing the Zapatistas themselves have been clear about is that people shouldn't import or export Zapatismo as a ready-made model. The useful thing isn't their diagram. It's how they learn: they build things and ask questions, and when they find out where the practice isn't working, they change the structure.

Now, the Zapatistas also don't prove my whole economic path. (That's the one that goes from unpriced internal necessities, to a common purse, to getting rid of outside money.) Do their communities use collective work to support their schools and clinics, and their autonomous government and resistance? Yes. But they also sell products and trade, and they get outside support. What they do show is that a community can stop buying major necessities and public functions on the market, and govern them collectively instead. Outside help can make a community more capable, sure. But if the specialist or the donor or the organization stays indispensable, it can also create gatekeepers and uneven dependence.

In celebration of the 10th anniversary of their autonomous governments, the Zapatistas opened up the [*Escuelita*](https://schoolsforchiapas.org/advances/schools/la-escuelita/), where outsiders came to live with families and learn autonomy by taking part in day to day life. The first sessions had about 1,500 students; later ones had thousands more.

One way an *escuelita* could fail is if it becomes a business, where residents spend their lives performing community for paying outsiders, and success gets measured by how many people come instead of how many daughter communities get started. A week of feeling transformed isn't what it's for. It's for teaching enough of the social and material system that some people can build it somewhere else.

In the context of [this research](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/) I have also looked for evidence of what happened after the *Escuelita*. While I found information on alumni networks, people bringing practices back with them, and organizing later, I couldn't find a clear chain of somebody having been to the *Escuelita*, then a new communal institution being set up, and then evidence about how its members and their children actually did over time. Zapatista influence was already spreading abroad before the *Escuelita*, so if some institution resembles a Zapatista one later, that alone doesn't prove the *Escuelita* was responsible. I'm borrowing the Zapatistas' teaching method and their ambition to replicate, not claiming they already proved my whole model.

[Sociocracy](https://www.sociocracyforall.org/sociocracy/) has a very different history, yet some similar features have emerged there as well: circles, decision-making by consent, distributed authority, and linking between the levels. Does this prove there's a universal formula? No. But it means that people building institutions of this sort have another serious system to study, instead of inventing their governance during their first dispute.
