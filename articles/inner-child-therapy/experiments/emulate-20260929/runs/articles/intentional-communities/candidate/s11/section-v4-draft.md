# Founderism: The Four Questions

In every community there was somebody who took the initiative to get it started. Somebody had to find the land, write the first documents, teach the method, recruit the first members, or even just keep things going when others lost interest.

That person ends up with real influence, and pretending they don't just makes that influence harder to look at.

I know this tension firsthand, because a lot of the methods in this article are mine. I wrote the materials. So it would be easy for me to become the guy who decides whether everyone else “understood” them correctly. Writing in anti-authoritarian language doesn't keep an author from enjoying being the author.

A few things help. One is to judge the method by results people can actually see. Are the members flourishing, and are they safe? Can conflicts get resolved? Can people disagree, leave, and later tell a different story about it? Are the kids doing well once they grow up? Is the community still materially viable? Can it correct a prediction that turned out wrong? Can it replace the founder? And if it's meant to spread, do new projects actually start up and last? How long a community has lasted, how full it is, whether it reaches consensus and how many members stay aren't enough on their own. A group can keep its members because people love it, or because leaving costs too much. And don't judge it by how closely people stick to the founder's own interpretation.

Allow for parallel implementations. Fork the method. If the community finds a way to alter Hearthwork and get better results, they've taught me something. If it needs the founder's “blessing”, then it's not really running in parallel, is it?

Here are the four questions:

Can the community override the founder, with all due respect? Can the community replace the role of the founder? Can the community alter the vision of the founder? Can the community exist without the founder?

Then follow the practical power. Whose name is on the deed? Who's in charge of the housing and the food stores, the vehicles and tools, and any bank account outside the community? Who keeps the records? Who decides who gets admitted, and who picks the reviewers? Who controls access to the kids, or to medicine, and who can make a complaint go away? The org chart might look flat and horizontal, while the keys, passwords, titles and even just general reputation all point to one person.

An override is only real if it can actually work against the person being challenged. If the founder picks the reviewer, holds the records, owns the land, and can kick out whoever complained, the community can't really override the founder, no matter how many circles it holds.

Ask these questions about any community, teacher or method. And ask them about me, if people end up living on land I buy.

Also watch for the daily signs. Increasing focus on the founder over time. Sense that disagreement is failure. Bad results are blamed on lack of good implementation. Increasing “insider” knowledge. Decreasing public accountability. Failure to measure results. Sense that those who leave are morally contaminated.

Presence of one of these alone isn't proof of abuse, but if several are present, they are worth paying attention to before the founder can explain how the situation is uniquely different in a spiritual way.

## How My Own Model Could Become a Cult

[image 16]

I could totally start a cult. Trust me.

Every practice in this article can be turned against the people using it.

“Nothing suppressed, everything processed” can turn into compulsory disclosure. I’ve seen that risk in communities that use ZEGG Forum. A group can start out inviting honesty and end up treating privacy as resistance.

Peer counseling can be turned into peer surveillance, even with a warm tone. Requiring practices like that as prerequisites for medicine can create gatekeepers who have power over people when they are most open. Even the idea of “willing to be seen” can be used to demand that people disclose to the group whatever the group wants them to.

The boundary is simple. These aren't things that give the community power over me, they are simply a set of principles I am attempting to live up to.

“Nothing suppressed” applies to myself and my relationship with my own material. It doesn't give anyone the right to pull anything out of me. Any member can choose not to share material, and they don't need to provide a therapeutic rationale for not sharing.

The medicine is optional. Choosing not to take the medicine in no way affects anyone's rights as a member. The right to let people into the group is not vested in any individual or permanent clique. And disagreement gets answered as disagreement.

“Your resistance is your wound talking” is pretty much a conversation ender. Once you notice a community diagnosing dissent like this instead of answering it, leave. Better yet, leave before it even gets to the point of people wanting to do group process about your leaving.

There are some basics that the majority should not be allowed to vote away from any member of a community. These basics include your bodily safety, adequate food and sleep, the right to go to a doctor of your choice, the right to keep your thoughts private, the right to communicate with people outside the community, the right to report harm without asking the accused person's friends for permission, the right to leave. Removing these rights from a community member is not consensual, even if a majority vote does it.
