Dissolution and Exit

This is the last thing you want to talk about when you are picking a name for your commune and imagining the first fruits of your labor. But it is vitally important to talk about.

If the group breaks up, what happens to the land, debts, buildings, equipment, common stores, common funds, and the people? Who will make sure they have housing and food in the interim period, especially medication, health care, child care, schooling? Who will be responsible for bridging gaps in people’s lives until they can get a foothold in the outside economy (if necessary, including securing food stamps, cash assistance, etc.)? Who will be responsible for transportation, records, contact with outside family, etc.? Who is responsible for the animals and for ensuring the group meets its obligations to the land? What if there are outstanding claims against the group?

Like a living will, you want to assign in advance someone to ensure that each critical function has a bridge or successor, on the assumption that the institution itself cannot be salvaged.

It is possible for a group to dissolve and for people to still be able to part as friends, or at least to part in a way that everyone leaves with their dignity intact and with a good story to tell about why the group didn’t work out.

Institutions die. Relationships do not always. This is a great comfort. For example, one of the sisters I grew up with on The Farm recently became ill. A number of people who had not seen her in a long time reached out to her in a very loving way, even though the economic structure we all used to be part of has changed dramatically.

The Bruderhof still impacts my life, even though I am no longer a member. It led me to start a foundation, make a movie, visit a number of interesting places, and think about a number of interesting issues.

Even when the legal entity is gone, some of the fruits can remain.
