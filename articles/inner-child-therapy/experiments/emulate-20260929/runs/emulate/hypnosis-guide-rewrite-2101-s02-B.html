Yes, if you are working with a trauma-informed clinician who is trained in this method and is with you the whole time as you intentionally go back to find a trauma you don’t know about yet, using an affect or somatic “bridge.” (To use a “bridge,” you follow associations from an affect or sensation to earlier memories. It’s a different thing than “regression,” which is going back to remember or imagine what it was like when you were younger, and can be used for many things, including installing resources.) If you have a lot of flashbacks already, or dissociative reactions, that’s a lot like being there, too. Hypnosis is not a way to know if something actually happened, though, even if you have a clinician there.

If you have a history of trauma, or come to tears easily, or want to have feelings you don’t currently have, it’s up to you how big a range of experience you want to work with. Some people choose to work only in a very small range. Some can work in a big range. It’s not about being confident. It’s about how much support you have.

The guidelines for people who have experienced major trauma generally point to trauma-specific variants of CBT, trauma-focused treatments like Cognitive Processing Therapy and Prolonged Exposure, and EMDR. Self-hypnosis and inner child work can be part of it. But the big trauma needs a big container.

The key indicator is: Does this make the rest of your life more loving, honest, functional, relational, grounded, precise, etc. If not, don’t go deeper. Go back to getting enough sleep, eating well, grounding yourself, setting boundaries, and finding support outside yourself.
