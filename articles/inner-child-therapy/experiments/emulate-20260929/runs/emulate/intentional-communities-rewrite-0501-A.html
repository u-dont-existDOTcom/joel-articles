The Medicine Part, Without Pretending It Isn’t There

In various places around the world, psychedelics are in the process of being legalized. Psilocybin will be available in licensed service centers in Oregon starting in 2023. Colorado is setting up a regulated natural medicine system, with licensing starting in 2025. New Mexico passed their Medical Psilocybin Act in 2025. In Australia, psychiatrists can prescribe psilocybin and MDMA for a few conditions since 2023.

However, this legalization process has been extremely bumpy. For example, MDMA hit a roadblock in 2024, when the US FDA issued a Complete Response Letter , indicating they would not approve MDMA for the data submitted and asking for more evidence. Furthermore, countries which have legalized certain substances are often not being correctly represented. For example, Portugal decriminalized small amounts of all drugs for personal use, but it’s often lazily misstated as if they legalized everything; trafficking drugs is still criminal there.

There are many differences in laws around psychedelics, depending on which country, state, etc, and depending on what substance, and whether it’s for religious purposes, and what the activity is, etc. If you’re thinking about setting up a community for medical purposes, please do get real legal advice!

Despite the increasing legalization of these substances, most communities I know of which use them are not very open about this. There’s still enough stigma around them that, even where it’s legal, they can scare away parents, neighbors, insurance companies, local political officials, etc.

I’m writing about this here because otherwise I would not be representing my model truthfully. Some of you may already be familiar with my writing on psychedelics, in particular my work at loveyhuasca / Haoma. I felt I needed to include this section, even though I also wanted to minimize risk to myself by writing this article.

The case against including medicine: The Global Ayahuasca Survey found that adverse physical and mental health effects from Ayahuasca are common , though severe effects are much less common and context matters. Medicine has the potential to open things up for people faster than they can support / handle it.

Medicine also attracts people who want the peak experience / revelatory information / new stories, without then doing the work to implement these changes in their lives. So communities which primarily attract people for the peak experience may not attract people who will do the work to actually contribute to building and running a community.

The case for including medicine: From my own experience, I know that communities which use psychedelics can be incredibly powerful for getting people out of their heads and connecting with each other in a non-defensive way. Of course, it can also be super messy and bring out all kinds of issues like grandiosity, dependency, etc. It can be great if the people involved are mature enough.
