Founderism: The Four Questions

Community projects require somebody to actually do things like finding land, writing documents, or teaching people the method, or else the project never gets off the ground or dies for lack of continued energetic work.

This gives that person a lot of influence in the project, whether we acknowledge it or not.

In the case of this article, it’s me. I’m the one who came up with most of the methods in here, and wrote the material that teaches them. I can’t very well blame myself for falling into the traps I’m warning about!

One thing that helps is to agree to judge the method by its results, rather than by how well it agrees with some preferred interpretation. (The preferred interpretation will inevitably be mine.) Are people happy and safe? Are they able to resolve conflicts when they arise? Are they able to disagree with each other, or to leave the community if they want? Do their kids turn out okay? Is the community economically viable? Is it able to change its mind when its predictions don’t come true? Is it able to replace the founder when the founder leaves? Does it go on to seed new projects, which themselves last for a while? Simply asking whether the community is old, still occupies its site, still uses consensus, and still has the same members, is not enough, since a high fraction of members might stay because it’s hard to leave rather than because they want to.
