The East Wind commune was almost the opposite way. There were maybe eighty people when I was there--enough to be diverse, small enough to actually be friends with everyone. I was very happy there, mostly. I especially liked showering in a big communal shower with both sexes present. I wonder if that still goes on at East Wind? It's the sort of thing that doesn't make it into the history books.

There was a power issue at East Wind, I think, mostly around electricity. The woman who started their nut butter factory brought in most of their income, and it was hard to question her authority. But otherwise, I thought it had a great social life, a good system for distributing work, and no obvious guru figure to worship.

There were like eight people at the Acorn commune when I was there, and no kids. I didn't feel like it was really a commune I could join so much as a group of people who were already friends, and who happened to live on some really beautiful land and be good at building cool stuff. It would have been hard to join; it would have been more like asking to be invited into their group chat.

Twin Oaks was big, like two or three hundred people, and almost institutional. It was friendly enough and interesting enough, but I didn't feel like I was in a tribe there. It was more like a tiny hippie town, with a really complicated labor credit system.

One thing that struck me as odd--about all of them, basically, was that they didn't have a communal therapy practice. All the usual human pain and suffering came into the commune with them, and it all got translated into politics.

Which meant weekly meetings, run according to Robert's Rules of Order, with enormous agendas, where most of the things you wanted to talk about couldn't be talked about, and you generally felt like you'd rather be sleeping. (Some people solved this problem by just going to sleep in the meeting and openly snoring.)
