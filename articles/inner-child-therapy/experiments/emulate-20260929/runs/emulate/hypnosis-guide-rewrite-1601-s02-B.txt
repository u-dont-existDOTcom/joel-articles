It's okay to be firm in your request for help. "Well, before we talk about that, I'd like you to tell me what you're feeling." Or, "Before you explain it to me, can you pay attention to what you're feeling?" Remember that you don't have to like what you're feeling. As long as you can stay with it, as long as you want to change and keep changing, there's no reason to look for a better feeling just because you're sad or tense.

Separate observations, meanings and thoughts about the meanings. These categories may blend together, but it's useful to be able to ask yourself, "What am I observing? What meaning am I getting from this observation? What do I think about this meaning?"

Statements about feelings can be observations or meanings. "I get tight in my stomach" is an observation. "I feel that he's an evil person" is a meaning. It may be useful to ask for observations to back up a meaning. If the meaning is nonverbal, it's okay to verbalize it for the purpose of analysis. Verbalizing a thought doesn't mean that it wasn't nonverbal when you first had it. Nonverbal messages are not necessarily better than verbal ones just because they may come first. Feelings can be analyzed just as well as facts.
