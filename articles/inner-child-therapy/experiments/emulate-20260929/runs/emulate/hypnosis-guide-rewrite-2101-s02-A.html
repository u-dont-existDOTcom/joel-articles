Methods to explore with a trained person present

If you are actively trying to find unknown traumatic origins of a specific affect or somatic sensation (“bridging”), or if you are experiencing substantial flashbacks or dissociation, it is better to have a trauma-informed clinician present who is trained in this method. Working with sensations and feelings by following associations is called “bridging,” whereas working by imagining or remembering how you may have experienced things when you were younger is called “regression.” Although these can be used for many purposes including resourcing, if you are using these specifically to uncover trauma, it’s better to have a trained professional present. Remember, however, that even in that circumstance hypnosis is not a valid way to determine what happened in your history.

If you have a trauma history, cry a lot, or just want to work with difficult feelings, there’s no reason you can’t work on your own with this material. You just have to choose how wide a range to work within. For some people, that range is very small. For some people, it’s quite wide. You have to judge for yourself how much support you need—it’s easy to be overconfident.

If you have major trauma, guidelines usually recommend a range of trauma-focused treatments, often variants of cognitive behavioral therapy, including Cognitive Processing Therapy, Prolonged Exposure, and EMDR. Working on self-hypnosis and inner child work can be a helpful adjunct to those treatments, but working on major trauma requires a big container if you’re at high risk.

Notice whether working on this material is helping you be more loving, honest, functional, relational, grounded, precise, etcetera in ordinary life. If not, don’t go deeper and work on sleeping, eating, grounding, setting boundaries, getting support, etcetera.
