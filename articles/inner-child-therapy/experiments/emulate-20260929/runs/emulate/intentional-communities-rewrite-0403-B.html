This is a capacity of the community as a whole, not any one individual, whether that person is a professional, a guru, or the emotionally needy butt-hole of the community.

The important part here is that the person providing care is not simultaneously in a position of administrative authority over the person receiving it. That is, in order for this to be real peer care, the person receiving it should not be administratively subject to the caregiver in terms of community membership, work, housing, access to children, access to medicine, or in terms of being able to provide or withhold evidence in a conflict. If the caregiver has administrative authority over the care-receiver, the care is no longer peer care; it is a relationship with hierarchy.

It is also important to know what is outside the realm of your competence as peers, and to be able to access outside or expert assistance when needed.

Crucial to solve at the source, not leave till it bubbles up to administrative meetings as agenda item #67 at the bottom of a 100 page agenda.

  1. The container: this is the boring shit that sounds least sexy to the people who are interested in community but nevertheless is absolutely crucial to a solid community; it includes things like membership, land, community resources, governance, children, and exit.

I personally am interested in moving towards ever-decreasing use of money in the community context. I see this as happening in three stages, generally speaking.

One: all basic life functions are taken care of by the community, with no money changing hands among community members. These basic life functions include food, housing, care, tools, education, transportation, etc. People could still have income from outside work, but if they do they must provide an agreed-upon portion of that income to the community. This is probably the best “starting off” place for communities that want to move in this direction.

Two: all income, including income from outside jobs and from any businesses run by the community, goes into a common purse. No one “accumulates” while a member of the community. This has been done for many years by communities like the Bruderhof (a religious/anarchist community where my dad lived for 17 years) and also by non-religious communities like Twin Oaks and many others.

Three: the community ceases to use money even in relationship to the outside world, instead relying on direct need-metting, exchange, gift, work, obligation, etc. I have never heard of a “modern” community that has actually done this, although it is my ultimate goal. It may be impossible to do this completely, due to taxes and other money that must be paid to the larger society for things like land, etc, so the closest we may be able to get is a community that is almost entirely moneyless, with a small collective cash boundary around it.

This may seem weird to a lot of people, since there is no money, but think about it; who has access to the food, housing, tools, land, medicine, transportation, and whatever currency your community may use with the outside world? That’s power!

The reason why these topics may not be as exciting to people in a spiritual context is that you’re not likely to get an ecstatic vision about how your provisional membership policy could use some tweaking.

And you can have the most beautiful land in the world, but if you don’t have it, it doesn’t do you much good. You can have the best peer counseling in the world, but if you don’t have a membership policy, you’ll take in people you can’t support. You can take the best medicine in the world, but if you don’t have an exit policy, you won’t have agreements with people about how to leave the community.

The first three are the “inside” of the community; this is the container, the place where the inside stuff takes place.

You might try ignoring this, but eventually you’ll have to deal with it.
