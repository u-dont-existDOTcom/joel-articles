Why Communities Don't Work

I’ve noticed that when the communities I’ve been a part of have failed it’s been for one of two reasons.

They’ve failed for secular reasons.

Mostly what this means is that the community has been organized in a way that doesn’t account for or deal with the inner world. And so the inner world–jealousy, early life trauma, feelings of humiliation, sexual attraction, resentment, sadness, etc–insinuates itself into the political process.

There are a number of groups–many of them member’s of the Federation of Egalitarian Communities and practicing some form of income sharing–that have very sophisticated ways of dealing with the political process. Who does what work, how money is spent, how decisions get made, etc. But they don’t have any way of dealing with the fact that sometimes people just hate each other.

And so emotional material finds its way into the political process and it’s basically impossible to deal with it when it’s tangled up with questions of schedules, committee membership, noise, food, or other policy issues.

They’ve failed for spiritual reasons.

What this means is that while the community was oriented towards inner world transformation, that inner world work got tangled up in questions of authority because it revolved around a central figure: a founder, a teacher, a channel, or someone else who was understood to have special access to the accurate interpretation of reality.

The Farm , a community of ~1500 people in Tennessee, had a lot of beautiful elements but in its early days much of the spiritual (and therefore social) life of the community revolved around Stephen Gaskin . From reading various things written by former members it seems that the community doctrine渗ed into a lot of intimate decision making processes and it wasn’t always easy to avoid being subject to Stephen’s opinions, despite the talk of universal consciousness.

These two problems are essentially two sides of the same coin: secular communities separate authority from inner world work, spiritual communities conflate the two. And the problem with conflating the two is that you inevitably end up giving someone authority in both their role and outside of their role. Whether that’s a spiritual founder, a therapist, or someone who is running the community’s economic life through farming or business, it’s not good when they can control all of the levers of power.

What I’m looking for is a way to have depth work without a guru and distributed authority with emotionally complete people. It’s going to need some pretty good peer-to-peer practices and a lot of play.
