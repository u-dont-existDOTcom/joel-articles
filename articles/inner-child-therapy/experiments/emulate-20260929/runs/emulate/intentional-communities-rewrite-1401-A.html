Dissolution and Exit

It may seem morbid to discuss dissolving your commune while you’re still naming it and waiting for your first harvest, but it’s really important.

If your commune dissolves, what happens to the land, to any debt you may have to outside parties, to any buildings or other equipment, to any stores or funds you have in common? What are each member’s rights at that point? And who will make sure that everyone has a place to live and eat, gets whatever medication or other care they need, has their children cared for and in school, has a bridge to whatever food/housing/medical/outside income/benefits they need, has transportation, has their records and contact with family, and so on, as well as to any animals or obligations to the land or any other parties who may have claims against the group?

Think of it as designing a living will. Even if the institution isn’t salvageable, many of these functions can and should continue, and it’s helpful to pre-assign them to a bridge or successor.

Furthermore, by pre-assigning these, you can allow the commune to dissolve in a way that doesn’t unnecessarily hurt people or strip them of dignity.

Remember that even though the institution/relationship may “die” the people don’t have to. There are many examples of people from a commune supporting a former member in time of illness, even though they haven’t seen each other in years and the communal economy has changed dramatically since they left (such as the two sisters I follow on American Commune, who grew up together on The Farm).

The Bruderhof has continued on in my dad’s life in many ways, and through the foundation he started, the film he’s made, and the people/places he’s shown me, in mine as well.

And so on, and on, even after the legal entity has ceased to exist.
