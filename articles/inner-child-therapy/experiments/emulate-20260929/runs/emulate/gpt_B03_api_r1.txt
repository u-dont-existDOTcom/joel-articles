Make the Protector Visible

Protecting your little ones are you setting boundaries? When the little one feels lonely and wants to text his ex who dumped him via text at 2am in the morning, can he rely on you to put the phone in another room so that in the morning it is easier for him to be able to believe your words that you are glad he is there with you?

Most of the time your actions as a protector are seen by the little one. Every time you set a boundary or say no to something that you know would be bad for the little one, you are building trust. Dealing with those letters from the bank, keeping a promise, cleaning your room – these things demonstrate your trustworthiness to your little one. If you try to soothe your little one but don’t then do what you said you would do, to the little one it feels like the unreliable people from their past. Leaving a degrading relationship is also a protector thing!

Other protecting activities are carried out in your head. Stopping yourself from acting on fear, rage, cravings, shame etc in terms of what you say, send, buy, take, do, have sex with, commit to etc. This often involves not acting right away when you feel like you don’t have time. These actions are between you and the little one. Allowing the little one to be sarcastic with you without retaliating is a good example. Or letting the little one not to trust you without trying to convince him otherwise.

The little one may grumble that you only turn up when things are bad but, yes, that is what you do and letting him know that is also a good thing. Calling yourself an idiot to yourself and then going back to the little one to say sorry is another. All of these things are seen by the little one. Not retaliating shows him that despite what he may believe, you do love him. If your nurturer isn’t in the picture yet, you as the protector can start to build a relationship with the little one. It may take a while to develop the nurturing side but you can start.

Choose one thing to do and work out when you are going to do it. Make it small and easy if you need to. Work out whether you need any help to do it. Rehearse the difficult bits in your mind before hand. If you are saying no to someone, it is often difficult in the pause after you’ve said no but before they have replied.

Once you have done it, review whether it helped. If you haven’t experienced any relief it doesn’t mean that it didn’t help or that it was a failure. Keep doing the things that help you feel more safe, more connected, more able and/or help you enjoy life more. If they don’t, stop doing them. If you haven’t done what you said you would, work out why. Was it fear? Or did you decide it wasn’t something you wanted to achieve? Or didn’t you know how? Were you too tired? Or the office was shut?
