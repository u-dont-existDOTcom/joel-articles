## Exact candidate

### Also Look Outward

One ugly conversation can make you think, okay, now I know who this person is. Then later they come back and actually talk about what you raised. They think you're wrong. You hate the boundary they're setting. At least now you're arguing about the actual complaint. That makes the first fight a lot less useful as a diagnosis. I pay more attention when completely different conversations keep ending in the same place: you bring up something that hurt you and somehow wind up managing their reaction while your own experience disappears. [Lindsay Gibson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) writes about that kind of repeated relational pattern in her work on emotional immaturity.

You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.

If you really did hurt them, repair it.

You can mean the apology. Then you notice you're also apologizing for having brought the complaint up at all, because they're still upset with you. Maybe you weren't clear, so you try again and say it better. They still may not understand why you said no. Guilt doesn't settle how much of the conflict was yours, and it doesn't prove they're manipulating you. You don't have to keep talking until they understand.
