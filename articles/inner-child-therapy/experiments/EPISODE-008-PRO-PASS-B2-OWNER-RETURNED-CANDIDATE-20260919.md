### Write It. Don't Send It Yet.

They don't answer right away. They write the reply they actually want to send and leave it unsent. Later they read it again. A sentence that felt necessary at first may now look unfair or muddled; something they were tempted to soften may still be exactly what they mean. If an apology is genuinely theirs, it can stay without swallowing the complaint.

They read it again for the parts that are trying to do too much. One sentence may be explaining what happened, defending their character, and demanding forgiveness all at once. They can separate those things. They can fix what was clumsy without withdrawing what was true.

Then they ask a plainer question: what do I actually want this person to know? Not everything they felt has to be delivered. Not every accurate thought will help. The message may get shorter. It may become an apology for one part and a firm no about another. Or they may realize there is nothing useful to send.

If they say they will answer more fully later, they choose words they can keep. “I need some time before I answer the rest” is better than promising tomorrow when they do not know whether tomorrow is possible. And if they need distance or protection now, they stop. The page can stay unfinished. The exercise can wait.
