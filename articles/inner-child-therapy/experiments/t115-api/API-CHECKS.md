Borrow Love—or Borrow the Perspective of Care

Sometimes it’s not that you can’t love in general. Your dog can chew up your good shoes and be forgiven before you’ve found the other one. But maybe you direct that love to your little one and the attempt goes flat, or starts to feel weirdly unsafe. Just feel that love you can feel for others for a minute, in your body, before you try to redirect anything. Then as you relax into that and enjoy it more, imagine it flowing downhill, like an overflowing river of delicious chocolate. Now imagine how happy your inner child is at the bottom of the hill to play in that chocolate love that rolled down!

Watch how it lands, though. “I care about you” can be patient, or it can be “I care about you, now hurry up and feel better.” Getting close can be too much, too, the way kids squirm away from an aunt they barely know who goes in for the big hug, and then climb all over the uncle who’s just sitting there. Knowing that love is in you somewhere counts for a lot, and you’re borrowing something you already know how to do.

Or put somebody you care about into your exact life, your family, all the conditions you face, and then step outside it and ask what you’d tell them as a friend. This psychological trick has a rich history around it, and the blind spot behind it even has a name, Solomon’s paradox, after the biblical king everybody brought their disputes to, who then wrecked his own life.

Then take a small piece of whatever you’d tell them and do it yourself. If it’s “get more sleep,” that could be brushing your teeth before midnight instead of at two. The technical term for this is “scaffolding.”

Love Doesn’t Have to Wait for Trust ❤

Sometimes the love is there for your little one and still feels unsafe to them, or safe for a minute and then not. Love and trust don’t have to come as a pair, but they got tied together, as if one had to vouch for the other. *[Human, Medium] So your love can sound like you’re asking them to trust you. *[Human, Medium] The love can go first, though, before their trust is done going over your track record (they’ve heard what you still call yourself when you mess up, sometimes out loud).* Your little one says “I don’t trust you.” You answer, “I believe you. And I love you.”

I was about five when my mom told me it was time for bed (or something equally outrageous to a five-year-old). I cried and screamed “I HATE YOU!” 😡 She answered, “But I love you!” 🥰

That completely threw me. Fights don’t usually go like that. Anger is a way to get more power when you feel a lack of justice or agency. You puff yourself up so you seem frightening or dangerous, or you demand to be heard. My anger was there to get me heard, and her answer showed me I didn’t need it for that. Whatever injustice I thought there was wasn’t coming from a lack of love, either. *[Human, Medium] So the anger seemed stupid.* I saw for myself that it was only hurting me, without helping me see clearly or get what I wanted. Still, what I screamed was true right then, and what she said back was true too, so I could hate somebody who loved me and she’d still be my mom the whole time.

When it’s your little one yelling at you, though, try not to turn “But I love you” into a line you say on cue, because then it can brush the anger off, and what it’s really saying is “so stop being angry.” I think you can mean it and still be doing that. Does the love stay afterward and take whatever’s true in the anger seriously?

The love can stay, and your little one might still come back with “Big fuckity whoopty doo. I don’t believe you.” 🙄 You answer, “Okay. I believe that you don’t believe me. I still love you. 🤗 You don’t have to love me back. Tell me what feels unsafe.”

Your little one might say it’s you. *[Human, Low] You don’t have to accept every conclusion they reach about you, but do listen for the concrete complaint without fighting the yelling and swearing around it.* *[Human, Medium] Like the anger at bedtime, the swearing is there to get them heard, so once you’re listening instead of fighting, they don’t need it anymore.*

You can see the same move in a dramatized scene from the movie A Beautiful Day in the Neighborhood. Lloyd, a reporter, tells Mr. Rogers, “I can’t imagine it was easy… growing up with you as a father.” He doesn’t collapse under that or fight it, but he does answer what’s worth answering in it. He tells Lloyd that until recently his oldest son never told people about his dad, and his youngest “genuinely tested” him. Eventually he and his youngest found their way, and now he’s very proud of both his sons. Then he says, “But you are right, Lloyd. It couldn’t have been easy on them. *[Human, Medium] Thank you.* Thank you for that perspective.”

Also notice what else in you answers your little one, besides what you choose to say. When they say “I don’t believe you,” something in you might fire back an accusation, and then they can learn your love is only there as long as they take it the right way. The resentment can be purely visceral: being doubted after you’ve tried feels unfair, but there’s nothing unfair in them going over your track record. The part that resents not being believed might be the same one that said “I love you” in the first place, or a different one, or a bit of both. *[Human, Medium] Both talk in your grown-up voice, so to your little one they’re the same.* *[Human, Low] Be careful not to take the accusation as proof your love really is conditional, when it could just be another part talking.*

*[Human, Medium] Write It.* Don’t Send It Yet.

If you’ve got something burning you up that you wanna say, write it out as a reply. *[Human, Medium] There.* That got it out. Ahh. Breathing easier. 😌 But don’t send it. Take that breather you earned and then come back to it from a cooler place.

It doesn’t mean you need to hide your upset, or apply for a permit to protest, but choose your battles and execute them wisely, so you don’t end up regretting how you communicated later. As Lao Tsu might have said (if I could ever have read that whole boring Art of War book), a war with no casualties is the best possible war. When high IQ & EQ communication hits a writer’s block, sometimes the best thing is to simply say, “I need some time to think about my response.” And if the conflict really requires a response right now, then obviously just do your best rather than wait for the perfect timing.

And yeah, going back to the writer, which is often my inner child when emotions pop up, is part of the process. It’s like I’m holding his hand and showing him a better way, not like I just stole his pencil because he wrote something embarrassing. So what was he actually trying to say? A lot of the time it’s just “that hurt.”

Other times it’s “I did this too.” My friend Tyler saw a guy smoking in front of a baby and asked him if he thought that was wise. The guy asked if he thought it was wise to mind his own business, and Tyler said, “Well, I believe in Christ, so I thought you might care about life too.” Afterward he wasn’t sure he’d handled it right, or whether he should have thrown the first stone at all, since he’d smoked in front of a baby himself.

But Jesus also said to take the log out of your own eye first, and then “you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother’s eye.” That’s exactly what made Tyler the right person to say something. If you actually have the problem they have, and you want to call them out for it, call yourself out for it instead. What we came up with was something like, “Hey man, when I was younger I smoked in front of an infant, not realizing how selfish that was, and I notice you doing the same thing. I’d ask you to please have some compassion for him. I have a young child now, so this really touches me.” Tyler’s an evangelical Christian who preaches on YouTube, so the fact that he really appreciated this take on Jesus’ message is pretty important to me.

*[Human, Medium] It’s also how your voice sounds, though.* You can say all the right words, but if the tone is accusatory, it doesn’t matter.

Before sending anything, I’d read it once as if I were them, sitting there with my phone. Stuff I wrote while I was scared can sound like a threat to them.

How to Forgive Without Forgetting

For many people, healing does get around to some forgiveness eventually, or at least to not being run by the resentment anymore. It can’t be put on a schedule. And you don’t have to forgive first to get safe, set boundaries, hold someone accountable, get justice or get better. Then again, active resentment narrows what you see, and your little one stays organized around the injury. And if someone pressures you into forgiving early, what you get is an enlightened-looking act, with the wound gone underground.

The understanding that helps me is seeing how a person’s conditioning, history, pain, and capacity shaped the harmful move they made. Picture everything that had made them who they were by that moment, what happened to them and what they learned, the pain they carried and what they could and couldn’t handle. From in there, it was the only move they had. Hurt people often pass on pain they never worked through. You may have heard that from somebody telling you to forgive. When you see it for yourself, though, you might find yourself forgiving them, and if you’ve held them uniquely to blame, the rage can soften into something more accurate.

They still have to answer for what they did, and it still hurt, even if you see how they got there. It’s just that punishing them now can’t reach back and give them what they were missing then, the sense to see it or the help. *[Human, Medium] Or the skills.* What’s left is fixing what can be fixed, and making it easier for something different to happen now.

So a boundary can still be right. So can the law, if it comes to that, or telling them plainly it was wrong (or telling yourself, if they’d never hear it). Keeping away from them for good can be right too, and you can still forgive them from over there. Understanding somebody’s limitations should make my expectations more realistic, not make those limitations less consequential. “I understand how you became this way” can sit right next to “I now understand why I shouldn’t rely on you for this.”

Five Stages of Reparenting

For now, I think of the whole path in five fairly predictable stages. You might be starting at any point here (and it may change even from hour to hour as well):

1. You’re still stuck identifying as the lost inner child, forced frequently into handling situations that require more maturity, even if it looks like you’ve got it all together. At this stage, use your childlike superpowers to come into the “Wow of Now” (have you seen the movie Waking Life?), and make sure there’s enough safety around you to continue with reparenting:

2. Begin to borrow adulthood — remember watching Mr. Rogers or Sesame Street? Those adult role models are what we’re looking for at this stage. This can almost feel like playing house as a kid where you imagined how you’d take care of a baby doll etc.

*[Human, Medium] 3.* Practice adulting — do at least one part of caring for yourself while support is still there from the previous step.

*[Human, Medium] 4.* Adult mode itself — after sufficient practice with reparenting, you become a natural at sending love to little you, protecting them, and directing them.

*[Human, Medium] 5.* Adult and child in relationship — the adult leads. The child isn’t pushed away, but doesn’t have to run the whole life alone. This can actually feel really weird if you’ve been stuck in forced adulthood for a while. Our goal is to go from weird to silly and playful. Lose self-consciousness while gaining self-awareness, if that makes sense!

Whichever stage you’re at, saying the right thing when you sit down with your little one helps, but you also have to start actually doing some of it in the rest of your life. Your little one starts believing the grown-up you when they catch you lying down because you’re wiped out, instead of pushing through one more chore.

Reparenting isn’t an exercise you finish, after all. *[Human, Medium] Say your boss asks you to stay late again.* You tell them no and go home feeling okay about it, and nobody had to cry on a meditation cushion for that to happen.

Remember When You Already Did It
