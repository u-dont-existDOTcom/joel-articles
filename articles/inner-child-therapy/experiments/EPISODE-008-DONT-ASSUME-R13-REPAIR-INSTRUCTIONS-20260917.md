## Three diagnostic prose predictions

These are intentionally different assumptions about how the frozen local operations might realize. None is the final candidate.

### Prediction A — one interaction carries almost everything

`### What Keeps Happening Between You?`

You tell somebody they hurt you. They get upset. Somehow the conversation ends with you comforting them, and the thing you originally brought up has disappeared. If this keeps happening, that's more useful information than another hour spent trying to figure out *why* they do it. [Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity is helpful here because it keeps coming back to what people actually show you over time.

One bad conversation is still one bad conversation. People need time sometimes, and they get to have boundaries too. But if the same ending keeps showing up, maybe the useful question is simply: what can I actually count on from this person right now? They don't have to agree with everything you feel. Your experience does need to be allowed to exist, and screwing up matters less if they can ever come back and own their part. If the behavior changes later, wonderful. A promise isn't the changed behavior yet, and you don't need another injury just to make the evidence prettier.

Guilt makes this slippery. You can genuinely owe somebody an apology and still be folding up your own position because you can't stand them being disappointed in you. Feeling guilty doesn't settle the whole case, and it doesn't diagnose them either.

Then you explain it one more time. Maybe *this* version will finally make them understand. Sometimes it doesn't. You can still say no, stop explaining, or leave the conversation unfinished. If the rest of the argument is still yelling in your head, write it there first.`

Assumption being tested: the gold-standard style transfers best when one recognizable interaction dominates and the theory rides inside it.

### Prediction B — the useful question replaces the diagnostic framework

`### What Can You Actually Rely On?`

You can burn a ridiculous amount of brainpower trying to decide whether somebody is emotionally immature, selfish, scared, overwhelmed, avoidant, whatever. Meanwhile the same conversation keeps happening. You tell them something hurt you, they get upset, and somehow you wind up taking care of them while your original point quietly dies on the floor.

[Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) is useful because it pulls attention back toward repeated behavior instead of making you solve the person's secret motive. One bad conversation proves very little. Maybe they needed time. Maybe they were holding a boundary too. But after a pattern starts becoming a pattern, you still have a very boring practical problem: what can you rely on from them *now*? Can disagreement happen without your experience disappearing? Can anything get repaired later? You don't have to know whether they can't do it or won't do it before you protect yourself accordingly. If what they actually do changes, you get new information. A promise is not new behavior yet.

And guilt is terrible at sorting this out. You may owe an apology and still be abandoning your own position just to make their disappointment stop. Both problems deserve attention; guilt doesn't get to choose the winner.

The hardest part may be giving up on being understood. You explain it again, nicer this time, clearer this time, and they still don't see it. You are allowed to stop the trial anyway. Whatever is left unsaid can go somewhere safer before it goes to them.`

Assumption being tested: this section becomes lighter if one simple practical question—`what can I rely on now?`—replaces most of the diagnostic explanation.

### Prediction C — the pressure to be understood is the organizing force

`### You Don't Have to Win the Case`

You tell somebody they hurt you, and somehow you end up comforting *them*. The original complaint has vanished. Maybe that was just one awful conversation; maybe they needed time, or had a boundary of their own. But when you keep arriving at the same ending, the reason starts mattering less than the fact that you keep arriving there.

This is the part of [Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) I want to keep: pay attention to what somebody repeatedly shows they can do in relationship. You may never know whether they couldn't meet you there that day or simply wouldn't. You can still notice whether your experience gets to exist when they disagree, whether anything gets repaired afterward, and what their behavior gives you reason to expect right now. If that behavior changes, great. You don't have to volunteer for another injury while waiting to see whether a promise becomes real.

Then guilt joins the argument. Maybe you really owe an apology. That does not automatically mean your original complaint evaporated. And being desperate to make their disappointment go away can make it very easy to throw your own side overboard.

So you explain yourself again. And again in your head. Maybe if you find the perfect wording they'll finally understand and everything can settle. They might not. You can still make the next sane move while they think you're wrong. The perfect response can stay unsent for a minute.`

Assumption being tested: the most readable route is to organize the subsection around the emotional pressure to win understanding, with repeated behavior/Gibson and guilt arriving only as complications inside that pressure.

## Recommendation

**Prediction A is the strongest starting assumption.**

Pros:
- closest to the gold-standard `Catch the Hook` communication mode: concrete, friendly, socially alive;
- keeps Gibson light rather than building a theory section;
- naturally compresses many source functions without visibly ticking them off;
- creates the cleanest handoff to `Write It. Don't Send It Yet.`.

Cons:
- still risks becoming too comprehensive in its middle paragraph if the writer tries to preserve every qualification explicitly;
- `what can I actually count on` plus `experience exists` plus `own their part` could still harden into a mini checklist during generation.

Prediction B has the clearest practical question but is more explanatory and could drift back toward mini-guide voice. Prediction C has the strongest handoff and emotional pressure, but it risks underweighting the repeated-behavior/Gibson function or turning `win the case` into another polished motif.

Therefore the later writer should apply the **frozen local operations**, with A as the best diagnostic assumption—not copy A as the final candidate.
