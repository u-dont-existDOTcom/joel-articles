## V10 replacement architecture

This draft is organized around two live mistakes, not around preservation-unit families:

1. **Mistake one:** treating present-day adult capacity as proof that vulnerability is safe with that adult.
2. **Mistake two:** fixing that mistake by treating frightening identities, beliefs, thoughts, or past conduct as proof of current harmful intent.

The difficult second mistake gets disproportionate thought. The sovereignty/obedience contradiction lives inside it because it tests freedom to refuse without settling theology.

Only after those two mistakes are sorted does the prose ask what adult-level work remains and what claimed change in intent would look like.

Protected functions remain outside this generative map and are traced afterward.

## Exact candidate

Central qualification:

> That assumes grown-up you is at least willing to protect vulnerability rather than exploit it.

New H2:

> ## When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child
>
> I was using “adult” a little too loosely above. Being able to look around the room, pause, and make choices tells me some grown-up capacity is there. It doesn't tell me whether vulnerability is safe with you. If frightening or exploiting it is something you currently want—or you want therapy to make you better at doing that—don't bring the child forward to find out. Their fear can't be the test, or part of the reward.
>
> I don't want that to become a purity test either. A religious identity, an unusual belief, an intrusive thought or unwanted fantasy, anger, charged language, or something harmful you did before does not by itself tell me what you intend now. The same goes for “sovereign.” If somebody says, “I'm sovereign,” and then, “I have to obey this urge, doctrine, group, spirit, or master,” I don't need to settle the metaphysics. I want to know whether they can pause, question it, refuse, change their mind—and separately whether they actually endorse frightening or exploiting vulnerability now. If you're helping, you can ask those questions without flattering cruelty, arguing theology, or teaching somebody to exploit people more effectively.
>
> If the person really does want to frighten or exploit vulnerability, keep the work with the grown-up problem. What is actually hurting, if anything, or what do they hope help will change? If the wish to harm is tied up with an identity, that identity is the work for now: what does it give them, what does it cost, who does it serve, and if they expected it to make life better or happier, did it?
>
> And if the intent changes, don't use the child as the proof. Let the change show up in repeated non-harmful choices, including when exploiting vulnerability would feel rewarding. Until then the side doors count too: no child dialogue, childhood photos, hypnosis, borrowed-parent imagery, spiritual figures, or another indirect route. Some people here may need sustained care from a real human being more than another internal exercise or a bot.
