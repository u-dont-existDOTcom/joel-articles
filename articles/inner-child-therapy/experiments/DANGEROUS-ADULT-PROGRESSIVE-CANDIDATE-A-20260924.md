## When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child

Readiness is not only a question of how much of the grown-up is present, though. An adult can be steady, present, and fully able to choose and still be the wrong person to sit beside a frightened child, because what matters here is less the amount of capacity than what that capacity is currently for. The real question is whether, right now, some part of you wants to frighten, humiliate, use, or hurt what is vulnerable, or finds that it enjoys doing so, and that is a narrower question than it may first seem. Firm religious conviction or unusual beliefs don't answer it. Neither does an intrusive thought you hate, anger, charged language, an unwanted fantasy, or something harmful you did in the past. None of that by itself establishes current harmful intent. Where the wish to harm, or the pleasure in another's fear, is actually alive now, it can be tempting to bring the child forward for just a moment to see what happens, but that moment settles nothing. If the child shrinks back, you have learned only that the child is afraid. If something in you is gratified by the shrinking, the experiment was never a test of safety at all, and it has already become part of the harm it claimed to be checking for.

Once the child is out of it, the work stays with the person making the adult choices, because that is where the identity or stance lives and where its consequences are already showing up. If the word “sovereign” is part of that stance, there is an odd collision worth sitting with: “I'm sovereign,” but also, “I have to obey this urge”—or the doctrine, group, spirit, master, whatever the claimed authority is. You don't need the child to settle that contradiction. Whether the person can actually reconsider, refuse, or change course says more about agency than the label does, and the same adult life can show whether the stance is giving them the life or happiness they expected, what it costs, and who else it serves. Care can stay alongside that without becoming agreement with cruelty, a fight over theology, or practical help for exploiting people.

A promise can sound decisive because it is immediate, but “I won't hurt the child” is not much evidence yet. The evidence comes later, in repeated chances to exploit vulnerability where doing it would feel rewarding and you don't. That is why a change of technique doesn't solve the same problem: whether contact comes through inner dialogue, a childhood photo, hypnosis, or an imagined parent or spiritual figure, the child is still meeting the same adult intent. Sometimes there may be nothing clever to do with that internally for a while, and steady care from real humans matters more than finding another exercise—or another bot—to mediate it.
