## Exact candidate

Central qualification:

> That assumes grown-up you is at least willing to protect vulnerability rather than exploit it.

New H2:

> ## When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child
>
> I was using “adult” a little too loosely above. Being able to look around the room, pause, and make choices tells me some grown-up capacity is there. It doesn't tell me whether vulnerability is safe with you. If frightening or exploiting it is something you currently want—or you want therapy to make you better at doing that—don't bring the child forward to find out. Their fear can't be the test, or part of the reward.
>
> The harder judgment is in the other direction. A frightening label is not the same thing as frightening intent. Someone can be religious, believe something unusual, have an intrusive thought or unwanted fantasy, be angry, use charged language, or have done harm before, and none of that by itself tells me what they want to do now. Even “sovereign” doesn't tell me much as a label. If somebody says, “I'm sovereign,” and then, “I have to obey this urge, doctrine, group, spirit, or master,” I don't need to settle the metaphysics. Can they pause? Question it? Refuse? Change course? And after that comes a different question: do they actually want to frighten or exploit vulnerability? If you're helping them, you can keep caring without flattering cruelty, arguing theology, or teaching them to exploit people more effectively.
>
> If the answer to that last question is yes, the child is not where I would work. Stay with the grown-up problem: what is actually hurting, if anything, or what do they want help to change? If the wish to harm is bound up with an identity, then the identity belongs in that work too—what does being that person give them, what does it cost, who does it serve, and if they expected it to make life better or happier, has it? If the intent later changes, don't ask the child to certify the change. Let it show up in repeated non-harmful choices, including when exploitation would feel rewarding. Until then, the side doors count too: no child dialogue, childhood photos, hypnosis, borrowed-parent imagery, spiritual figures, or another indirect route. Some people may need sustained care from a real human being more than another internal exercise or a bot.
