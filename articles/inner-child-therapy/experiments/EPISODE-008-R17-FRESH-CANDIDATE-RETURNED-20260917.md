### When You Still Don't Know Who's Right

Once you're calmer, you may still not know what to make of the other person. It is tempting to decide that the whole ugly conversation proves they're emotionally immature. But maybe they need time. Maybe they genuinely disagree with you. Maybe the thing driving you crazy is a boundary they're allowed to have. I would care more about what keeps happening over time. [Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) is useful if you want to look at those patterns rather than one bad encounter.

You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.

The really confusing case is when you did hurt them. Maybe there is something you need to repair. But you can also start giving up your own position in the middle of that because you can't stand that they're still disappointed in you, and both things can be happening at once. The guilt can feel like it settles the question. It doesn't. Guilt by itself doesn't establish that you did something wrong, and it doesn't establish that the other person is manipulating you either.

And maybe you never get the satisfying conversation where both of you understand each other. You say what mattered and they still don't see it. You say no and they still think the no is unfair. At some point you may realize you're explaining the same thing again because you're still waiting for agreement to make the interaction feel finished. It may not come. You can leave the disagreement there and still decide what you're going to do.
