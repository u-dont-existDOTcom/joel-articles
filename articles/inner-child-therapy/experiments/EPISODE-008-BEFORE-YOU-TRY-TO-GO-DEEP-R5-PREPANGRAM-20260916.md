# Before You Try to Go Deep

First check whether there's enough grown-up you around to do this right now. Can you look around the room, pause before acting, and make a choice you can actually follow through on? If you're basically the frightened kid and those things have gone offline, skip the deep dive. Get somebody steady around you or do what helps you come back a bit. You can talk to the child later.

## You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue

If you're thinking, “Okay, but I don't actually hear a little kid talking in my head,” don't make one up. Some people have words going through their mind all day and some barely do. You might still be able to think one sentence on purpose even if sentences almost never show up by themselves.

And “ask the child” doesn't have to mean a conversation anyway. Ask out loud. Write something down. Draw. Look at a picture or put on a song that gets closer to it. Maybe the answer is just a sensation, an urge, or the caring thing you do next. A quiet verbal mind doesn't mean you're supposed to compensate with vivid imagery. Sometimes quiet is just quiet; it isn't proof of resistance.

Don't worry too much about whether the feeling or the words came first, either. It changes from moment to moment. A feeling can stay wordless until words would actually help you understand what happened, compare the possible explanations, or decide what to do. Thinking about it later isn't automatically avoidance, and getting there before the words doesn't make the feeling more true.

## Maybe Calm the Body First

Sometimes the problem isn't words at all. You're just too tense to get anywhere with the conversation.

If your solar plexus, abdomen, or somewhere else feels clenched and it's hard to relax into that heart-chakra love, try a slower, non-forced exhale or some gentle massage. I love using one foot to massage the other like a Yin/Yang symbol. Circular or spiral massage over the tense area can be nice too.

Sometimes you need more bottom-up help than that: Shaking, tapping (Emotional Freedom Technique), EMDR, Brainspotting, Somatic Experiencing, Water Therapy, Massage, Energy Healing, or another approach that works from the body upward.

Order matters. If the body is still freaking out, conversation can wait. That's what the roadmap below is for.

If you close your eyes and start losing the actual room, open them again. Look around. Know where you are and what time it is. Feel your body here. If these sessions leave you more disoriented, more compelled, or less able to function afterward, shorten them and get some grounded support instead of trying to win a contest with your own nervous system.

### Catch the Hook Before the Story Takes Over

You don't have to know yet whether it's the child, a protector, or something old. Catch the hook first. The jaw tightens. Your belly contracts. Heat comes up. Something in you pulls back. Sometimes you only realize you're hooked because half the text is already written or you're reaching for a drink. Maybe you've started scrolling, overexplaining, attacking, disappearing, making a promise, or deciding what the whole relationship means. [Pema Chödrön](https://pemachodronfoundation.org/product/taking-the-leap-book/) uses the Tibetan word *shenpa* for that tightening and urge before the whole story has finished forming.

Look around and say, “I'm hooked.” Unless somebody needs protection right now, give yourself one to three breaths before doing whatever you're about to do. Don't make the feeling prove anything yet. What can the adult do in the next minute—give the kid a little warmth, protect somebody, or choose one next step? Do only that. If even that is too much, let the charge settle and get on with your day without acting on it yet.

A lot can wait ten minutes. Leave the text unsent. Put the drink down. Don't blurt out the thing, make the promise, or decide the whole relationship yet. Whether you're right can wait too. Sometimes something real is happening now and old stuff is all through it; sometimes you've read the situation wrong.

When you've come down a bit, look again. Maybe somebody really crossed a line and it also hit an old state or protector. There may be a boundary here, grief, something to repair, or some care you never got. Start with what actually happened. “My stomach tightened” is something you noticed. “That person is evil” is a judgment. What did they do? What supports that conclusion, what doesn't, and what else could explain it? If there were literal words in your head at the time, those were part of the event. Words you find afterward can still help, but they're the later translation.

One breath can be enough. If turning inward makes you less oriented, less able to function, or more compelled, stop and get support from outside yourself.

### Don't Assume It's All You

Inner-child work can make you start explaining every conflict as your wound. Don't. Sometimes the other person is actually doing something that needs to be dealt with. Before digging for another childhood explanation, look at what happens when you disagree. Can they look at what they did, take responsibility when they need to, and make room for your side? Or do you keep ending up carrying their feelings while your own position disappears?

[Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity shifts the question away from endlessly guessing motives and back to what somebody repeatedly shows they can do. Sometimes you've already explained enough.

One bad conversation doesn't diagnose anybody. People can disagree with you, need time, or set a boundary of their own. Look at what keeps happening. You may never know whether they can't respond differently, won't, or simply couldn't that day, but you can still know what you cannot rely on today. If their behavior changes, update your judgment. Another promise by itself isn't the change, and you don't need to keep getting hurt just to collect enough evidence to protect yourself.

Guilt doesn't settle it. You may really have hurt somebody and need to repair that while also abandoning your own position because you need their disappointment to stop.

Before the next conversation, decide what would count as enough. Mutual understanding would be nice, but maybe all that is actually available is getting one thing across, saying no, or ending the exchange without forty minutes of defending yourself. You don't have to make somebody understand before you act. Let the Witness keep track of what's happening inside you while the Protector watches what's actually happening between you; then the Guide can decide what to do next.

### Write It. Don't Send It Yet.

Write the response you actually want to give—or just say it in your head. Don't send it yet.

Now read it as somebody who is on the writer's side and still wants to be fair. Fix what is unfair or confusing, but don't erase a real protest, request, or boundary just because it came out angry. If some responsibility really is yours, keep that too. Better doesn't necessarily mean nicer. Maybe the final answer is shorter. Maybe you wait, say less, or don't respond. “I need some time. I'll give you a fuller response tomorrow” is fine if you can actually keep that promise. If protection is needed now, don't postpone it to finish the exercise.

Then go back to the first draft and stop editing. What was the person who wrote it trying so hard to get somebody to understand? Did they need protection, reassurance, or actual help? The first version isn't automatically childish just because it was messy or angry. It may contain the most mature protest in the whole exchange. Care for the writer even if you decide not to send what they wrote.

### When Healing Turns Into Checking

You can get hooked on healing too. The wound can be completely real while you're checking it for the hundredth time to see whether you've finally grieved it correctly.

Handle whatever safety issue or practical thing actually needs doing. Then, before another round, ask what changed the last time you processed this. Did you finally feel something you had only been thinking about? Did you notice something new enough to change a decision or how you respond? Or are you asking the same question again because the answer didn't feel certain enough? Grief can come back to familiar ground. Repetition by itself doesn't mean the child has a new request.

If it's checking, let the thought stay there without arguing with it or trying to make it disappear, and go back to the person, activity, or life you chose. You do not have to wait until it feels completely resolved. Caring for the child does not require holding a meeting every time your mind produces a sentence.

Before doing the same exercise again, know what it is supposed to help and what would actually look different if it were working. You can leave a session calmer and full of insight while the original problem doesn't move at all. If the method keeps giving you no useful contact—or mostly makes you monitor yourself more while daily life gets worse—don't answer that by doing more of it. Keep what genuinely helps and try another way of meeting the same need.

If these exercises start feeling like a ritual you have to complete before you're allowed to live, stop there.
