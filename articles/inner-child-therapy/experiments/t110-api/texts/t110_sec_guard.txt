Hear the Guard First
Whichever protective part showed up, try talking to it in the same Nurturer voice you meant for your little one. At some point you can ask that part, “What are you afraid would happen if you stepped back?”
It might say this will just be one more performance, and you’ll fool yourself again. That one can sting, because there could be something to it. Maybe you’ve fooled yourself before, and that part remembers. “Tell me what you remember, I’m listening.” works well here. It puts you into the role of the Nurturer, which alleviates the instinct to argue back. Usually your little one only comes forward after that part feels heard. Peek-a-boo! If you push past it, you might get exactly the performance it was afraid of. Even if you didn’t win an Emmy last year, that can look convincing, with the right words and maybe even tears, while nothing underneath feels any safer.
A harder voice can seem more fitting for something that’s been in your way. I’d still keep the Nurturer voice. If that voice isn’t there in you yet, borrow it from someone who’s been safe for you.
A new roommate doesn’t win you over with a speech on move-in day (hopefully, right?). The rent just has to keep showing up. Your Protector earns that guard’s trust about the same way, mostly out in ordinary life. The things you do can be small, like keeping a promise to yourself nobody else knew about. Or saying no to someone, and not texting an hour later to take it back. That second one can be really hard.
That part doesn’t have to move out. It’s been living there way longer than the grown-up you. It can keep watch while you take one small step toward your little one. I think watching is what it wants anyway, to find out if there’s a grown-up here now who could call it off if things got to be too much. It doesn’t have to be all or nothing, either. Maybe it would settle for a lighter cover for now, like blinds tilted open a crack, and let you peek out for a minute (ST-CA, section 9.3.4). If it says no, I’d listen. When it lets you, keep an eye on that part too, for any change at all.
If it’s been calling all this stupid, it might turn out to be scared of getting its hopes up. I wouldn’t want to get mine up either. I’d tell it something like, “You don’t have to hope yet, I’ll keep coming back even when I mess up.” Sometimes it doesn’t say anything back for a while.
Blaming yourself can be doing a job like that, too. “It’s all my fault” might be your parent’s line, word for word. Then again, if it’s your fault, maybe you can fix it before they leave. I’d stop the cruelty either way. And I’d get curious about what makes it so hard to give up.
