# Before You Try to Go Deep

Sometimes you're so far inside the scared kid that there isn't much adult left to do the exercise. You can still know what you're supposed to do and be completely unable to get enough distance from the feeling to do it. If that's where you are, going deeper can wait. Get some steadiness around you first, and come back when you can pause long enough to choose what happens next.

## You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue

If you don't hear a little kid talking in your head, don't invent one. Actually, this is one place where the usual inner-child prompt can get confusing, because “ask” sounds like you're supposed to ask a question and then wait for a second voice to answer. Maybe that's what happens for some people, but you might not be some people.

You might barely have spontaneous words in your mind and yet still be able to think a sentence on purpose. Try it and see. If words are there, speak or write them. You might feel more comfortable that way than just staying in imagination land.  And if that starts feeling like homework you're doing for a child who isn't answering, drop it. Maybe what's actually requiring your attention now is a knot in your stomach. Or perhaps you're in a place you want to leave. Sometimes nothing “answers” and you just know what would be nice to do for yourself.

And it might not be the same order all the time. You could have a feeling before thoughts, or thoughts might come first and provoke a feeling. They say hindsight is 20/20, but you actually might not even know which happened first. That's ok, too.

## Maybe Calm the Body First

If your solar plexus, abdomen, or somewhere else feels clenched and it's hard to relax into that heart-chakra love, try a slower, non-forced exhale or some gentle massage. I love using one foot to massage the other like a Yin/Yang symbol. Circular or spiral massage over the tense area can be nice too.

Sometimes you need more bottom-up help than that: Shaking, tapping (Emotional Freedom Technique), EMDR, Brainspotting, Somatic Experiencing, Water Therapy, Massage, Energy Healing, or another approach that works from the body upward.

Sometimes the words are all there and they're doing absolutely nothing. You understand the problem, you can explain it, and the body is still clenched exactly where it was. That's when I would stay with the body-first work in the roadmap below for a while instead of trying to talk harder.

And if you finish the session worse than you started—less present, functioning worse, or stuck with an urge that is harder to leave alone—make the next attempt smaller or get some grounded help.

### Catch the Hook Before the Story Takes Over

You may notice the hook before you have any idea whether it's the child, a protector, or something old. Sometimes you notice embarrassingly late: the jaw is tight, the stomach is clenched, and meanwhile half the text is already written. Or you're reaching for a drink, disappearing into scrolling, explaining the entire relationship to yourself—maybe your version is attacking, vanishing, making a promise, or deciding what the whole relationship means. [Pema Chödrön](https://pemachodronfoundation.org/product/taking-the-leap-book/) calls this tightening and urge before the story has finished forming *shenpa*.

Once you catch it, look around and say, “I'm hooked.” Unless somebody needs protection right now, give yourself one to three breaths before doing the thing you were already halfway into doing. You don't need those breaths to tell you whether you're right. See whether there's one adult move available—some warmth for the kid, something protective, enough direction to keep you from making the next decision while completely flooded. If there isn't, waiting counts.

For ten minutes, the text can stay unsent. The drink, the promise, the relationship verdict, the thing you desperately want to blurt out can wait with it. If something is actually unsafe, deal with the unsafe thing.

Later, come back to a much less exciting question: what actually happened? Start with the other person's behavior. You can be genuinely triggered by old material and still have a real grievance in the present. Maybe there is a boundary to set now; maybe the older part of it is grief, something unfinished, or care you never got.

“My stomach tightened” is something you noticed. “That person is evil” is already an interpretation. What did they actually do? Does the conclusion really fit that, or did your mind make a jump? If literal words were in your head at the time, those belong with what happened too. Words you come up with later can still help you understand it, but they're later words.

One breath is enough if that's all you can manage. If going inward keeps making you less present or less functional, or makes an urge harder to step away from, stop and get support from outside yourself.

### Don't Assume It's All You

If every conflict turns into “this is my wound,” eventually the other person barely exists in the analysis. So look at what actually happens between you.

You tell them something hurt you. They get upset. Somehow the conversation ends with you taking care of their feelings while the thing you originally brought up has disappeared. That doesn't automatically mean they're emotionally immature, but it is something to notice if it keeps happening.

[Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity is useful here because she brings the attention back to what somebody repeatedly shows they can do instead of making you solve their motives forever. One ugly conversation can still just be one ugly conversation. Someone may need time, or be holding a boundary of their own. It starts meaning more when the same ending keeps showing up. Maybe they can't meet you there, maybe they won't; sometimes you really won't know. What you do know is what keeps actually happening. If that changes, your assessment can change too. A promise isn't the change itself, and you don't have to collect another injury just to be sure.

Guilt can make this especially confusing because two things can be true at once. Maybe you really hurt somebody and owe them an apology. And maybe you're also dropping your own position because you can't bear their disappointment. Feeling guilty doesn't decide which parts are true, and it doesn't prove they're manipulating you either.

Then there's the part where you need them to understand. You explain it one more time because if they could finally see what you mean, maybe everything would settle. Sometimes they still don't. Before going back into the conversation, it helps to know what actually has to happen there. Maybe one thing needs to be said, or maybe you need to say no and leave. The Witness can stay with what's happening inside you while the Protector watches what's actually happening between you, and the Guide can deal with the next move instead of trying to win the whole case.

### Write It. Don't Send It Yet.

Write the response exactly as it comes—or just say it in your head. Don't send it yet.

Now read it as the adult who's still on the side of the person who wrote it. Maybe one sentence is cruel, so fix it. Another is angry but true—leave the truth in it. If you really did something wrong, say that too; don't use the apology to erase what hurt, what you're asking for, or what needs to stop. After that there may be three lines worth sending instead of thirty, or nothing that needs to go out yet. If protection is needed now, don't postpone it while you finish the exercise.

Then go back to the first draft and stop editing. You may already have a much better message by now, but the person who wrote the first one hasn't necessarily gotten anything from you yet. What were they trying so hard to make somebody understand? What did they need from the adult in you? Sometimes that ugly draft is the first place where “this hurt” or “this needs to stop” showed up without immediately getting talked out of existence.

### When Healing Turns Into Checking

You can get hooked on healing too. The wound can be completely real while you're checking for the hundredth time whether you've finally grieved it correctly, then wondering whether the fact that you're checking means you still haven't done enough healing.

Take care of any real safety issue or practical problem first. After that, think about the last round before starting another one. Did anything actually follow you back into life? Maybe something that had only been an idea finally became emotionally real and you behaved differently afterward. Maybe you saw something that changed a decision. Or maybe you had another intense, insightful session and came back to the same question because the answer still didn't feel certain enough. Grief can revisit the same ground, so repetition by itself doesn't settle which one is happening.

If it is checking, leave the thought unfinished and go back to the person, activity, or life you chose. The thought can still be there while you go eat dinner; it doesn't have to be settled first. Caring for the child doesn't require a meeting every time the mind says something.

Sometimes the strangest version is when the session feels profound and the original problem is still sitting there afterward. You come out calmer, full of insight, convinced you got somewhere—and then notice that nothing you were trying to change is actually changing. Or you're getting better at monitoring yourself while daily life is getting worse. At that point, doing the same exercise harder isn't automatically more healing. Keep whatever genuinely helps, but try another way of meeting the need; if the exercise itself has become something you have to complete before you can get on with the day, that's part of the problem too.
