# Repeats report: Sometimes There Isn't a Clear Child Yet

**Scope.** The prompt says to report only repeats and contradictions that involve at least one block in the h1 Sometimes There Isn't a Clear Child Yet (B206–B229). It also says to check the rest of the article against itself. I checked every block in the h1 against every other block there and against the whole article. I used the check of the rest of the article to find every earlier instance of a point that also appears in the h1. Repeats and contradictions that lie wholly outside B206–B229 are not listed.

The h1 has an opening (B207–B208) and two h2s: From Survival to Experimental Play (B209–B215) and Untangling the Belonging Bargains (B216–B229, which includes the h3 Four Pairs That Commonly Hook the Child from B220). The study chart (B28) previews this h1 in its panel “no clear child yet”. I treated that as the article's map, not as a repeat.

## 1. Repeats

### 1. B32, B207, B208, B211, B213: not knowing what you want on your own

Where: B32 in Don’t Give the Inner Adult Away; B207–B208 in the h1's opening; B211 and B213 in From Survival to Experimental Play.

- B32: “Now you're only just finding out what you actually think, maybe even learning to say no.”
- B207: “the real one may never have had room to form”
- B208: “"What do I believe?" or "What do I want?" has no clear answer of your own”
- B211: “It might feel wrong somehow to like something before you've checked anyone's face.”
- B213: “That's assuming the self that didn't get much practice is easy to find, though.” and “you might not know what you'd choose without the fear of disappointing somebody”

**Shared point:** your own wants and beliefs may be hard to find, because they grew around other people's reactions.

**Adds or repeats:** B213 adds that your preferences and beliefs shift with whoever is around, and that a guessed feeling isn't proof there's no little one. But it opens by raising, as if for the first time, the doubt the h1 opens with (B207–B208). Its “fear of disappointing somebody” is B211's “checked anyone's face” again. A reader would feel told the same thing again. B32 uses the same situation for a different point (keeping the right to say no to a teacher or therapist), so there the overlap is the situation, not the advice.

**Better:** B207–B208. They state it as the premise of the whole h1; B213 raises it again a few paragraphs later.

### 2. B62, B210, B215: who you are can stay open while you sort out what's yours

Where: B62 in Catch the Hook Before the Story Takes Over; B210 and B215 in From Survival to Experimental Play.

- B62: “Give yourself time to see what patterns form more durably, and what feels authentically yours vs something planted or manipulated into being there”
- B210: “People grow into who they are by trying things on and then changing their minds”
- B215: “Any words you put to them can stay tentative” and “long enough for conditioning to quiet down and real preferences to surface”

**Shared point:** take your time, try things and revise, and tell what's really yours from what family, religion and society put there.

**Adds or repeats:** the h1 adds where the planted part comes from (needing to belong) and ways to find what's yours (Focusing, journaling, distance). A reader who remembers B62 would recognize its one sentence spread over B210 and B215. Because the h1 adds methods, much of it still reads as new.

**Better:** B215. The h1 is the section built for this and gives a way to do it; in B62 it's a side step inside the section on getting hooked.

### 3. B74, B215, B228: stepping back from the people you've been performing for

Where: B74 in Also Look Outward; B215 in From Survival to Experimental Play; B228 in Untangling the Belonging Bargains.

- B74: “you're still keeping up a fake normal, happy version of yourself to hold onto it” and “I'd either bring more of the truth in, carefully, or let it be less deep for now”
- B215: “Some people also benefit from temporary, intentional distance from whoever they've been playing the part for”
- B228: “If a family, group or relationship won't let you do this while you're still part of it, temporary distance may be necessary.”

**Shared point:** when keeping a relationship or group means performing a self, stepping back for a while is an option.

**Adds or repeats:** B215 adds a purpose (letting conditioning quiet down so real preferences surface). B228 adds when it's needed (they won't let you be your own person while you're inside) and what can follow (coming back on new terms, or leaving for good). Inside the h1, a reader still gets the temporary-distance advice twice, once in each h2. B74 is about a relationship that's getting deeper rather than a family or group, so it's the instance least likely to be noticed.

**Better:** B228. It says when distance is needed and what can come after; B215's is a one-line aside.

### 4. B167, B207, B208, B210, B219: as a child you hid or traded away your real self to keep belonging

Where: B167 in The Parent You Inherited; B207–B208 in the h1's opening; B210 in From Survival to Experimental Play; B219 in Untangling the Belonging Bargains.

- B167: “your little one learned bit by bit to hide that part of their authentic self which wasn't allowed, so they could keep belonging”
- B207: “if you grew up somewhere you only belonged as long as you played somebody else”
- B208: “Back then, asking might have gotten you in trouble, or laughed at.”
- B210: “when curiosity or mistakes got you punished, or it felt like they loved you less, you got better at the part”
- B219: “Your little one, with nobody parenting it, takes those bargains to survive, because belonging feels like life or death.”

**Shared point:** a child hides or swaps out its real self when belonging depends on it.

**Adds or repeats:** B207 goes further than B167 (the real self may never have formed, not just been hidden). B210 adds how it happened (attention used up reading danger) and what got lost (room to experiment). B219 adds that belonging feels like survival and puts it in terms of the bargain. Still, B210's first sentence tells B207–B208 again right after the first h2 heading, and B219 tells it again in the second h2. A reader would feel told the same thing again.

**Better:** B210. It shows how it happens and leads into what was missed; B167 is brief and sits in a section about the inherited critic.

### 5. B211, B214: the first step is getting your own preferences back

Where: both in From Survival to Experimental Play.

- B211: “if your little one isn't easy to feel yet, making it safe like that is the first phase”
- B214: “So early on, the practice is finding out who's actually in there” and “getting back the preferences that got buried”

**Shared point:** when the child isn't clear yet, the first thing is to let your own preferences come back.

**Adds or repeats:** B214 adds concrete practices (long reflection with no outside input, journaling without picturing a reader, time alone in nature, the foods you really like). But a reader is told twice, a few paragraphs apart, what comes first, and the second time doesn't refer to the first. B211 makes the first step safety for curiosity and experimenting; B214 makes it reflection and time alone.

**Better:** B214. It says what to actually do.

### 6. B219, B224: the good things are fine; trouble starts when your worth hangs on them

Where: both in Untangling the Belonging Bargains (B224 under its h3).

- B219: “These can all be real gifts. The bargain begins when your worth depends on them”
- B224: “You don't have to stop enjoying pleasure, praise, gain or belonging. The question is whether losing one of them would feel like losing yourself.”

**Shared point:** having and enjoying these things is fine; the problem is when your worth or sense of self depends on them.

**Adds or repeats:** B224 turns it into a test (would losing one feel like losing yourself?) and applies it to Pema Chödrön's four pairs. Otherwise the same line is drawn twice in one h2, a few paragraphs apart. B217 already draws a similar line once, for going along with a group (“Most groups expect some conformity, which is ordinary.”).

**Better:** B224. It gives the reader a test to apply, right after the questions it belongs with.

### 7. B225, B227: you can question and disagree without leaving or loving anyone less

Where: both in Untangling the Belonging Bargains, under its h3.

- B225: “As a differentiated adult you can stay deeply embedded, free to question things or disagree.”
- B227: “Questioning what you were programmed to believe while holding the discomfort, and without having to stop loving anyone over it.”

**Shared point:** staying close and questioning can go together.

**Adds or repeats:** the bullet adds “holding the discomfort”; otherwise it repeats B225 a few lines later. Mild, since B226 introduces the list as day-to-day examples of what B225 describes.

## 2. Contradictions

### 1. B87 and B228: does this work end, or go on for life?

Where: B87 in When Healing Turns Into Checking; B228 in Untangling the Belonging Bargains.

- B87: “Real healing is ultimately measured by reduced need for the therapy, just like real growing up is not something you do forever.”
- B228: “Differentiation is slow, and it goes on your whole life.”

**Point in conflict:** B87 says real healing shows up as needing the work less, the way growing up ends. B228 says differentiation, which this h1 treats as part of building who you are alongside reparenting, goes on for life.

**Does the article say why:** no. A reader could square them if differentiation counts as ordinary living rather than as the therapy, but the article doesn't say which it is.

**Which sits more naturally:** B87, which matches the article's other warnings against endless work on yourself, such as B20 (“Spending your whole life processing your wounded inner child isn't freedom either.”). B104 (“The quest for self-knowledge is a life's journey”) leans toward B228, so the article holds both.

### 2. B73 and B213–B215: reach out now, or spend the early phase apart?

Where: B73 in Also Look Outward; B213–B215 in From Survival to Experimental Play.

- B73: “Do you have to get yourself sorted out first, so you'll be better company? I really don't think so.”
- B213: “something like "lonely, probably?"”
- B214: “So early on, the practice is finding out who's actually in there, through long reflection with no outside input” and “Time alone in nature helps too”
- B215: “temporary, intentional distance”

**Point in conflict:** B73 says not to wait until you've sorted yourself out, and to reach toward people now, because when the hurt was being alone, waiting means more of being alone. B214–B215 set the early phase apart from other people: reflection with no outside input, time alone, and distance from the people you've performed for.

**Does the article say why:** only partly. Each passage has its own condition (B73: when being alone was the hurt; B214: when your self formed around other people's cues). But the h1's own example of what the little one feels is “lonely, probably?”, so the same reader can fit both, and the article doesn't say which comes first then.

**Which sits more naturally:** B73, which states its condition outright; B214 doesn't say how its time alone applies when loneliness is part of the hurt.
