## The part to read

[0] # Understanding Reparenting

[1] Welcome, lovey bunnies!

[2] Here's the idea behind Inner Child Reparenting, in a nutshell:

[3] Growing up, all of us picked up automatic ways of understanding situations we find ourselves in, reacting based on those understandings, and then treating ourselves partly based on how we happened to handle things. Some of those patterns are still wise in general, although even then, we are always learning from what life throws at us. Others of those patterns were built for a crisis that's long over, and some just seem to make every situation worse.

[4] Reparenting means consciously evolving and molding a better Inner Adult inside you, comprising a loving Nurturer, a safe Adult Protector (hereafter just the Protector) and a wise Guide. This chosen Inner Adult super hero gets into a real, loving relationship with the little one you still carry. That grown-up notices the old patterns and practices better ways of responding. At first, it's deliberate and might feel difficult. Many struggle to get past the silly, awkward feeling of it. Over time, instead of shying away from the silly, we can use the silly feeling in the work, to make it more playful. I call it pl/ork (play + work)! With time, the adult/child roles become second nature, so you spend less of your life managing yourself and more of it living with purpose, loving, relaxing, and playing.

[5] If that got to you at all, good. That was half the idea. I used to listen to Suzanne Vega's The Queen and the Soldier every night because it reliably got the tears out.

[6] The crying helped me a lot in terms of not going numb inside. It didn't help me develop insight, though. I could have kept crying forever. It was Buddhism and then inner child therapy which helped create a new perspective that was more healthy. But I'm really happy I did do the crying that was needed at the time.

[7] Music can help with feeling love, not just sadness, although sadness is often the first step to open up. So if you sit down to try any of this and feel nothing, that's when I'd put a song on. Céline's right up there if you don't have one, and Rosey Grier's It's All Right to Cry is a little further down. My sister used to sing that one to me almost daily in such a silly way. It was really sweet of her.

[8] Some people, like another ex of mine (X), got so deep into crying that it became traumatizing. She actually had panic attacks from it. If you start getting scared of your own crying, turn the song off for tonight.

[9] ## The Chicken-and-Egg Problem

[10] Céline's song is called On ne change pas – "we don't change." I think that's both fortunately, and unfortunately, true for most of us. Fortunately in the sense that the sweet, innocent, curious, vivacious little one is still inside us, even if hiding. Unfortunate in the sense that we often don't truly develop the adult qualities that we might pretend to have, so we continue burdening that little one inside us with troubles they aren't made for handling. This shows up in how we may feel abandoned, helpless, impulsive, frightened, or desperate to be chosen.

[11] And I realized that it doesn't make sense to ask the child to "heal itself," which is really a disguised request for the child to abandon its own nature and turn into an adult. I call that "forced adulthood," where the child has to deal with situations the evolved inner adult should be handling--we see this in actual children all the time when their parents are not providing care. People even wear t-shirts saying, "I don't wanna adult today." The forced adulting often goes along with a misperception that there is something wrong with the inner child. Instead, we need to separately develop the qualities of a good inner parent for that rightly vulnerable child: the loving Nurturer, safe Protector, and wise Guide.

[12] That's one grown-up doing three jobs, not three more people moving in to help with the rent, and each job keeps the others in balance. Say a party gets to be too much for your little one. The Protector gets you both out of there, and on the way home the Nurturer tells them they did great, even though mostly they hid in the bathroom. And the Guide helps them think twice about the next weird party they might go to, avoiding another cycle of bathroom vacationing. 🙈

[13] The Guide is the one people misunderstand the most, because a lot of the bad parenting or misdirection we originally received was likely lacking in Protector and Nurturer functions, while over-relying on harsh guidance. So I left it out of the first version of this framework until my friend Key pointed it out as crucial. Without a healed Inner Guide, our lives can become small and like a never-ending therapy session when we focus only on caring for ourselves rather than giving ourselves a mission as well. That's the Guide's job. But on the other hand, some people over-rely on their discipline or their mission to avoid caring for themselves. And many folks try to find an ideal guide via some kind of guru-led political or religious cult.

[14] If the phrase "inner parent" calls up the wrong archetype for you, pick another one. Your future self might work, or the captain of a ship, so long as they'd do those three jobs.

[15] But here's the chicken-and-egg problem. What if you're so far inside the child-state that you can't become the adult? In this case, you sit there trying to be loving while your mind is becoming controlled again by the inherited critic voice, or you start going numb, throwing a fit, or panicking.

[16] Sometimes somebody else may have to do one part of the adult role with you or for you first. You get to feel what that is like and watch how they do it. Maybe you copy one small thing you've seen them do, or imagine them there when they're not, and try one peace yourself while they're still helping with the rest. That's what I mean by borrowed adulthood. You may still need help with almost all of it for a while, but over time there are more and more of those little parts you can do yourself.

[17] ## Five Stages of Reparenting

[18] For now, I think of the whole path in five progressive stages. You might be starting at any point here (and it may change even from hour to hour as well):

[19] Forced adulthood: You're still stuck identifying as the lost inner child, forced frequently into handling situations that require more maturity, even if it looks like you've got it all together. At this stage, use your childlike superpowers to come into the "Wow of Now" (have you seen the movie Waking Life?), and make sure there's enough safety around you to continue with reparenting. 2. Begin to borrow adulthood – remember watching Mr. Rogers or Sesame Street? Those adult role models are what we're looking for at this stage. This can almost feel like playing house as a kid where you imagined how you'd take care of a baby, etc. 3. Practice being the adult – do at least one part of caring for yourself while support is still there from the previous step. 4. Adult mode itself – after sufficient practice with reparenting, you become a natural at sending love to little you, protecting them, and shepherding them. 5. Adult and child in relationship – the adult leads. The child isn't pushed away, but doesn't have to run the whole life alone. This can actually feel really weird if you've been stuck in forced adulthood for a while. Our goal is to go from weird to silly and playful. Lose self-consciousness while gaining self-awareness, if that makes sense!

[20] Whichever stage you're at, saying the right thing when you sit down with your little one helps, but you also have to start actually doing some of it in the rest of your life. Depending on your maladaptive patterns, improvements could look opposite to each other. If you're a workaholic normally, then your little one might start believing in grown-up you when they catch you lying down because you're wiped out, instead of pushing through one more chore. Or the opposite, when you stop procrastinating so much and clean your room, then they start believing in you.

[21] Reparenting isn't an exercise you finish, even if you make excellent progress. You might feel like you've mastered the adult/child inner relationship, and then it's Friday night and your boss asks you to stay late again. You get up the nerve to tell them no, and go home feeling okay about it, and nobody had to cry on a meditation cushion for that to happen.

[22] ## My Journey

[23] I used to preach Buddhist enlightenment too much, because that's what helped me out of a long existential crisis.

[24] From what I've seen, most people who chase spiritual freedom, as valid as that is, are, at least at first, trying to get away from something painful in themselves, usually childhood trauma of some kind. That's spiritual bypassing. Plenty of gurus do it too; I've written about the Dalai Lama as one example.

[25] The way I think this works is simple. Imagine you're broke and starving, and your baby is starving too. Understandable: there is no food. Then a food bank opens nearby and suddenly you've got lots of nutritious munchies. But you're eating all of it yourself while baby you is still starving. *Unforgivable*.

[26] Nobody leaves a baby starving on purpose. I just didn't believe there was a baby. Inner-child stuff sounded silly to me until I could actually feel him. Then there was this little me actually hurting, and I could send some of the love I'd already found his way.

[27] Spending your whole life processing your wounded inner child isn't freedom either. Spirituality opened a much bigger horizon for me – love and happiness beyond the conditions of my life story. Maybe ordinary life already gives many folks enough love and meaning. It didn't for me. Conditional love felt radically insufficient, so I kept looking for something deeper.

[28] I was well-loved as a child, but from age 12 to 26, I was depressed, and cried every night from feeling the pain of the world. Loving-kindness (metta) meditation healed that depression almost instantly for me, so it became my regular practice automatically. I later found out that many people weren't able to do a deep metta meditation, because they literally didn't know what love felt like, as they hadn't received it as a child. My 2nd wife told me she didn't know the word for love in her native language, because her mom never said that word to her.

[29] But even though my depression was gone, the sending-love-outward metta practice didn't reach everything in me. An old childhood wound could still hit, like if someone accused me of being lazy, and then suddenly it's like I was that same little kid again, even tho the spiritual part of me felt free as a bird. And a lot of the childhood stuff around my attachment to girls/women was still there.

[30] At some point I guess I heard folks talking about inner child therapy, and being curious about different healing approaches, I thought I'd give it a go even if I wasn't the prime candidate. It felt like I was making it up at first, which seems quite common. That didn't stop me from occasionally practicing, but I did stop "pushing" it to be a certain way, and I'd stop trying to feel it was realer than it was. I would go more into listening and waiting mode rather than imagining mode.

[31] At one pivotal point, I used San Pedro cactus and CBD tea with sublingual mapacho, and that cracked it open. I had a full-on audio-visual communion with my little Joelito and I heard a few things he was still sad about from so long ago. Things which most people would laugh at, because they're seemingly so minor. Like when I hit my neighbor while riding my bicycle, and even after I profusely apologized, her mom said, "Don't you have any common sense???" And I found out why I was always pushing myself to help others. It was because when I was 5yrs old, at the Zendik community in Texas that we were visiting, they held a meeting with everyone there (like 50 people) and me in the center. I was critiqued for not offering to help anyone I saw who was working. Well, sorry! I never knew I was supposed to. That's a pattern that has stuck, although it does help to remember what caused it, so I don't take it so seriously.

[32] Years later, I found a warm, intuitive inner child therapist, named Daja, randomly (if you believe anything is random), as a friend of a friend, while in Kenya. I figured I already knew this stuff, but I gave it a shot. She did actually help me see some things more clearly. For example, she had me write down how many people I could fully trust as a child (lots), then how many people I could fully trust as an adult (zero). That was difficult, and it's actually the opposite of what you might expect from inner-child therapy, since usually that's for people who had problems trusting as a child.

[33] What it showed me is that I was ignoring a lot of things that were really important to look at, and that was one of them. I wouldn't have even thought to ask the question she asked me if I were doing it on my own. It's also an example of how therapy can sometimes make you feel worse than before, when you become more aware of problems. But personally I prefer to see things as they are, so I can figure out how to work with them, rather than just hide in delusions.

[34] It also showed me that I really do need to count more on myself than I assumed. I need to be much better as an inner adult for my inner child, because my childhood was actually so nice that I kind of just assume I can trust everyone, and that gets me into trouble! So it got me curious to learn more about inner-child therapy, which is a process I'm still in the midst of as I test this guide with people.

[36] # Safety and Readiness

[37] ## Dip Your Toes In Before You Go Deep

[38] How much of the grown-up you is actually here right now? Check if you can pause before you act. Are you following through after making decisions? If you can't do those things because you're basically still stuck identifying as the frightened kid, then let's leave the deeper conversation until later. Do whatever helps you come back a bit before you try to talk to little you. And have someone steady there to support you if possible.

[39] And if the grown-up you is there, and it's just how deep you're going that's too much, dip your toes in the kiddie pool first. Like the flight attendants say, it's good to know where the exits are. 😅 So can you stop in the middle and come back to the room? If you can't, or going in at all keeps making it worse, I'd come all the way back out first. Some people try one technique after another instead, to prove to themselves they can take it. Your little one may have had to just take it back then, though. Now there's a grown-up around who can say, "Okay, that's enough for today," and try something gentler, like saying hi to them with your eyes open.

[40] If you need a break from the communion, simply jot down the reason and the progress made so far. Then come back later, once that reason has changed and you feel comfortable.

[41] If being alone was a big part of what hurt during your childhood, I'd call somebody now, mess and all, since reaching toward people is part of taking care of your little one too. Even if you don't have someone you know who wants to listen, there are warmlines for this (not hotlines unless it's an emergency).

[42] ## Your Body Might Need Some Love First

[43] If you notice tension in your solar plexus or abdomen, or anywhere else that seems to be keeping you from relaxing into that heart chakra love, try some non-forced, slower exhales and a little gentle self-massage. I love using one foot to massage the other foot like a yin-yang symbol, as well as gentle circular or varied directional massage in the solar plexus, neck, head, abdomen, or whatever spot is feeling tense.

[44] I'd also recommend checking into deeper somatic therapies like Shaking, tapping (EFT), EMDR, Brainspotting, Somatic Experiencing, Water Therapy, Massage, Energy Healing, or some other bottom-up approach.

[46] ### When Therapy Stirs Things Up

[47] Some somatic work or reparenting therapy can feel pleasant, and some can feel like a raging storm got unleashed. It can bring up grief and fear, and sometimes nightmares later on as the memories begin to unwind. It's a bit like going to the gym. You want to make gains, and sore muscles the next day can be good, but proper range of (e)motion here is also key to avoid injury.

[48] Keep track of how you feel over the next few days, like how refreshing your sleep is, and how easy it feels to get through your day. Do you want to go back toward what came up, or is some part of you saying no? If you can't quite tell yet what feels different, I'd just make the next session simpler, one change at a time, like a science nerd, so you learn what made the difference. You can play around with variables like shorter session length, or shallower diving, time of day, keeping eyes open, having a friend around, or holding a teddy bear as a stand-in for your little one.

[49] And what about those hard dreams, or images that pop up during a session? They can show what feels important or frightening right now. Sometimes they show what feels missing, or what feels needed. Although sometimes dreams can show you real memories and even predict the future on occasion, we need to be careful not to assume that they are doing that each time.

[50] ### Notice the Part That Came Here

[51] Some people hear all this about loving themself and answer, "No. I hate myself." Like my friend Malia, who told me, "My inner child is just capricious! What's to love?" If that's what you find, just listen to it, without arguing back. But if you opened this article, something in you wanted things to be different, whatever the rest of you was saying. The same goes if you came to therapy or asked someone for help.

[52] It might have been hope, or desperation. Maybe you were curious, or scared. Some people come here out of duty (my sister kept telling me to see therapists; she says everyone needs therapy). Or it was more like, "I'm not going to keep suffering like this." And maybe it was love. Ask that part what it hoped might happen when it brought you here, and follow that thread.

[53] ## When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child

[54] I've been writing this whole guide as if everybody who tries it wants to take care of the inner child. Some people get a little rush out of making somebody scared or small, though. For such folks, inner child therapy is clearly not the place to start. They need to first deconstruct the adult identity they've got.

[55] When I talk with folks like that, I mostly want to know what the identity gets them and what it costs, and especially what it's really serving. Some of them even call themselves "sovereign," yet somehow there's an urge they have to obey, which sounds more like slavery to me.

[56] This part tends to scare the wrong people, like somebody who's horrified by their own thoughts. Everybody gets scary thoughts they'd rather not have sometimes, and they don't make you a bad person, but why would you bring your inner child into the middle of them? The nice thing about inner child therapy is that you can wait until you're calm and actually ready. A real parent doesn't get to do that, which is one reason nobody manages to get a perfect 4.0 GPA on the parent report-card their children bring home. 😛

[57] And if somebody actually wonders whether they're malicious, they'd do better to meditate on it and find out why they're asking before they bring their inner child anywhere near it. Are they really feeling malicious, or is it some voice in their head saying so, like the inherited critic? If some of it turns out to be malice they actually want to act on, the inner child can wait.

[58] ### Ill Will and Loving-Kindness

[59] In one of the Buddha's own accounts of how he woke up from identity, ill will shows up in his mind and he sees it's hurting him. If he had to figure that out by watching, it's not obvious. Ill will can make you feel powerful. Being on fire would too, and people would definitely get out of your way, but unless you're the Human Torch from the Fantastic Four, you'd be the one getting burnt first. Has it ever left anybody at ease, with nothing to regret afterward?

[60] The Buddha also told his son, Rahula, that loving-kindness is what gets rid of ill will, and compassion does the same for cruelty. I'm biased on loving-kindness, since it got me out of my depression. For these folks it starts with the grown-up they are now, though, and their little one stays out of it.

[61] As for when their little one gets a turn, I'd say once they've had some bad days where scaring somebody would've felt good, and they didn't do it. Until then it helps a lot to have a real person in their life who likes them for the good they see deep down, and won't laugh along with the mask persona, since loving-kindness is easier to pick up from someone who already has some.

[62] ## If You Want to Kill Yourself

[63] Maybe you're reading this about to act, or with the means close by. Then before anything else, get another human being involved, and put some distance between you and the means. A crisis line counts, and the person on the other end can be a total stranger.

[64] As for the internal psychology of it, when the part that wants to die feels like all of you, go ahead and notice who's doing the noticing (more on that in Noticing Counts, further on), or borrow an adult from the adult lending library in your head :).

[65] ### The Spiritual Side of the Question

[66] I try not to proselytize about spirituality, because I've made that mistake before, and I now see that it's better to let folks find it when they are ready. But when someone tells me they want to kill themself, I consider the topic to be brutally opened by default, regardless of whether they meant to do that, because if they actually do kill their body (which they might mistake to be their self), that's a very powerful spiritual challenge right there, even if they didn't realize it would be.

[67] Then ask: what is the self you want to kill? Is that self the same thing as your body, and if yes, why do you think so? Do you have the same body in dreams? What exactly do you expect your body's death to accomplish? If the problem is a state of mind, like self-hatred or despair, shame or loneliness, what makes you so sure that destroying the body ends it?

[68] In Buddhism, death isn't an off switch, and it isn't a free new-age get-out-of-jail-free card back to the Divine Source. Repeated thought and intention shape the mind, and that keeps going through death and rebirth. Yes, that can include hell realms (and hell can be right here on Earth, also). A human birth is a rare chance to understand suffering and end its causes.

[69] You don't have to believe that, but if you're still thinking of staking your life on the opposite claim, that death is just nothing, might that be a double standard to look into? Curiosity killed the cat, you might say, so research could be dangerous. 😅 But despite the risk of curiosity, researchers at the University of Virginia have been busy little bees, collecting cases for 60 YEARS now, of young kids who talk about a past life, and there's a big pile of near-death accounts too. Even if every case is different, I'd read a few of those cases before betting my life on "nothing."

[70] Right here in this one life, it's apparent that the more you hate yourself, the better your mind gets at it, and being alone tends to deepen the despair. But lucky us, because physical life keeps handing out redirections, like that guy who found me in a random park crying one time, and asked me "What's wrong?" If the many traditions and the accumulating scientific evidence are right that the mind's causal process goes on after the body dies, killing the body may take away some of those redirections and leave the suffering right where it was. That sounds like a substantially worse deal to me.

[71] My father used to tell suicidal people, "Oh, you want to kill yourself? That's wonderful! Now you can change what you've always been too afraid to change in your life, and then see if you still want to end it!" So, what would you tell my dad? What's the change you still can't bring yourself to make? Leaving a relationship or quitting a job? Telling your family who you really are? Those might sound difficult, but they're actually considerably easier and more likely to help than dying.

[75] ## Borrow the Adult To Become the Adult

[76] Now, don't tell anyone about this! I'm going to reveal my Magician's Secret. I call it the "borrowed adulthood trick". The new grown-up you're moulding can start outside your head, as someone who likes you just the way you are, or as something you do to protect your little sweetie pie, like letting the phone ring when you know already how that call goes. It may take many such ignored calls before your inner child can start trusting you (and all the while, the parent you inherited calls your efforts fake).

[78] ### Don't Sell Your Inner Adult To Buy An Outer Adult

[79] If you still feel like a kid inside, somebody who seems grown-up can feel like salvation, and letting them do some of the grown-up jobs for you can actually help for a while. But they can know things you don't and still be wrong for you to follow too much. The test is whether the relationship makes you more grounded, truthful, and free.

[80] How do you check that? I'd keep what you feel around them in mind, and look at their track record too. Do they respect a no, or keep pushing on it, and what happens when you disagree? If something outside of them backs up what they're telling you, and it all adds up, that's a good sign.

[81] Even Pema Chödrön (who inspired parts of this guide), who comes from a tradition where devotion to a guru is a big deal, still says a teacher should "wean us from dependency altogether" and help us grow up.

[82] ### Signs of Manipulation

[83] Some gurus really do demand surrender, and they're the opposite of the Guide: they point you at themselves for everything, as if they want to be your God. Others are so professional that they don't even need to ask anyone to follow them. Someone seems to get your little one like nobody ever has, or treats it like it's special, and it starts thinking, "They know me better than I know myself! Can you believe it?" 🙊 I still remember a Sadhguru video where he said anyone who follows him is an idiot, and his followers were still worshipping him in the comments, proud idiots. 🤪

[84] Here's where I want to clearly depart from the new-age wizdumb that all your problems are self-created: Somebody may really have crossed a boundary. That's not just because they are your karmic partner mirroring what God wants to show you in yourself, even if that also applies sometimes. Your reaction is what you can control the most, though.

[85] Some manipulative people are too smart to act perfect. They lead with their wounds, or with how brutally honest they are about their flaws (very disarming, which is sort of the point), and every warning you hear about them just becomes more proof of how profound they are. Your Protector should be called to step in whenever love bombing hides obvious red flags your more skeptical friends would love to point out if you'd glisten. After all, "I have honesty issues and I'm working on them" may be a perfectly honest thing to say. It's also telling you there's an honesty problem right now, and knowing about a problem isn't fixing it.

[86] Other types of manipulation to check for include: **Gaslighting**, when your perception of reality is slowly giving way to someone else's. Combat this by trusting your perceptions (after documenting or double checking).

[87] In abusive relationships, gaslighting often turns into **D.A.R.V.O.**: Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender. This is how the abuser flips the script on accountability. If you notice this happening, extricate yourself quickly.

[88] **Guilt tripping,** where you're made to feel responsible for things you shouldn't be. Like someone did something for you and then later makes you seem like a bad person because you haven't done enough to give back. Check if the guilt is deserved or not.

[89] **White Knight**: this is when problems are created that you need to be saved from, which can then lead back into guilt-tripping, or dependence. Solve your own challenges rather than letting yourself over-rely on someone as a pattern.

[90] **Triangulation** is where another person is brought in to the conversation so it becomes 2 vs 1. Insist on direct communication with one person if this tactic is employed.

[91] **Silent treatment**: when someone cuts back their emotional involvement to punish or control you (can be confused with simply avoiding situations they aren't able to handle). Don't chase these people, nor reverse it onto them.

[92] **Breadcrumbing** is when silent treatment is reduced just enough to make you hooked on trying to get their attention. Value yourself enough so this doesn't work.

[93] That's a lot of manipulation tactics! And those sneaky sneaks are always coming up with new ones, so it's time we develop our spidey sense. We can also look inward to check if we are using those on ourselves or others.

[94] ### Borrow One Competency at a Time

[95] A complete ideal parent may be impossible to imagine (unless you saw The Brady Bunch). And imagination is pretty hard for adults anyway since it kinda gets stamped out of kids at an early age (along with spirituality), but it's not just a foolish waste of time. It's actually a genius mode unlock. So since the adult is actually a baby when it comes to power to imagine, start small: Borrow one capacity at a time from a figure who embodies it well.

[96] While all of these super powers should be developed alongside each other, the inner child's receptivity will naturally follow this order: first he/she wants to feel safe (Protector), then, building on that safety, feel loved (Nurturer), and then building on the safety & love, trust allows the little one to be eager to take guidance, and that's where Guide-building comes.

[97] ### Noticing Counts

[98] Even before you build up any of these capacities, you can start with simply being present with your little one, which does a lot more than you might think to set the stage for the proper division of identity. Just saying, "Huh, somebody in here is really scared." might be the most adult thing you've got right now, but notice that whoever's noticing isn't all the way inside the scared one.

[99] It can help to use your own name. For me, instead of "I'm terrified," that'd be "Little Joel is terrified. What does Little Joel need right now?" You'll sound a bit like Elmo :) In lab studies, though, people who talked to themselves this way reacted less to upsetting pictures and memories, and their brains didn't seem to be working any harder to do it.

[100] If you can tell your parts apart pretty easily, you've probably got plenty of noticing already. Don't keep drilling it just because the love isn't coming yet. A lot of meditators are great at watching. I was, and baby me was still starving.

[101] ## Protecting and Building Trust With Your Little One

[102] Plenty of people are still at the putting-up-with part of reparenting. Even with little or no warmth for your little one yet, the relationship can start with the Protector's plain acts and the warmth can come after, which feels backwards, because you'd think you have to feel it first before you could do anything for them. But yeah, bonding starts somewhere. And this is not to say you've never done anything to care for yourself. If not, you'd be dead right now. But we're making additional acts of care intentional now as a base for love to grow.

[103] As you may have heard, not every hero wears a cape! Maybe an older sibling protected you well at some point, so they were your hero then, even if they weren't a full adult. So whatever is troubling your inner child right now, start with asking what a minimally competent adult would do in this situation.

[104] ### Talk to the hand!✋🏿

[105] Setting boundaries is an obvious first protector move. "What's a 'boundary'?", I might be asking. That looks like saying no to something that's costing you too much (money, time, health, or whatever resource). Handle that task you've been putting off, or the tax man, or your DUIs in Maryland, even if all you do today is open the envelope.

[106] Maybe it's something seemingly simple, like actually smelling that old food you're about to eat and then trashing it if it smells a little weird (that's one I'm still working on). If you're about to send an angry text, hit pause. If the room itself triggers your spidey sense, leave, or lock the door and get help. Even if this still feels like the child acting like the adult, for now, that's ok. "Fake it til you make it" isn't exactly how I'd phrase it, but yeah. Afterward, you can tell your little one, "I did that for us. That was the Protector." If it sounds funny, laugh! Laughter is the best medicine.

[107] Keep one small promise to your little one. If you promise to clean your room, then do that. Maybe your little one had grown-ups who said sweet things and then did nothing, and maybe you've done it too, so this is how they start to believe you.

[108] ### Making It Intentional

[109] Intentional, meaning instead of waiting for crisis to hit to react, you pick one of those protector acts and decide when you'll do it, and what the first step is. If it needs money or somebody's help, sort that out before the day, and if you'll have to tell someone something they won't like, say it out loud to yourself ahead of time. Then put yourself in their shoes to see how it sounds, and go back & forth like that a couple times until both sides feel good about it, or good enough, as long as what you needed to say is still in there.

[110] After the day, look at what actually happened. Say you did the Protector action(s) and you don't feel any better, or you feel worse. Standing up for yourself can do that at first. Not doing it is harder to figure out. Fear is the first guess. But did you know how to do it, and why? Were you worn out? Maybe whoever said they'd help couldn't make it. 🤗

[111] ### Protecting them from yourself

[112] Even if you mean well, things don't always come out the way you hoped. You can protect your little one from yourself too, like by repairing after an internal attack. "What's an 'internal attack'?" Calling yourself an idiot for losing your keys again, for one. Your little one was listening, so you go back and apologize to them. If they're sarcastic about it, you hear it out instead of snapping back. They don't trust you yet, and you don't ask them to. They complain about something, and if they're right, you say so. All of that is evidence they're accepted and you're safe to be around. Love needs those to start with, but without warmth they can still feel like they're only being put up with.

[113] ### Protecting from the Parent You Inherited

[114] I'd look at the parent "script" you inherited: how you learned to treat yourself from how people treated you growing up. Maybe sometimes that script feels warm, but maybe too often it feels more like a critic, or a warden with rules. The rules can even sound totally reasonable, like "You can come back to the table when you're done with your embarrassing crying fit." If your childhood was like that, your little one learned bit by bit to hide that part of their authentic self which wasn't allowed to show up, so they could keep belonging. And if so, do a double check: they might still be doing it!

[115] If the script is mostly critical, it means the parent you inherited is busy measuring whatever you show the world against an impossible ideal. Then you can feel attacked from both sides: not only are you not good enough, but if you do seem to be good enough, it's probably fake, so it's like nothing you do really counts. When a new grown-up voice shows up saying "Trust me," with a background like that, well, of course your little one doesn't answer.

[116] Keep in mind that while a critical voice might be that inherited parent script, or any outside influence with no concern for your welfare, it could also be trying to protect you from rejection or failure. It may be pointing to a real problem, but in a cruel way.

[117] #### Reframing attacks as unskillful attempts to help

[118] One of my favorite teachers, Mr. Rogers, gave us an excellent example of how to extract the sweet nectar of truth from what less advanced souls might recoil at. In the movie A Beautiful Day in the Neighborhood, Lloyd, a reporter, tells Mr. Rogers, "I can't imagine it was easy… growing up with you as a father." He doesn't collapse under that or fight it, but he does answer what's worth answering in it. He tells Lloyd that until recently his oldest son never told people about his dad, and his youngest "genuinely tested" him. Eventually he and his youngest found their way, and now he's very proud of both his sons. Then he says, "But you are right, Lloyd. It couldn't have been easy on them. Thank you. Thank you for that perspective."

[119] We want to use the Mr. Rogers reframing trick to stay curious about the critical voice's motivations, and open to any kernels of truth it points out, without accepting its insults and cruelty. Then we send it on its way with compassion, since that voice surely needs some.

[120] ### Earning their trust, day to day

[121] A new roommate doesn't win you over with a speech on move-in day (hopefully, right?). The rent just has to keep showing up each month. Your Protector earns your little one's trust about the same way, out there in the real world, day after day. Little things make a big difference, like keeping a promise to yourself nobody else knew about, or saying no to the 13th friend today who asked you to Google something for them (I'm still working on that one).

[122] ## The Nurturer

[123] ### Love Can Start Before It's Trusted ❤️

[124] Sometimes the love is there for your little one and still feels unsafe to them, or safe for a minute and then not. Some researchers found a phrase for that: "compassion-threat." People in their study with anxious or avoidant attachment, or a tendency to be self-critical, reacted to compassion imagery like a threat, especially at first.

[125] Love and trust are reciprocal and intertangled, like the Chicken and Egg problem. Without trust, the love seems fake. And without love, the trust is shallow. When you show your love, it can sound like you're asking them to trust that you love them. But your love doesn't need their trust. It does need you to be honest with them, which isn't the same as perfect. They're still going over your track record (they've heard what you still call yourself when you mess up, sometimes out loud).

[126] When your little one says "I don't trust you," you can answer, "I believe you. And I love you." I learned this when I was 5. My mom told me it was time for bed (or something equally outrageous to a 5-year-old). I cried and screamed "I HATE YOU! I wish you were dead!" 😡 She answered, "But I love you!" 🥰

[127] That completely threw me. Fights don't usually go like that. Anger is a way to get more power when you feel a lack of justice or agency. You puff yourself up so you seem frightening or dangerous, or you demand to be heard. My anger was there to get me heard, and her answer showed me I didn't need it for that. Whatever injustice I thought there was wasn't coming from a lack of love, either. So the anger seemed stupid. I saw for myself that it was only hurting me, without helping me see clearly or get what I wanted. Still, what I screamed was true for a part of me right then, and what she said back was true too, so I could hate somebody who loved me and she'd still be my mom the whole time.

[128] When it's your little one yelling at you, though, try not to turn "But I love you" into a prompted line you say on cue, because then it can feel like a tactical ruse to brush the anger off. Your little one will be watching to see if the love stays afterward and takes whatever's true in their anger seriously.

[129] But even when the love does stay, your little one might still come back with "Big fuckity whoopty doo. I don't believe you." 🙄 You answer, "Okay. I believe that you don't believe me. I still love you. 🤗 You don't have to love me back. Tell me what makes the love feel unreal to you."

[130] Your little one might say it's you, without giving more details at first. Keep practicing your listening, and eventually you'll get more of a concrete complaint, which you can consider without getting flustered from the meanness they packaged it in. Like my anger at bedtime, their swearing is there to get them heard, so once your ear guards are off, they don't need it anymore.

[131] And sometimes you might feel the love for them, yet there's also some other voice at the same time, besides what you choose to say. When they say "I don't believe you," something in you might resent that, and maybe even fire back an accusation, and then they can learn your love is only there as long as they take it the right way. The resentment can be purely visceral: being doubted after you've tried feels unfair, but there's nothing unfair in them going over your track record. On the other hand, don't give in and start believing your love is more conditional than it really is, regardless of the past. Apply the Mr. Rogers reframing trick here as well.

[132] ### If the Love Isn't Coming Yet

[133] If you weren't loved well as a child, than the suggestion to "love your inner child" can sound like nonsense. Love them how? Maybe you really want the best for your little one. Maybe you hug them in your imagination, or miss them. Yet you still can't feel the warmth people mean when they say love. One direction to work with is to start smaller.

[134] Maybe just imagining a caring grown-up makes you feel more threatened, or lonelier. Lonelier is a strange one, with somebody right there. Then go smaller still, to the least cruel response a decent adult could offer your little one. This method of finding the most feasible next challenge is called scaffolding.

[135] Taking the hostility out makes room for warmth. Sometimes the warmth is actually there, with a cold part standing in front of it. If you've never been safe being loved, your ego can act like a rigid security guard that won't let the feeling in. But it generally will stand down once it feels its concerns have been heard and addressed. But the guard is still available for dealing with people who haven't earned your trust.

[136] If the love still isn't coming, you can at least stop judging your little one. Obviously no insults. Helpful advice is great, we all need advice. Let them absorb it at their pace. And don't expect a thank-you for the session, like they booked it. That's enough to start being the adult, even if it isn't enough yet for them to trust you. That takes warmth, and if there isn't any yet, borrow some.

[137] Your little one might not take it well at first. More like "Oh, so NOW you care?" Your first instinct might be "But I'm trying!" Some days, just hearing it out like a decent adult is the only thing you'll manage to borrow.

[138] ### Borrow Someone Else's Love

[139] When a good friend sits with you while you fall apart it does help you feel better. You call them again the next time, and they get a little better at it every time while you don't. So steal some of it. Usually what helped was how they just sat there without trying to fix you. Ten seconds of sitting with yourself like that is good enough for a start, so try it before you call next time.

[140] If you don't have anyone like that right now, try remembering when you did. Perhaps a therapist or your grandmother really cared for you. Think of someone like that you remember fondly, and imagine how they would sit beside your little one.

[141] ### Borrow Your Love For Someone Else

[142] Sometimes it's not that you can't love in general. Your dog can chew up your good shoes and be forgiven before you've found the other one. But maybe you direct that love to your little one and the attempt goes flat, or starts to feel weirdly unsafe. Just feel that love you can feel for others for a minute, in your body, before you try to redirect anything. Then as you relax into that and enjoy it more, imagine it flowing downhill, like an overflowing river of delicious chocolate. Now imagine how happy your inner child is at the bottom of the hill to play in that chocolate love that rolled down!

[143] Or put somebody you care about into your exact life, your family, all the conditions you face, and then step outside it and ask what you'd tell them as a friend. This psychological trick has a rich history around it, and the blind spot behind it even has a name, Solomon's paradox, after the biblical king everybody brought their disputes to, who then wrecked his own life.

[144] Then take a small piece of whatever you'd tell them and do it yourself. If it's "get more sleep," that could be brushing your teeth before midnight instead of at two.

[145] ### Show Up When Nothing's Wrong

[146] If the only time the Nurturer shows up is when something's wrong, your little one can start to feel like they're your job. Sometimes show up just to say "I'm glad you're here," and then enjoy them for a minute, the way you'd enjoy a little kid who wants to show you a rock they found.

[147] Watch how your love sounds to your little boo, in context. "I care about you" can be patient, or it can be "I care about you, now hurry up and feel better." Getting close can be too much, too, the way kids squirm away from an aunt they barely know who goes in for the big hug, and then climb all over the uncle who's just sitting there. Knowing that love is in you somewhere counts for a lot, and you're borrowing something you already know how to do.

[148] ### A Smaller Doorway: Goodwill

[149] Sometimes "I love you" is more than you can honestly say, especially to somebody who's treated you badly. Even the usual whittled-down loving-kindness prayer, "May you be happy," can feel like rewarding them. That's why I go with the Buddha's overall wish (paraphrasing): "**May you be loving, peaceful, and free**," since if they were all of those, they wouldn't have done what they did, and then they actually would be lovable.

[150] This goodwill isn't just for the target of it, though. You may find it bounces back and opens up a little warmth in your heart. And if "I love you" goes flat when said to yourself or your little one, the same goodwill wish can be a stabler place to start. 🫶🏿

[151] ### Ask God for a Loan

[152] The dog kind of love is real love. Some people also know a love that's way more profound, like God's. Maybe it opened up for them in prayer or on a long retreat, and I don't want to lump that in with the dog. If you've felt that, you don't need to shrink your love down to a thought experiment about a friend.

[153] If you haven't been there yet (and even if you have), I have good news for you!

[154] #### Your Loan is PRE-APPROVED!

[155] One practical door anyone can use to slowly experience divine love is gratitude (even after the Turkey's done & Thanksgiving is over!). Genuinely cultivated gratitude (not just empty/forced ritual) is one way to experience divine love for the first time, or to get it back when the feeling fades. I think it's essential if we want to live in that love instead of occasionally visiting it.

[156] This is a bit tricky to put into words, but let me try anyway… You may be suffering now, but you could be suffering more. That's something to be grateful for (although please don't fall into the common trap of being grateful that you have what others don't have, as that's more like selfishness). I'm not talking just about changing your "glass half empty" perception to a glass "half full perception." I'm not pushing "toxic positivity." Being thankful shouldn't be pushed on people who just need to grieve for the moment. On the other hand, I've noticed that I can sometimes completely flip a bad psychedelic trip into a good one with gratitude alone.

[157] If you have a moment right now to breathe, and to really feel the beauty of that breath as it goes in and out, that's something to be grateful for. I learned this many times the hard way. For example, when I got tuberculosis from a cave in Mexico at age 20, and coughed for six months straight, eventually having my throat close up, leading to a near death experience. And then in Senegal, I had a suspected bilharzia infection in 2021, and could only painfully pee blood. I promised to always be thankful if I could ever pee normally again. Miraculously, I then found some obscure ethnobotanical research (on Google Scholar, before AI) which led me to drink roselle tea. Within an hour, I was cured. I don't always remember to say "thank you" when I pee now, but I try.

[158] That may sound like a silly, quirky waste of time to some folks. But what I notice is that saying thank you for every little thing, whenever I remember to, is not about imposing some kind of "spiritual discipline." It is directly reconnecting me to something pure and sacred.

[159] ##### Mo' Money, Mo' Problems

[160] And I'm certainly not preaching "prosperity philosophy," like the mega churches, even though it can seem to start at that. We still need to remember that if we can only be thankful for the good things we have, we can fall into the trap of thinking that what we have is happiness itself. I'm ultimately reaching for something much deeper. Because getting what you want is an endless loop, and it doesn't lead to being content with less. When you can see that less is more, at a deeper level, that's where the real gratitude flourishes.

[161] And even (or especially) when I am suffering, I try to remember to be grateful for that also, because if not for suffering, I would not even think about how much I have enjoyed not suffering as much, and how much this tribulation will help me to develop more humility, compassion, wisdom, and gratitude in future. Most importantly, it connects me with the plight of the countless other beings suffering at that very moment, authentically bringing up a wellspring of compassion in my heart for all of us. And I see clearly that it is my inner child who is feeling this pain, and what an honor it is that I can be there for him.

[162] Of course, that's not to say you should not try to suffer less, or get away from abusers. The point of life, according to every spiritual tradition and religion, I think, is to suffer less, which first requires us to understand the cause of suffering, and what real suffering and real happiness are. If you, like Biggie Smalls, have come to understand that having money is not real happiness, that's a big deal! That's something to be grateful for. If we get a chance to meditate or pray today, while on the toilet, upon waking up, while ChatGPT is "thinking" for us, that's something to be thankful for, for sure.

[163] Notice how, when you offer thanks (to the universe, to life, to God, whatever you may believe in), a space in your heart opens up. It might not feel immediately like you suddenly know the real meaning of Divine Love, but it's a little spark of … something.... There's a tiny bit of freedom, of peace, stillness, softness. And as that softness deepens each time, you start to understand what I mean by Divine Love. Many people have written books on this topic (like A. Helwa's book, Secrets of Divine Love, for example). I hope you are able to experience this when you try it out for a while, and I'd really love to know how it works for you!

[164] ### Invite Your Little One In

[165] Once you start to feel that lovely softness of the heart (which can become a very deep field of "perfect freedom"), invite your little one in with you as your spiritual integration practice. They might go numb, or call BS on that. If so, they might just need to see if you're actually humble enough, and grateful enough for them, to listen to their rejection, be patient, and be open to the possibility that your Higher View is missing the trees for the forest. If you instead retreat back into your cozy corner, it's the food bank feast all over again: You can have an enormous spiritual feast upstairs while the child is still starving downstairs.
