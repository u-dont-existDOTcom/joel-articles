# Part 2 of the article: blocks 166 to 343

## Context before this part (read it, don't report on it)

[164] ### Invite Your Little One In

[165] Once you start to feel that lovely softness of the heart (which can become a very deep field of "perfect freedom"), invite your little one in with you as your spiritual integration practice. They might go numb, or call BS on that. If so, they might just need to see if you're actually humble enough, and grateful enough for them, to listen to their rejection, be patient, and be open to the possibility that your Higher View is missing the trees for the forest. If you instead retreat back into your cozy corner, it's the food bank feast all over again: You can have an enormous spiritual feast upstairs while the child is still starving downstairs.

## The part to read

[166] #### A Heart to Child Loop

[167] With a little warmth to work with, rest one hand at the center of your chest (the heart chakra) and feel for love, and the other on the solar plexus, just below, and feel for your little one. The solar plexus is the most vulnerable part of the torso. One punch there hurts a lot (as I sadly learned while getting beaten up every day at kindergarten recess by a gang organized by my best friend. Can you believe it?), so it makes sense to look for your little one there.

[168] Picture the love traveling from your heart down to your little one. If some comes back up, let it, and send it on down again, so it goes around like a loop. That part's a gift from them, and they don't owe it to you, so if nothing comes back, just keep sending yours. If you don't feel the warmth just yet, rest your hands there and notice what you find, no faking required. Non-judgement is a place to start. It can blossom into love over time. I also enjoy massaging my solar plexus gently.

[169] ### When the Spiritual Relationship Hurts

[170] If a spiritual teacher you trusted betrayed you, even sitting down to meditate can feel like it belongs to them now. But if you let an abuser control your appreciation of life, then you've given them an unearned win.

[171] Being the grown-up doesn't mean controlling everything yourself, though. After a betrayal like that, part of you might want to. We can use Niebuhr's Serenity Prayer here to help: "God, grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change the courage to change the things I can and the wisdom to know the difference." (Replace "God" with universe if you wish.)

[172] ### Make a Simple Vow

[173] If your promises still mean something to your little one, maybe because you've kept a few small ones, a quite vow can help too. As many real-life parents know, your heart really explodes in such an unexpected and wonderful way when you hold your newborn baby for the first time (happened to me!). And if you're not yet a parent, it can really help to imagine holding a baby in your arms to make the vow feel real. Imagine you just had a baby, how would you feel? Would you look at him/her with adoration and joy? Carry that into visualizing yourself with your own inner child:

[174] "I know you're in there. I love you. You don't have to love me back or trust me yet. I can't promise that nothing painful will happen. Or that I'll always know what to do, or never react badly. But I can promise to tell you the truth and listen when you're angry. I'll make our life together safer where I can, and take the next step I can see to protect you. When I fail, I'll come back to make it right. You don't have to believe these words. Watch what I do."

[175] Notice it only promises what you can keep, so your little one can actually watch you keep it.

[176] Whenever your little one shows up looking for their parent, even if they don't know that's what they are doing, you go back to being the one they can rely on, and you're kind to them while you do it. If they show up to have some innocent fun, don't "put them in their place" as the adult. But sometimes they show up as the one who doesn't want to go to bed. Now you're in my mom's place: you still go to bed, and staying kind about it shows them bedtime isn't a lack of love.

[177] #### Remind Yourself

[178] The vow can live somewhere you'll see it, too, like on a stuffed animal from when you were little, a photo of yourself as a child, or just a sticky note on the bathroom mirror. Whatever helps you feel connected to the Nurturer and Protector you've been practicing. The Teddy bear note may remind you to think, "When I see you, I remember to stop attacking myself and go to bed on time."

[179] A real-life child of yours, or a calm pet, can also act as a companion that brings out the relationship with your own inner child. My son did that for me, and I think that's very common for parents. But those are also their own beings with their own needs as well. Let them come and go without making them responsible for the session.

[180] ### How Much Happiness Do You Know Is Possible?

[181] When you wish someone well, what do you have in mind? Probably something like, "I want them to have enough to eat, a safe place to sleep, some people who like them, etc." Wanting this for someone is a form of love (this is the kind babies already understand). It's a good and important thing, this practical love. But the horizon of your wishes may be partly determined by how much happiness/peace/freedom you know to be possible.

[182] Sometimes your little one already feels cared for in those normal ways, but they need help with deeper existential questions. Maybe your life isn't meant to be just something to put up with, something to slog through, a hamster wheel, a rat race. Maybe you could live it with a sense of zest and discovery. What would you need for that?

[183] Maybe you've had some big spiritual experiences, and for a while knew a kind of peace/love etc. that you hadn't known before. Now when you wish someone well, your wishes may be bigger – perhaps much bigger – than they would have been before. You might "love them in a language they will never learn." This is one reason why loving-kindness meditation can seem kind of silly but actually be very powerful. Saying, "May you be happy," over and over won't necessarily zap you into a state of profound love for all beings, but it's one way people discover that there is such a love and learn to expand it.

[184] If you're curious about this bigger love and happiness, follow that curiosity. You don't have to pretend to believe anything, but you might read a few people from different traditions (try a Sufi poet or a Zen teacher). If you know anyone who seems consistently happy in this bigger way, check out how they deal with people when things are going badly. And if they still pass that test, consider, "I don't know if I believe their explanation. But what if the experience is possible for me?" That's what I did, and it was the best move I've ever made!

[185] ### The Joy In Sorrow

[186] Jelaluddin Rumi wrote: "The most secure place to hide a treasure of gold is some desolate, unnoticed place. Why would anyone hide treasure in plain sight? And so it is said: 'Joy is hidden in sorrow.'"

[187] Another Sufi teacher, Hazrat Inayat Khan, wrote, "God breaks the heart again and again and again until it stays open."

[188] Some people find that deep grieving gives way to feeling connected to something much deeper than what was taken away. Being loved can open grief, too. Someone takes care of you, and you think, "Why does this make me sad?" It can be sadness for the care that was missing.

[189] As Sister Medhanandi said, "When we are able to come face to face with our own direst fears and vulnerability, when we can step into the unknown with courage and openness, we touch near to the mysteries of this traverse through the human realm to an authentic self-fulfilment. We touch what we fear the most, we transform it, we see the emptiness of it. In that emptiness, all things can abide, all things come to fruition. In this very moment, we can free ourselves."

[190] ## The Inner Guide: Becoming Your Own Shepherd

[191] As the Guide, you choose what your little one learns and what it gets to experience, and you hold standards, the way a good parent would. Your parent's harsh tone doesn't come with the job, though.

[192] By the way, this job doesn't have to be called the Guide. The Wise One works, or your Inner Teacher, and "Guru" does for some people.

[193] If you don't feel like the wisest apple on the tree just yet, that's perfectly fine, because we can borrow the guiding function to start with, and you can get better at it day by day. Perhaps your teacher at some age was a great Guide. Ask what the wise person you respect would have you practice today. A wise tradition ("What would Jesus do?" or "What would Buddha do?") can give some direction, too, while you develop your own internal compass.

[194] ### Borrow Hindsight

[195] I sometimes ask: "How would I respond to this once I understood it more clearly, in balance and with compassion?" Some moments of silence then allow incredible wisdom and compassion to pour down in moments of grace. At other times, you might just have a slightly better understanding develop slowly.

[196] Your future self probably also knows why you can't pull this off perfectly today. What would they do for you, if they were here? Maybe instead of a magic solution, they'd remind you of what my mom often reminded me: "Everything always works out in the end, somehow!". I thought that was actually pretty funny, but it consoled her. One man's humor is another's wisdom.

[197] You can borrow a wiser future version of whoever else is involved, too, although don't assume they'll definitely end up on your side, or sorry. What the current one keeps doing is still what you go by, but I've definitely felt a lot more patience in arguments when I could imagine the future versions of us saying "that was just a silly misunderstanding!".

[198] ### Remember When You Already Did It Well

[199] My father often suggested folks remember a similar-level (or even harder) challenge that they were able to handle. Maybe you survived a car crash and surgery a few years ago, but now you're having trouble with emotional eating. You can draw strength from the past. As my sister, a historian, likes to say, "The past didn't go anywhere." Your own challenge doesn't need to be as dramatic as a car crash, as long as it really happened. So who or what helped you get through it? Is one piece of that still within reach? If you catch yourself thinking "I survived a car crash, so why can't I stop eating when I'm upset?", that's the past turned into a stick to hit yourself with. Maybe you're more worn out now, or missing whatever helped back then. Getting hit with a stick doesn't solve the problem, unless you're a Zen student. 🙃

[200] ### A Healthy Mind Is Not a Perfect Mind

[201] The Guide doesn't need to know everything for sure (that would be an OCD Guide, and then you'd need a Guide for the OCD Guide). It chooses the next sane step that fits your values based on the info available, and that's how it helps your little one grow toward a whole life. Waiting to be sure first is sometimes proper & prudent, but if it's a pattern, it can keep life on hold, and then your little one's world stays small. Sometimes something really is stuck on someone else's end (you've asked, and followed up, and there's nothing left for you to do yet), and waiting on that isn't avoidance. The Guide can let it wait, keeping an eye out for when it frees up, and keep the rest of your life moving, so your little one keeps growing anyway.

[202] Forced adulthood can get into your head too, and it might set the bar too high sometimes. At your grandpa's funeral, some dumb jingle starts up in your head and you nearly laugh during the eulogy. That doesn't mean you didn't love him, or that something's wrong with you. Minds come up with ugly and embarrassing stuff all the time, some of it the opposite of what you believe, and bodies do their own thing too. The Guide doesn't need to police your every thought. It notices the jingle and knows you didn't mean it, and that it isn't who you are. Then it goes with what you'd stand behind, which here is probably squeezing your mom's hand and listening, and lets the jingle go.

[203] I think this is closer to Buddhist practice too. The point is not an impossible performance in which nothing unskillful ever appears. In one discourse (sutta), AN 6.63, the Buddha defines karma in terms of intention (so if you didn't mean to cause harm, your mind is not poisoned). In DN 2, he explains that in the training to purify the mind, one first recognizes a fault, makes amends, and then restrains themself to prevent that kind of thing in future. So it's not like, "you're either pure, or you're not," which is where a lot of folks stay stuck. If you're ashamed about some pattern you have, that might be a signal to give some attention to how you can improve on that pattern. If it's shame just because of an intrusive thought, then it's probably not adaptive, and can be simply let go of.

[204] ### You Belong Everywhere and Nowhere

[205] The Guide also points beyond itself, toward somewhere you belong that doesn't ask you to give up being yourself. That might be nature, or existence itself. It might be whatever your tradition sees as ultimate. Love can come from any of these places, and you also belong there. Maybe you've felt that randomly while wandering around outside somewhere, like you didn't need anybody's permission to be there. The way into "the zone" is time in nature and contemplative practice.

[206] With that under you, belonging with people can get a lot less desperate. Your little one might still think, "If they stop liking me, I won't belong anywhere," and the Guide can remind it there's more to belong to than them. And then one day you might have the opposite problem, with people wanting to belong too much to you.

[207] ### Timing Is Individual

[208] Most of us need enough Nurturer and Protector first. Otherwise the Guide just overrules your little one in the name of something higher (spiritual bypassing, dressed up as direction). Like a shepherd who's beating sheep to make them move.

[209] Say you had a spiritual opening years before you had much of a Nurturer or Protector. Has your love grown because of it, and has that love reached your little one yet? Look at how you are with people, too, whether you're more compassionate and understanding. Are you getting better at relationships, including telling the truth when it's awkward and doing what you said you'd do?

[210] ### Between Spoiling and Clamping Down

[211] Maybe you've let a small child win at Go Fish and never found a good time to start playing for real. Who wants to be the grown-up who finally wins? Without the Guide, you can slide from looking after your little one into spoiling it the same way. Sweets and screens can pass for loving yourself, and so can getting it out of anything hard. Treats and pampering do better at set times, where they can't get out of hand. They shouldn't get canceled as a punishment after a bad day, either.

[212] After all, sometimes "I want it NOW!" is just that: a rung on the desire treadmill. No hidden guard or old trauma required, because all creatures have endless desires naturally anyway, especially when immature. You can hear that want without shame about it. Hearing it doesn't require giving into it, especially if getting your way would hurt you or somebody else.

[213] We also need to remember that many of our desires are covering over deeper desires which we may be afraid to look at. Relationship desires, for example, can be scary because we don't have unilateral ability to satisfy them. Learning to acknowledge the unmet needs without a definite solution is a deep Guide capacity which takes maturity to appreciate.

[214] # Practicing With Your Little One

[215] ## Start With Whatever Showed Up

[216] You might go looking for your little one and get mad instead, or realize you've been staring at the rug. I count that as reparenting, too, even if it feels like it's in the way. Before you decide where it's coming from, ask it what it's trying to stop, or what it wants. Maybe it just has something to say. It could be your little one, mad that you took so long to come back, or a part of you that's trying to protect you. Maybe it's that parent you inherited, or something from earlier today, or a bit of each.

[217] You can tell that part something like, "I see you. I'm not going to force my way past you. What's going on, boobaloo?" Or however you'd actually say that. Then wait and listen patiently.

[218] ## You Don't Need an Inner Monologue

[219] If you don't hear a little kid talking in your head, don't invent one. Actually, this is one place where the usual inner-child prompt to "ask your little one what they need" can get confusing, because "ask" sounds like you're supposed to ask a question and then wait for a second voice to answer. Maybe that's what happens for some people, but you might not be some people (hehe). If talking into an empty room feels silly, small figures on a table can help (so that you feel even more silly!?), like one for your little one and one for the rest of the gang that shows up. Put whichever one's running the show on a throne. Both ideas come from sections 12.2 and 13.1 of a book called Schema Therapy for Children and Adolescents, which I'll call ST-CA.

[220] You might barely have spontaneous words in your mind and yet still be able to think a sentence on purpose. Try it and see. If words are there, speak or write them. You might feel more comfortable that way than just staying in imagination land.

[221] Then again, maybe what's actually requiring your attention now is a knot in your stomach. Or perhaps you're in a place you want to leave. Sometimes nothing "answers" and you just know what would be nice to do for yourself. And if you don't know, it's fine to guess. Maybe it's some genuine human closeness, or figuring out some way to have more say in your own life.

[222] Life is always switching up on us. You could have a feeling before thoughts, or thoughts might come first and provoke a feeling. They say hindsight is 20/20, but you actually might not even know which happened first.

[223] ## Use Childhood Photographs Gently

[224] My friend Cody had trouble imagining his inner child, so he started looking at photos of himself when he was young. That did the trick! If you have the same trouble, see if you can find some childhood photos.

[225] While you look, notice any new feelings or urges, and any tightness or unusual feelings in your body, including the good ones. Use Pema Chödrön's "feather touch" approach to gently bring some light and love to the edges of any contracted feelings. Is there something that younger you seems to want you to understand? Some of it you'll remember yourself, and some of it you might even glean from talking to family/friends from then ("You hated that coat"). It might just be an inference you're guessing from the picture, or perhaps it triggers an image or a dream. Or just nothing comes up. Ask what your little one needs from you now, and do a bit of it.

[226] We don't need to ask it leading questions, like "Who abused me & how?" or "What memory is my body hiding?" Grilling one photo until a story shows up is a good way to get a story, but stories aren't necessarily the truth, and even if they are accurate, the feelings beneath them are still more important. Memory is an amorphous thing, easily influenced by guided imagery and suggestions. Look at this study, for example, where people were shown a doctored photo of a childhood hot-air balloon ride they never took. Half of them actually started sorta remembering it. I'm sure you can relate.

[227] A photo is just one moment that somebody picked and framed (like O.J.😅)... not necessarily representative. Flipping thru an album may give different jolts in your body.

[228] If you start to suspect someone of something serious, like abusing you, please don't confront or accuse anyone based only on what came up here. You might be looking at the photo thinking, "That would explain so much." Journal what came up and how it came up, and see if you can get support from someone level-headed who won't lead you toward an answer. (If the glove doesn't fit, you must acquit!)

[229] Put the photo back in its drawer when you're finished. If it starts tugging at you, so you keep going back to search it or you need to know for sure, or you're getting overwhelmed, take a break from it right then. Settle yourself in the present to close out properly. Re-evaluation Counseling, a global movement of peer counseling, always ends on this "Present Time" reminder. Can be as simple and silly as "Oh, Today is Monday, my favorite day of the week!"

[230] ## Journal With Each Hand

[231] Lucia Capacchione, an art therapist, wrote a whole book on this one, The Power of Your Other Hand. As titles go, it's pretty literal. Your other hand, the clumsy one, writes like a first grader anyway, so it gets the scared or hurt feelings, as well as some extra creative juice. The usual hand answers, as the grown-up. Some people who get nothing but silence from their little one are surprised by what the other hand comes up with. A protective part might grab the pen first, and then it gets that hand until it's had its say, even if all it can do is scribble angrily.

[232] For some people, writing might make them feel more confused, especially at first, or nothing might want to be written. Then just keep track for a while, and save the patterns for later, if they turn out to matter at all.

[233] ### Which thoughts to talk to

[234] When journaling, you might be surprised sometimes by the myriad things that come into your mind seemingly out of nowhere. Let a stray thought go as just thinking, and come back to the room you're in. A worry that just keeps asking to be reassured can go the same way, unless it's your little one asking.

[235] I wouldn't spend long deciding what to call each part you meet. Every part of you learns from how the others treat it, your little one included. A part of you that's trying to protect you might warn you about something, and when you hear it out, it doesn't have to get any louder.

[236] ### If no answer comes

[237] That's fine for now. No need to answer for that part just because you had set this up as a healing session. Even without the answer, you can notice what sets the reaction off, and you can understand what the reaction is propelling you toward, and away from.

[238] ### If you do get an answer

[239] An answer could come in different forms, like as a voice or just a hunch. It's often more like a feeling. Maybe grief or anger. If so just glisten and be present for it if that's what it wants. How you picture all that is up to you, as long as it helps, and the simpler the better.

[240] ### If it tells you to do something

[241] I'd only do it if it's loving and leaves you free. It should make sense, too. Even then, anything big or hard to undo can wait until you're calm and you've checked it against what you actually know.

[242] ### If it's anxiety

[243] Anxiety is often a part of you that's trying to protect you, making a guess about what's coming, like that people will laugh at you. Sometimes it's right, sometimes it's useful, sometimes it's not. Anxiety is not necessarily bad by itself, but as they say, the fear of fear is worse than the fear itself. Hearing it out and taking any action that seems legitimately beneficial as a result, often helps it to pass.

[244] ## Catch the Hook Before the Story Takes Over

[245] Pema Chödrön calls the tightening and urge you get before the story has finished forming "shenpa," and she says "hooked" is a more descriptive translation for that Tibetan concept than the usual "attachment." You might notice the hook before you have any clue what part of you is doing the hooking. 💃🏿 Or you might notice it embarrassingly late. The jaw and stomach are already tight, and/or half the text is written, and then suddenly it dawns on you: you're supposed to be observing yourself. 🤦‍♂️ Maybe forget the parts detective work for a minute.

[246] When you catch it, look around and just take a moment to enjoy some free honesty: "Oh! I'm hooked again! That's awesome I could notice it!" Take a breath or three before you finish whatever you were doing. Maybe then you'll find there's enough adult around to be nice to your little boo for a second. Or enough Protector to stop something you'd regret later. Or enough Guide to redirect attention to where it's supposed to be.

[247] ## Gentle Meditation and Mantra

[248] If the hook is stuck a bit deeper in your mouth and you're flailing around like a fish out of water, gentle mantra meditation or self-hypnosis (which overlap a lot) are some tools that can really calm your nervous system down. A lot of people find that a mantra they feel some heart connection to, repeated in their head or out loud, especially with a nice melody, can lower the walls enough for the heart to open up. It's not something to lean on all day as a way of coping, but used as therapy it can be super effective. [249] ## What to Make of a Passing Feeling

[250] If what someone did hit something old in you, notice how that feels in your body. And notice how your feelings can also amplify or distort your perceptions of others. Just because your stomach tightened up doesn't mean their boundary crossing indicates "They're evil." You can defer that judgement for when you're in your happy place.

[251] Feelings about yourself work the same way. "I feel attracted" or "I feel ashamed" is real as a feeling, but it doesn't settle who you are, or who you love. Making too much out of something momentary is one side of the human psyche's pendulum. Give yourself time to see what patterns form more durably, and what feels authentically yours vs something planted or manipulated into being there from society, influencers, family, friends, religion, etc., etc.

[252] You may find an exceptional freedom in exactly that in-between place where you don't have to declare your pronouns on social media, or whether you believe in this or that. If you stay in flux forever, though, it can seem like there's nothing solid about you. In that case, sometimes it helps to meditate or talk with a good unbiased therapist who can help you see if there's some avoidance of differentiation (we get deeper into that further on). I myself for a long time was accused of "having no opinions" (which I almost prided myself on🙈), and now it's often the opposite accusation.

[253] The opposite extreme of our pendulum, throwing everything transient away, as some people do with "thought-noting" practice, also isn't healthy, except as a meditation exercise. Transient feelings and thoughts can be meaningful signals. For example, if you don't want to be touched, say no now and work out what it means later.

[254] ## One Intention in the Morning, One Review at Night

[255] In the morning, begin the day intentionally, not just because the sun chose to come up once more, since we can't count on that. 🫣 Here's one way you might pl/orkfully start off:

[256] "Today I will neither be Peter Pan nor Captain Hook. I will notice a hook or 2 which lead to unconscious behaviors."

[257] Then at night, pay yourself to be your own therapist so as not to break the bank. Equipoisedly look into how the practice went for you today:

[258] "Where did I notice the hook?" At dinner with the in-laws? Watching animé?

[259] "Where did it still seem to control me despite noticing it?" (That's the most frustrating part, when you know better, and you still act the same.)

[260] Then get practical so it doesn't become another OCD loop: "What small repair or protective act shall I bring in for a proper response to that?"

[261] And don't forget to appreciate the good! Moments of warm, beneficial connection (whether internally from child to adult, socially, spiritually, etc) are all to be celebrated. Were you maybe firm like an elephant today, or courageous like a lion? Adopt the entire spirit animal zoo if need be! Turtles are good if you need impulse control.

[262] Count recognition as progress, regardless whether it came up a bit late, as the goal is to bring the awareness closer and closer to the present. All or nothing from the get go would be cool, but we normally have to learn step by step. If/when you interrupted the pattern, pat yourself on the back, or even kiss yourself on the shoulder. You can renew your intention for tomorrow, getting better & better, every day, in every way, as my Dad used to say. And enjoy your well-earned rest tonight.

[263] ## Close the Session and Return to Life

[264] Without an ending, the processing can just start looping. So when you're done, look around and say where you are. Feel your feet on the floor, or the chair under you, and remind yourself about something silly and obvious, like what year Pharaoh Tutankhamun was crowned (1332 BCE in case you forgot!)…

[265] And what's next for you two? Maybe it's making food, or just going to bed. Whatever it is, tell your little one, "That's enough for today. We're going back to the life we're building." Nothing has to be resolved first, and whatever you're still feeling can come along.

[266] # Discovering and Healing Old Patterns

[267] ## How Distrust Reproduces

[268] Say you learned somewhere along the way that if you need someone, they end up leaving you. You probably never said it to yourself in words, but it steers you before you know why. But the need for socialization remains a pull, regardless of how unlikely it may be to work. And so we develop various coping strategies, ranging from distracting ourselves from that natural pull with other things (whether harmless hobbies or bingeing on TastyKakes), to people-pleasing, to insisting we don't need anybody (sour grapes). (The TastyKake corporation has asked me to remind you that sometimes a sweet treat is more a celebration than an escape.)

[269] Some people pleasers go along for months as if nothing's wrong while the pressure builds and then they blow up out of nowhere (one of my old patterns). Then they feel so bad about it they go right back to going along to get along. With any of these patterns, I'd ask what it's protecting you from, because it doesn't really help to just label it a bad habit.

[270] The most insidious part of these expectations is that we can get stuck in them even when they no longer make valid predictions about the people in our life. Like my second wife, Bee, who wanted with all her heart to trust me, but set up so many impossible tests that not even Einstein could pass with a perfect score. I tried to explain to her that she was an unwitting prophet.

[271] Have you ever heard of a self-fulfilling prophecy? Perhaps nobody ever hears that you're struggling, since you didn't think they'd want to. Then nobody offers to help, and that can look like proof nobody cares. Keeping quiet might have been the smartest thing you could do as a youngin' with nobody safe to tell, but now, if there are people who are safely available, it can keep help away.

[272] ## Shame can make you feel unlovable

[273] Shame comes when you're holding a dark secret. It might be a secret for someone else who abused you, because it made you feel dirty or worthless. Or it might be due to thoughts in your head that bother you. Or it might be from bad patterns of behavior you have. Whatever the cause, finding someone trusted that you can slowly open up to is a magic cure. You still have to take accountability and repair any damage you caused if you did.

[274] Shame becomes toxic when it gets stuck, and you don't know what to do with it. Then you can become ashamed of being ashamed, and on and on. Get curious about what makes it so hard to give up. I went through toxic shame for 12 years. I'm sure it will help you to read my full guide on shame. Here's one trick from that article:

[275] ### "And So That's the Way It Happened!"

[276] My ex, X, taught me to append "And so that's the way it happened!" to a difficult memory. Say it the way a grandpa would finish a story on the porch. ("Once upon a time…" helps if the start is where you get stuck.) Somehow it moves the whole thing out of the courtroom, where the case was still being argued, and into your life story. Pick a memory you can handle for the first few tries.

[277] ## How to Forgive Without Forgetting

[278] Healing does need to get around to some forgiveness eventually, or at least to not being run by the resentment anymore. It can't be put on a schedule. If someone pressures you into forgiving early, what you get is an enlightened-looking act, with the wound gone underground. And you don't have to forgive first to get safe, set boundaries, hold someone accountable, get justice or get better. Then again, active resentment narrows what you see, and your little one stays organized around the injury.

[279] The understanding that helps me is seeing how a person's conditioning, history, pain, and capacity shaped the harmful move they made. Picture everything that had made them who they were by that moment, what happened to them and what they learned, the pain they carried and what they could and couldn't handle. From in there, it was the only move they had.

[280] "Hurt people hurt people, healed people heal people" means that hurt people pass on pain they never worked through, although "externalizers" are the ones who directly hurt others, while "internalizers" try to protect others by holding that "secret burning thread" inside themself, which ends up exploding onto others anyway at times when it gets to be too much to hold. You may have heard this story from somebody telling you to forgive, and it sounded like they must have no clue how deep the betrayal was. When you see it for yourself, though, you might find yourself forgiving them, and if you've held them uniquely to blame, the rage can soften into something more accurate.

[281] They still have to answer for what they did, and it still hurt, even if you see how they got there. It's just that punishing them now can't reach back and give them what they were missing then, the sense, the support, or the skills they needed for a respectable response. What's left is fixing what can be fixed, and making it easier for something different to happen now.

[282] So a boundary can still be appropriate to enforce with them. So can the law, if it comes to that, or telling them plainly it was wrong (or telling yourself, if they'd never hear it). Keeping away from them for good can be right too, and you can still forgive them from over there. Understanding somebody's limitations should make my expectations more realistic, not make those limitations less consequential. "I understand how you became this way" can sit right next to "I now understand why I shouldn't rely on you for this."

[283] ### Forgiving Yourself

[284] Forgiving yourself works the same way. Own what you did (only what was really yours), and let yourself feel the remorse. Repair what you can, by apologizing or making amends, and by changing behavior going forward. Then you're allowed to stop holding yourself hostage, and the responsibility stays. If you're thinking "not me, not after what I did," forgiving other people tends to be harder too.

[285] ## Using hindsight to "re-do" memories of trauma in a healthier way

[286] Now, once you've got this cast of perfected future characters (from Borrow Hindsight) in your mind, you can also go back to earlier traumatic memories and relive them in a better way. For example, I'll take one instance from my own childhood. I was only spanked one time as a child. I hung up the (expensive) phone call my dad was on with a very important official in another country, because I wanted some attention. I don't think he was exactly furious at me, but he decided this deserved a spanking, even if he didn't agree with spanking in general. Since he wasn't furious, the spanking didn't really hurt, but it hurt me emotionally quite a lot, because I was so extremely shocked that my parents would use physical violence on me. Like I lost my sense of safety with them at that moment. And I may have learned that I was undeserving of attention, that I behaved in ways that made me deserve physical violence, that I was bad, etc.

[287] Many people experienced something like this where the consequences for their little mind were much more severe than anyone would imagine, and in almost every case, the adult themself does not currently understand the extent of the damage until the little one feels safe to tell them. I can't tell you how many people I've worked with have laughed at how brutally they were beaten as kids. Just laughed it off like it was nothing.

[288] Whatever the situation was, we will contrast it with the corrected version. For my friend J., whose dad savagely beat him every time he smiled, I'd have the corrected version be, he smiles, and his dad smiles, happy to see him happy. Ok, that one might seem too easy, although for J, it might take years to imagine that correction.

[289] ### How should discipline have been done right?

[290] While often it is obvious how a perfect parent could have responded, in my example, I'm not exactly sure what a perfect father would have done here. But usually what my dad preferred as discipline was a forced "time-in" via a "punishment hug", and I feel like that worked well for me. I did that with my son, Bear, as well. I would hold him as lovingly as possible, until he started to cry, because he did not seem interested in learning any lessons until that point. After he cried for a bit, we talked about what happened and what was wrong with it. When I felt satisfied he was understanding and aligning his values in a genuine way, the time-in was over, although he might want to continue the time-in sometimes a bit longer also, as it began to feel nice after the ego came down.

[291] My sister disagrees that this was therapeutic for her. She thinks it caused her to feel like hugs in general are punishment. A regular time-out could work, but sometimes it has to be enforced somehow, like locking the kid in their room for a bit (and assuming they can't just have fun in their room). Recognizing the difficulty in finding a perfect parental response can also help us. Sometimes there was no perfect response that would have been harmless to all parties. But my sister should probably go back to those memories of punishment hugs and ensure that she learns that hugs are loving, even if that particular hug was also designed to teach a lesson about improving her behavior.

[292] I think that what children really need when they are acting narcissistically is some way to break through to real emotional connection. With very stubborn children (most are at times), that may require induced crying. It didn't always require a punishment hug to do that, either. Just shouting "BEAR!" could make my son cry at times. It's crucial here that the caregiver producing the child's egoic breakdown must have the moral high ground along with compassion, so they aren't seen as just someone abusing their power. And they should be open themselves to being corrected by the child if the child has a valid counter-argument for who is in the right.

[293] Importantly, there is currently no expert consensus on how children should be disciplined, aside from using the least amount of force possible, in the least traumatizing way, whatever that may happen to be. There isn't even agreement yet if logical consequences work. And one thing Visakan made me consider is that the parent-child relationship is the only one that's supposed to change in major ways many times over the years. A parent might be great at a certain phase of child-rearing, and terrible at another (like treating you forever as if you're 5).

[294] ## Answering the Strict Disciplinarian

[295] A good routine, like yoga in the morning and a proper bedtime, still feels like lockdown to your little one when it's done in a disciplinarian style. One exercise you can do to handle this is to go back and rewrite your responses to that strict disciplinarian. First, remember who it was that drove you so hard as a child. Then start a little rap sheet of their provoking lines. Comfort your little one before you answer any of it. Your answers can be firm or funny, as long as they aren't mean, since mean is the Critic's department.

[296] The Critic's voice can often pile on, to the point where you can't even understand why or what you're really feeling anymore. You feel sad at first, then the Inner Critic calls your inner child pathetic for being sad, and now you're ashamed and want to hide. You could probably have handled the sadness with some simple nurturing instead from the beginning, but now you have to peel back this other nonsense to get to it.

[297] # When the Practice Gets Difficult

[298] ## When the Adult Voice Feels Fake

[299] A lot of people get stuck right at square one. You call up the Nurturer and try to talk to your little one, and you get silence or a numb feeling, and all of a sudden your phone or the laundry feels urgent. Or some voice says this is silly, and it's hard to tell which part of you is saying it.

[300] My friend Scott said:

[301] "Half the time when I try to connect to baby me, I feel fake and it seems crazy."

[302] I used to be all personal trainer and teach persistence on this as the remedy. Keeping at it does help, since a little one who learned to expect being dropped won't thrust you right away. But I don't think that explains the adult feeling fully. More importantly, I think it could even be like unintentional gaslighting to the little one to ask to connect with them when you don't believe they're even real. Like when my mom pretended to listen to me when I was young, but even less excusable (from the inner child's view) because she was not asking for my time when she did that. So, it's worth meditating first on why it feels fake.

[303] ### For some people it only stops feeling fake in an altered state

[304] Like on MDMA or deep in meditation. All of a sudden your little one is right there! Oh my gosh! And the love or grief that sounded like therapy talk is just obvious. Old memories might come up too. They can feel totally real, but that alone doesn't prove they happened. I'd still hang on to that connection you felt, even once it's gone, and try to bring a little of it into your normal life. You've felt that your little one is real now, so talking to them afterward isn't just pretending. If you're "lucky", it's not a question of trying to maintain that connection, but trying to figure out how to deal with the rawness of it after the high wears off. That's what integration therapy is for. I have more advice on using psychedelics with the therapy in the "Altered States Can Deepen Therapy or Escapism" section further on.

[305] ## Sometimes There Isn't a Clear Child Yet

[306] Reparenting assumes there's a clear little one in there to love, and for a lot of people there is. But if you grew up somewhere you only belonged as long as you played somebody else, like a really controlling family or a strict church, the real one may never have had room to form. All this stuff about your little one could have left you wondering who I'm even talking about.

[307] Maybe you started playing that part so early, and it took over so completely, that "What do I believe?" or "What do I want?" has no clear answer of your own. They sound like such easy questions, too. Back then, asking might have gotten you in trouble, or laughed at. Or there was just no way to ask. That can make "love yourself" feel pretty empty (love what, when the part you've been playing doesn't feel real?), and the authentic self never received the conditions needed to develop. We get deeper into finding and building that self in "Untangling the Belonging Bargains" further on.

[308] ## When the Practice Feels Real but Stays Shallow

[309] Some people have the opposite problem, where it feels like they're connecting with their little boo, but then they find out somehow that it was still pretty superficial. Dreams can open up much more emotionally direct stuff, especially in the second half of the night's sleep, if you wake up in the middle to meditate, as I've discovered. That's a good point to ponder over what the dream let you feel that the session didn't, and look at it again in the morning (but write it down so you remember!). And if nightmares start wrecking your sleep, shorten the practice or do it earlier.

[310] ## When Healing Turns Into Checking

[311] You can get hooked on healing too. The wound can be completely real while you're checking it for the hundredth time to see whether you've finally grieved it correctly.

[312] Going back to reconnect with your little one is normal parenting/reparenting, but returning to lick the same wound over and over can become a search for certainty in an inherently uncertain existence. Spending all day asking, "Did I process it properly? Did I grieve it enough?" is clearly not what we're after here.

[313] And the checking can take many forms, aside from inner mental checks. A body scan can turn into one, or a confession. Even going on a date, or checking your dating profile matches, can be a version of this if what you're really after is proof that you're good and lovable. It might calm things down for a while, and then the next little wobble matters even more.

[314] If you're feeling guilty because you didn't put your seat belt on, just put it on. If you still want to investigate it later, fine. Perhaps now and then you feel bad about that time you refused to put it on, broke your collar bone in a crash, and then got blamed for not wearing it. That's natural, but doesn't mean there's something new to look at.

[315] ## How Would You Know It's Helping?

[316] Don't be afraid to ask your therapist/yourself, "What is this practice supposed to help, and how would I know if it's helping?" This sovereign, gently curious stance should apply to everything in life.

[317] Feeling calmer or having deeper insights or explanations for the trauma response are some possible signs of initial progress, but if that's the endgame, it's just a cope. Real healing is ultimately measured by reduced need for the therapy.

[318] It's like real parenting: you might start out reading parenting books and thinking hard about what to say or do, but after a while you get into the groove and it becomes natural. Inner parenting can be like that too. You don't need to keep checking this guide or planning every conversation with your little one; the nurturing, protecting and guiding become more automatic. You might still turn to therapy when you need it, and life will keep offering chances to grow wiser and more compassionate.

[319] And if the therapy is actually working, it should improve your daily life, and especially the health of the inner child-inner adult relationship. Going at it harder isn't as useful as going at it with more wisdom and compassion. Take the parts that work, and innovate on the rest.

[320] If these exercises start feeling like a ritual you have to complete before you're allowed to live, stop there.

[321] ## When to Change the Strategy

[322] Weeks in and nothing much is changing? I'd want to quit too, or else keep doing the exact same thing forever so I never have to find out what's underneath this. Before going with either of those paths, look for what exactly is in the way.

[323] ### How many tries would I give it?

[324] It's not like we can just put a number on it. Some things shift in a day. Others take about as long as learning guitar. But do notice if progress is stagnating. If it's making things worse, or you just can't do it right now, I'd change course sooner.

[325] ### If you keep following the reparenting steps correctly without getting the expected results

[326] Consider if you have a unique case these steps don't match yet. Maybe you'll find something that works better for you, or you can ask me what I'd do for this case, or ask a therapist you work with. Either way, I'd like to hear about it! Leave a comment here in the article.

[327] ### If you'd rather stop

[328] Not wanting to do it anymore might be reason enough, but if that "nope" sounds like a part of you that's trying to protect you, I'd hear it out first to get a better understanding.

[329] ## When the Urge to Escape Arrives

[330] The avoidant guard shows up when you get close to some painful memory or insight. Suddenly you'd rather have a drink, check your phone, or do anything aside from directly feeling it (even a yawn can come with an "I'm so done with this" feeling). Maybe this guard doesn't believe there's a grown-up around who could handle what's coming? That yawn might actually be the opposite of checking out, though. Re-evaluation Counseling counts yawning as productive emotional discharge, a feeling on its way out.

[331] I've found many times that simply staying with the feelings in a meditative way is miraculously transformative. One time, my second wife, Bee, was yelling at me in the kitchen for quite a while, about some kind of accusations that didn't make much sense to me. I was feeling defensive, but instead of doing or saying something in my defense, on this occasion, I visualized my energetic shield disappearing, and I imagined her negative energy passing right through me. All that clenching relaxed, and it became a peak spiritual experience, where I felt like a conduit for some massive energy flow. My giant smile didn't seem to help her feel better, though. I can't say either of us learned how to integrate the lesson from that in a way that made practical sense for our relationship, although I do believe there's immense freedom there to harness.

[332] ## Physical Pain Makes It Harder

[333] Physical pain can bring on the urge to escape too. Say an attack has you curled up on the bathroom floor, and whatever knocked the pain out last time (say, fent, or ketamine) starts looking real good, though you know it'll leave you worse if you keep resorting to that quick fix.

[334] By now, you may have already been through a million doctors who shrugged their shoulders, or even alternative treatments that were too costly or hard to grade. Sometimes it really is so simple that the best doctors and scanners can't figure it out, like your house is moldy. It is worth going through all the basic checks first that you can do yourself, and trying the things that make most sense. For more on that, check my detox guide.

[335] And when the symptoms come and go randomly, the best time to get diagnostics done could be right at the actual time of the crisis, rather than waiting until you feel well enough to make an appointment, and they may be able to treat it while it's happening. But what if the attacks come almost every day? You can't move into the ER. And normal test results don't prove the pain is imaginary, just as a treatment that helps doesn't explain what's causing it.

[336] I'd ask for a chronic pain specialist and a written plan for the flare-ups you can see coming, including the new or severe signs that mean you go to the ER right away, but don't let amelioration of symptoms be the end of the investigation.

[340] ## Altered States Can Deepen Therapy or Escapism

[341] When the physical or mental pain is intense and impenetrable, the urge usually sits on the fence between escapism and doing hardcore battle with the demons. Why not just blast right past that guard part, with intensive meditation or psychedelics? Sometimes that can help tremendously, but both can get you past the guards before there's a grown-up big enough to hold whatever comes through, so I'd hold off until there's enough Nurturer and Protector around. How much is enough depends partly on the medicine and what therapy is done with it. Borrowed is fine, as long as it's from someone steady who won't try to run your trip for you.

[342] I'd learn to do inner-child therapy sober first, in your ordinary mindstate, since that's the one you'll be living in. Cars don't start at 60mph, that's not the way the universe seems to work. First gear leads to second leads to third, etc. Second gear, in this case, may refer to gentle meditation or self-hypnosis. [343] Low doses of entheogens can also synergize compatibly with various gears. Like MDMA, female iboga, Loveyhuasca, certain mushrooms, like Orissa India, and San Pedro cactus (esp. with sublingual mapacho + full spectrum CBD tea) are more protective and nurturing than many other medicines, especially at lower doses, so you don't need as much Nurturer and Protector built up for those, though you still need some.
