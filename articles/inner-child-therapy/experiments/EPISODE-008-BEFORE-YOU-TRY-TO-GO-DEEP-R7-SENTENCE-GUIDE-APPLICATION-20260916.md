# Before You Try to Go Deep

If you feel so much like the scared kid that even an ordinary pause or choice has disappeared, this is not the moment to go deeper. Get somebody steady around you now; the inner-child conversation can wait until you aren't completely inside it.

## You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue

If you don't hear a little kid talking in your head, don't invent one. Try making one silent sentence on purpose, even if sentences almost never pop up by themselves. If it comes, use it. If it doesn't, forget the voice idea and work another way.

When I say “ask the child,” try words only if they actually work. Say it out loud or write it down. If that goes nowhere, a drawing, picture, or song may get closer. Sometimes all you get is a body feeling or an urge. And sometimes there isn't an answer to decode at all; the caring thing you do next is the answer. Not having much inner speech doesn't mean you're supposed to compensate with vivid pictures. A quiet mind may simply be quiet.

Feelings and words don't have to show up in a particular order. Sometimes you know which came first and sometimes you don't. If words come later, they can help you check whether your first explanation really fits and decide what to do next; later thinking still counts even if the feeling arrived first.

## Maybe Calm the Body First

Sometimes you have plenty of words but your body is too wound up for them to help.

If your solar plexus, abdomen, or somewhere else feels clenched and it's hard to relax into that heart-chakra love, try a slower, non-forced exhale or some gentle massage. I love using one foot to massage the other like a Yin/Yang symbol. Circular or spiral massage over the tense area can be nice too.

Sometimes you need more bottom-up help than that: Shaking, tapping (Emotional Freedom Technique), EMDR, Brainspotting, Somatic Experiencing, Water Therapy, Massage, Energy Healing, or another approach that works from the body upward.

If your body is still too clenched for talking to work, use the roadmap below first and come back to the conversation afterward.

### Catch the Hook Before the Story Takes Over

You may feel the hook before you know whether it's the child, a protector, or something old. Maybe your jaw clenches, your belly pulls tight, heat comes up, or you suddenly want out. Sometimes the clue arrives late: half the text is already written or your hand is already going for the drink. If the momentum keeps going, you can find yourself attacking, disappearing, making a promise, or deciding the whole relationship before you even know how you got there. [Pema Chödrön](https://pemachodronfoundation.org/product/taking-the-leap-book/) uses the Tibetan word *shenpa* for exactly this tightening and urge that starts before the whole story has finished forming.

Once you catch it, look around and say, “I'm hooked.” If nobody needs protection right now, take one to three breaths before acting. Stay with the charge for those breaths instead of using them to decide the relationship or whether you're right. Then ask what the adult can actually do right now. Does the kid need some warmth, does somebody need protection, or do you just need enough direction to choose one next move? Do the smallest version available. If none of that is available, waiting is the adult move; let the charge come down and get on with your day.

For ten minutes, don't send the text, take the drink, make the promise, blurt out the thing, or decide the relationship. If something actually unsafe is happening, deal with that instead.

At that point, all you know for sure is that you're activated. Leave the verdict for later. When you've come down, check what the other person actually did and what old material got pulled in.

When you're less activated, go back to the actual event. Somebody can cross a line now and wake up an old child-state or protector at the same time. There may be a boundary to set now. The older part may turn out to be grief, something unfinished that needs repair, or care you never received. Start with what happened before deciding what it means. “My stomach tightened” is something you noticed. “That person is evil” is a judgment. What did they actually do? Does what they did really support that conclusion, or could something else fit? If literal words were in your head at the time, keep them with the event. Words you find later can still help you understand it, but those are the later translation.

One breath is enough if that's all you can do. If turning inward makes you less oriented, less able to function, or more compelled, stop and get support from outside yourself.

### Don't Assume It's All You

If every conflict gets turned into “this is my wound,” the other person disappears from the picture. Sometimes they're actually doing something that matters. When you disagree, do they ever look at what they did and make room for your side, or does the conversation keep ending with you managing their feelings and dropping your own position?

[Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity is useful here because it gets you out of motive-guessing for a minute: watch what the person does over and over. Sometimes you've already explained enough.

Anybody can have one terrible conversation. They may just need time, or be holding a boundary of their own. The question is what happens next time, and next time after that. If the same thing keeps happening, that's what you actually have to live with. You may not know whether they truly can't do something or simply won't; sometimes all you know is that they didn't do it today, which means it wasn't there to rely on today. Let changed behavior change your assessment. A promise isn't changed behavior, and you don't need one more injury before protecting yourself.

Feeling guilty isn't enough to tell you what happened. You may really have hurt somebody and owe a repair while also giving up your own position because you can't stand their disappointment. And the guilt itself doesn't prove they're manipulating you.

Before you go back, decide the one thing this conversation actually needs to accomplish. If being understood isn't happening, maybe the whole job is to say no and end it. If one point genuinely needs to get across, say that one point. You don't have to defend yourself for forty minutes just because the other person still doesn't understand.

While you're talking, the Witness watches what's happening inside you and the Protector watches what the other person is actually doing. The Guide only needs to decide what you do next.

### Write It. Don't Send It Yet.

Write the response exactly as it comes—or just say it in your head. Don't send it yet.

Then read it again as the adult who's on the writer's side. Fix what is unfair or confusing. If you really did something wrong, keep that in. Don't edit away the part that says what hurt, what you're asking for, or what needs to stop just because the first version was angry. Better doesn't necessarily mean softer. Then decide whether any of it should be sent. Maybe after editing there is much less to send; maybe it needs to wait. If you tell them you'll answer tomorrow, actually answer tomorrow. If somebody needs protection now, don't wait on the exercise.

Then read the first draft again and stop fixing it. What was that person trying so hard to make somebody understand? What did they need from you in that moment—maybe protection, reassurance, or practical help? The first version isn't automatically childish. The angry draft may be the first place you actually said what was wrong or what needed to stop.

### When Healing Turns Into Checking

You can get hooked on healing too. The wound can be real and you can still be checking it for the hundredth time, trying to find out whether you've finally grieved it correctly.

Take care of whatever safety issue or practical thing is actually there. Then ask one question before doing another round: did the last one actually move anything? Maybe a feeling finally became real instead of only intellectual, or you saw something that changed a decision or how you respond. If you're back at the same question only because the answer still doesn't feel certain enough, that's different. Grief can come back to familiar ground without every return being a new request from the child.

If it's checking, leave the thought unfinished and go back to the person, activity, or life you chose. The thought can still be there; you don't have to settle it before going back to what you were doing. Caring for the child doesn't require a meeting every time the mind says something.

What was the exercise supposed to change? Compare that with what actually changed. You can leave calmer and full of insight while the original problem hasn't moved. If that keeps happening—or you're mostly monitoring yourself more while daily life gets worse—stop repeating the same exercise. Keep the part that genuinely helps and meet the remaining need another way.

If you notice you can't get on with your day until you've completed the exercise, that's the point to stop.
