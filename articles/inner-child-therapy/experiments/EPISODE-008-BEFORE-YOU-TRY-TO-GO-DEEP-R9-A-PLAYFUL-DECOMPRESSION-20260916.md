# Before You Try to Go Deep

If you're so far inside the scared kid that the grown-up who's supposed to be doing this exercise is barely in the room, the whole idea of “going deeper” gets a little strange. It's like handing the steering wheel to a five-year-old and then asking them to navigate home. This is not the moment to dig deeper. Get somebody steady around you first, and come back when there's at least a little more grown-up you available.

## You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue

If you don't hear a little kid talking in your head, don't invent one. Inner-child instructions can sound pretty weird when they quietly assume everybody has a tiny Pixar committee behind the forehead, complete with speech bubbles, and some people simply don't.

One thing you can actually test is whether you can deliberately think one silent sentence right now, even if sentences almost never pop up by themselves. You may discover that spontaneous words are rare while intentional words are still available. And when I say “ask the child,” that's all I need the word *ask* to mean: give the question some form and see what happens. Say it out loud or write it down if that feels natural.

If the verbal route gives you silence, putting it on paper can sometimes get you closer—a drawing, a picture, a song—but other times the only thing that shows up is in the body: a feeling becomes obvious, an urge gets louder, something in you leans one way instead of another. You don't have to interrogate it until it turns into English. Sometimes the caring thing you do next is the only answer you're going to get. And if your mind is quiet in words, don't assume vivid pictures are supposed to appear and save the exercise; a quiet internal screen isn't evidence that you're resisting.

The timing gets messy too. A feeling can already be there before language catches up. Another time you hear yourself think something and only then notice what it stirred, and later you may not honestly know which one came first. Words that arrive afterward can still help you look back at the feeling and ask whether the first explanation really fits, and using them later isn't automatically avoidance just because they weren't there first. They can help with what you do next too. A feeling isn't automatically truer because it arrived before the words.

## Maybe Calm the Body First

If your solar plexus, abdomen, or somewhere else feels clenched and it's hard to relax into that heart-chakra love, try a slower, non-forced exhale or some gentle massage. I love using one foot to massage the other like a Yin/Yang symbol. Circular or spiral massage over the tense area can be nice too.

Sometimes you need more bottom-up help than that: Shaking, tapping (Emotional Freedom Technique), EMDR, Brainspotting, Somatic Experiencing, Water Therapy, Massage, Energy Healing, or another approach that works from the body upward.

Sometimes the words are all there and they're doing absolutely nothing. You can explain the problem beautifully and the body is still just as clenched. Sometimes the sequence really is body first, talking later, so stay with the body-first work in the roadmap below for a while and come back to dialogue when talking actually reaches something.

### Catch the Hook Before the Story Takes Over

You may feel the hook before you know whether it's the child, a protector, or something old. Often the ridiculous part is how late you notice. Your jaw clenches or your belly pulls tight, maybe heat comes up or you suddenly want out, and meanwhile your thumb is already three paragraphs into the text message. On another day your hand has gone for the drink, or you've been scrolling and explaining things in your head long enough to forget what started it. Once that momentum is moving, attacking, disappearing, making a promise, or deciding the whole relationship can feel like the obvious next thing. [Pema Chödrön](https://pemachodronfoundation.org/product/taking-the-leap-book/) calls this tightening and urge before the story has finished forming *shenpa*.

Once you catch it, look around and say, “I'm hooked.” If nobody needs protection right now, take one to three breaths before acting. You're only making a tiny patch of time between the urge and whatever was already loading. The Supreme Court of My Relationship can stay adjourned for three breaths.

During those breaths, see whether one adult thing is actually reachable. If the kid needs warmth, start there. Maybe somebody needs protecting. Sometimes there's only enough Guide left to choose one next move, and that is still something. You don't need all three adult roles online at once. Use what is genuinely available; if none of it is, wait rather than pretending you're more adult than you are, and if the charge comes down, get on with your day.

For ten minutes, let the text sit in drafts instead of finishing it or hitting Send. The drink can wait too, along with the thing you were about to blurt out, promise, or decide about the whole relationship. If something is actually unsafe, deal with that now.

When you're less activated, go back to the actual event. This part is less dramatic and more useful: what actually happened? Start with what the other person did. Being hooked tells you that something in you lit up; it doesn't settle whether they crossed a line. And sometimes they did. Somebody can cross a line now and wake up an old child-state or protector at the same time.

If there is a boundary to set in the present, let that question stay in the present. The older layer may take longer to understand. It may be grief, something unfinished that needs repair, or care you never got, and none of that has to be solved in the same minute as the boundary.

Start with what happened before deciding what it means. “My stomach tightened” is something you noticed. “That person is evil” is a judgment. What did they actually do? Let the answer sit there for a moment before you improve it into a theory. Does what they did really support the conclusion, or could something else fit?

If literal words were in your head at the time, keep them with the event. They're part of the original footage. Words you find later can still help you understand what happened, but they're subtitles added in post-production.

One breath is enough if that's all you can do. If turning inward is making you more disoriented, making everyday functioning worse, or leaving an urge harder to step away from, stop and get support from outside yourself.

### Don't Assume It's All You

If every conflict gets turned into “this is my wound,” the other person disappears from the picture. The whole relationship problem suddenly has one character—your nervous system—while what the other person actually did slips offstage.

Look at what actually happens when you disagree. Maybe you bring up the thing that hurt, they get upset, and ten minutes later you're comforting them while your original complaint has somehow evaporated. How did you become customer support for the person you were complaining to? Can they ever look at what they did, take responsibility for some part of it, and make room for your side without the conversation turning back into their distress?

[Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity keeps pulling attention back to repeated behavior instead of the endless game of solving somebody's motives. Watch what they actually do over and over. Sometimes you've already explained enough.

One terrible conversation doesn't tell you much by itself. Maybe they need time; maybe what feels like rejection to you is actually a boundary they're allowed to have. The harder part is when the same ending keeps showing up. You can go in circles trying to solve whether they *can't* meet you there or just *won't*, and sometimes the honest answer is: who knows? What you do know is that it wasn't available today. If it keeps being unavailable, that's the relationship you're actually living in. Change your assessment if their behavior changes. A promise that it will change isn't changed behavior, and you don't need one more injury before protecting yourself.

Guilt by itself doesn't settle what happened. Maybe you did hurt them, and maybe you owe an apology or some repair. You can owe them something and still be abandoning yourself at the same time because their disappointment feels unbearable. The guilt doesn't settle that question either, and it doesn't prove they're manipulating you.

Then there is the urge to make them understand. You explain it one more way, and then another, because surely if they finally see it the whole thing can settle. Sometimes that understanding simply isn't available in this conversation, and you can still act without winning it. Before you go back in, ask what the conversation actually has to accomplish. Maybe one point needs to get across. Maybe the whole job is saying no and leaving. While you're talking, the Witness can keep an eye on what is happening inside you, the Protector can keep an eye on what the other person is actually doing, and the Guide only has to choose the next move. None of them need a forty-minute closing argument.

### Write It. Don't Send It Yet.

Write the response exactly as it comes—or just say it in your head. Don't send it yet.

Read it again as the adult who's on the writer's side, but actually do the edit. Find the sentence you know is unfair, or the part nobody could understand unless they were already inside your head, and fix that. Then look at the angry line you most want to soften. Is it true? If it says *this hurt*, don't delete that just to sound more evolved. If you really did something wrong, don't delete that either. Keep the responsibility that belongs to you and keep the protest, request, or boundary that belongs to you. A better message can still be a hard no.

After that, decide whether anything is worth sending. Often there's much less of it by then. It can wait, and sometimes nothing needs to go out yet. If you tell them you'll answer tomorrow, answer tomorrow. If somebody needs protection now, don't postpone that while you perfect the draft.

Then read the first draft again and stop fixing it. This is easy to skip because now you have the better message and it feels as if the job is finished. But the furious version is still sitting there like a note somebody shoved under a door. What was that person trying so hard to make somebody understand? Read the ugly sentence again before answering. Maybe what they needed from the adult in you starts to show up there—protection, reassurance, some actual help, whatever it is. Sometimes the angry draft is the first place *this hurt* or *this needs to stop* appears without being immediately talked out of existence.

### When Healing Turns Into Checking

You can get hooked on healing too. The wound can be real while you're checking for the hundredth time whether you've finally grieved it correctly, then checking whether you're still checking, and maybe checking whether checking the checking means you haven't healed enough yet. The loop can get ridiculous without the original pain being fake, and another round of analysis isn't automatically another act of care.

Take care of whatever safety issue or practical thing is actually there. Then, before opening another session, think about the last one after you got up and went back to your life. Did anything follow you out of the room? Maybe a feeling that had only been an idea became real enough that you handled something differently later. Maybe a decision genuinely shifted because you saw something you hadn't seen before. That's different from having a powerful hour and then reopening the same question because you need the answer to feel certain one more time. Grief really can circle back over familiar ground, so repetition alone doesn't tell you which one is happening; it also doesn't automatically mean the child has brought you a new request.

If it is checking, leave the thought unfinished and go back to the person, activity, or life you chose. The thought can still be there while you go eat dinner; it doesn't have to be settled first. Caring for the child doesn't require a meeting every time the mind says something.

Sometimes these sessions leave you calmer, full of insight, maybe even feeling as if something profound happened—and the original problem is sitting in exactly the same chair when you get back. Stranger still is getting better and better at monitoring yourself while everyday life gets worse. If that keeps happening, don't automatically do another round just because the session itself feels meaningful. Keep whatever genuinely helps and meet the remaining need another way. And if you can't get on with the day until you've completed the exercise, the exercise itself has joined the checking loop.
