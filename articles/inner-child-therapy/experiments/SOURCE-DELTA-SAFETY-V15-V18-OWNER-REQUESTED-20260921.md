## V15 — safety is not the same as adult capacity

> ## When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child
>
> Being able to look around the room, slow down, and make a choice is not the same thing as being safe for the child. If you still enjoy frightening or exploiting vulnerability—or you want therapy to make you better at it—don't bring the child into that. Not even just to see what happens. The child's fear should not have to prove anything for you.
>
> I also wouldn't decide somebody is dangerous because they have an ugly thought, a strange belief, a religious identity, anger, or something harmful in their past. What they endorse now matters much more. And if somebody says they're sovereign but also says they have to obey an urge, doctrine, group, spirit, or master, I don't need to win an argument about what that means. Can they pause? Can they refuse? Can they change their mind?
>
> If the answer is still no—or the answer to “what do you want to do with vulnerability?” is still harm—stay with the adult. Work there. Maybe the useful question is whether this identity or urge is actually giving you the life you wanted, and whether you even want something different. Someone helping can care without flattering cruelty or helping the person manipulate better.
>
> A later promise isn't enough reason to bring the child back in. Let change become believable through repeated choices, especially when exploiting vulnerability would still feel rewarding. Until then, keep the child out of the exercise, including the indirect versions. Sometimes a steady human relationship is the work.

## V16 — don't use the child as a lie detector

> ## When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child
>
> Don't use the child as a lie detector for the adult. If you already know the present-day person enjoys humiliating, frightening, or exploiting vulnerability, bringing the child forward “just to see” is not neutral. Now the child's fear has become part of the experiment, and possibly part of what the adult enjoys.
>
> That does not mean scary-looking inner material tells you the same thing. People can have bizarre beliefs, intrusive thoughts, anger, unwanted fantasies, or a harmful past without wanting to hurt anyone now. I care about what they actually endorse and choose. If they say some urge, doctrine, group, spirit, or master has to be obeyed, then the interesting question is very simple: can they disobey it?
>
> If not, keep the work with the adult. What do they want help with? Is the identity they are protecting making their life better, or just making the harm easier to justify? You don't have to flatter cruelty to stay in relationship with someone, and you don't have to debate their theology either.
>
> Don't reopen child work because someone says the right words. Let the evidence be what happens over time, including when exploiting somebody vulnerable would be satisfying. Until then, no child contact by the front door or a side door. Real human care may be more useful than another internal exercise.

## V17 — sovereignty starts with the ability to refuse

> ## When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child
>
> “Sovereign” is an easy word. The useful part is whether you can say no—to an urge, a group, a doctrine, a spirit, a master, whatever it is. If you can't pause, question it, and refuse it, the label doesn't do much for me.
>
> There's a separate issue I care about more here: what do you want to do with vulnerability? If the answer is frighten it, humiliate it, exploit it, or get better at manipulating it, don't bring the child forward. The child isn't there to test whether you mean it, and their fear isn't evidence you get to collect.
>
> That boundary shouldn't turn into a purity test. An intrusive thought is not intent. Neither is anger, a strange belief, a religious label, an unwanted fantasy, or even the fact that you did something harmful before. Someone can look alarming and not currently intend harm; someone else can sound very composed and still intend it. So stay with what is actually endorsed now.
>
> If harm is still endorsed, the work is with the adult life around it. Is this identity giving you what you thought it would? Do you want anything to change? Let credibility grow from choices, not declarations. Until then the child stays out of the process, directly or indirectly. A steady human being who can care without enabling the harm may be more important than another exercise.

## V18 — behavior over promises

> ## When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child
>
> A promise doesn't make somebody safe for the child. If the adult still likes exploiting or frightening vulnerability, “I won't do it here” is not much to build on. What I would trust more is what happens when exploiting vulnerability would still be rewarding and they choose not to.
>
> Until that pattern exists, keep the child out of it. I mean actually out of it—not brought forward for a reaction, not smuggled in through hypnosis, imagery, or a spiritual figure. The child doesn't need to become the evidence that the adult has changed.
>
> At the same time, I don't want this turned into a test for whether somebody has the right beliefs or thoughts. Weird religion, intrusive thoughts, anger, unwanted fantasies, past harm: none of that answers the question. What are you choosing now? And when you say you “have to” obey an urge, doctrine, group, spirit, or master, can you stop and refuse?
>
> If not, that's plenty to work on without involving the child. What is this identity doing for your life? What do you actually want help with? Somebody can stay kind and curious without flattering cruelty or helping you do it better. Sometimes that relationship with a real person is more useful than another trip inside.
