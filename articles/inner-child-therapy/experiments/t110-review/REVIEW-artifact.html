P2Welcome, lovey bunnies!

P3Here's the idea behind Inner Child Reparenting, in a nutshell:

P4Growing up, all of us picked up automatic ways of understanding situations we find ourselves in, reacting based on those understandings, and then treating ourselves partly based on how we happened to handle things. Some of those patterns are wise in general, although even then, we are always learning from what life throws at us. Others of those patterns were built for a crisis that’s long over, and some just seem to make every situation worse.

P5Reparenting means consciously evolving and molding an Inner Adult inside you, comprising a loving Nurturer, a safe Adult Protector (hereafter just the Protector) and a wise Guide. This chosen Inner Adult super hero gets into a real, loving relationship with the little one you still carry. That grown-up notices the old patterns and practices better ways of responding. At first, it’s deliberate, even awkward. With time, the roles become second nature, so you spend less of your life managing yourself and more of it living with purpose, loving, relaxing, and playing.

P6If that got to you at all, good. That was half the idea. I used to listen to Suzanne Vega's The Queen and the Soldier every night because it reliably got the tears out.

P7The crying helped me a lot in terms of not going numb inside. It didn't help me develop insight, though. I could have kept crying forever. It was Buddhism and then inner child therapy which helped create a new perspective that was more healthy. But I'm really happy I did do the crying that was needed at the time.

P8Music can help with feeling love, not just sadness, although sadness is often the first step to open up. So if you sit down to try any of this and feel nothing, that's when I'd put a song on. Céline's right up there if you don't have one, and Rosey Grier's It's All Right to Cry is a little further down. My sister used to sing that one to me almost daily in such a silly way it was really sweet of her.

P9Some people, like another ex of mine, got so deep into crying that it became traumatizing. She actually had panic attacks from it. If you start getting scared of your own crying, turn the song off for tonight.

The Chicken-and-Egg Problem

P1Céline's song is called On ne change pas—"we don't change." I think that's both fortunately and unfortunately true for most of us. Fortunately in the sense that the sweet, innocent, curious, vivacious little one is still inside us, even if hiding. Unfortunate in the sense that we often don't truly develop the adult qualities that we might pretend to have, so we continue burdening that little one inside us with troubles they aren't made for handling. This shows up in how we may feel abandoned, helpless, impulsive, frightened, or desperate to be chosen.

P2And I realized that it doesn’t make sense to ask the child to heal itself, which is really a disguised request for the child to abandon its own nature and turn into an adult. I call that "forced adulthood," where the child has to deal with situations the evolved inner adult should be handling--we see this in actual children all the time when their parents are not providing care. People even wear t-shirts saying, "I don't wanna adult today." The forced adulting often goes along with a misperception that there is something wrong with the inner child. Instead, we need to separately develop the qualities of a good inner parent for that rightly vulnerable child: the loving Nurturer, safe Protector, and wise Guide.

P3That’s one grown-up doing three jobs, not three more people moving in to help with the rent, and each job keeps the others in balance. Say a party gets to be too much for your little one. The Protector gets you both out of there, and on the way home the Nurturer tells them they did great, even though mostly they hid in the bathroom. And the Guide helps them think twice about the next weird party they might go to, avoiding another cycle of bathroom vacationing. 🙈

P4The Guide is the one people misunderstand the most. I left it out of my first version of this guide until my friend Key pointed it out, and without an inner Guide, it’s easy to go looking for one in a cult. Our lives can become small and like a never-ending therapy session when we focus only on caring for ourselves rather than giving ourselves a mission as well. That’s the Guide’s job. But on the other hand, some people over-rely on their discipline or their mission to avoid caring for themselves.

P5If the phrase “inner parent” calls up the wrong archetype for you, pick another one. Your future self might work, or the captain of a ship, so long as they’d do those three jobs.

P6But here’s the chicken-and-egg problem. What if you’re so far inside the child-state that you can’t become the adult? This is much easier when you can already reach one of those adult roles. But when you can't, it's like you sit there trying to be loving while your mind is becoming controlled again by the inherited critic voice, or you start going numb, throwing a fit, or panicking.

P7Sometimes somebody else may have to do one part of the adult role with you or for you first. You get to feel what that is like and watch how they do it. Maybe you copy one small thing you've seen them do, or imagine them there when they're not, and try one piece yourself while they're still helping with the rest. That's what I mean by borrowed adulthood. You may still need help with almost all of it for a while, but over time there are more and more of those little parts you can do yourself.

Five Stages of Reparenting

P1For now, I think of the whole path in five fairly predictable stages. You might be starting at any point here (and it may change even from hour to hour as well):

1.You’re still stuck identifying as the lost inner child, forced frequently into handling situations that require more maturity, even if it looks like you’ve got it all together. At this stage, use your childlike superpowers to come into the “Wow of Now” (have you seen the movie Waking Life?), and make sure there’s enough safety around you to continue with reparenting: 2. Begin to borrow adulthood — remember watching Mr. Rogers or Sesame Street? Those adult role models are what we're looking for at this stage. This can almost feel like playing house as a kid where you imagined how you'd take care of a baby doll etc. 3. Practice adulting — do at least one part of caring for yourself while support is still there from the previous step. 4. Adult mode itself — after sufficient practice with reparenting, you become a natural at sending love to little you, protecting them, and directing them. 5. Adult and child in relationship — the adult leads. The child isn't pushed away, but doesn't have to run the whole life alone. This can actually feel really weird if you've been stuck in forced adulthood for a while. Our goal is to go from weird to silly and playful. Lose self-consciousness while gaining self-awareness, if that makes sense!

P2Whichever stage you're at, saying the right thing when you sit down with your little one helps, but you also have to start actually doing some of it in the rest of your life. Your little one starts believing the grown-up you when they catch you lying down because you're wiped out, instead of pushing through one more chore.

P3Reparenting isn't an exercise you finish, after all. Say your boss asks you to stay late again. You tell them no and go home feeling okay about it, and nobody had to cry on a meditation cushion for that to happen. The cushion was practice for it.

My Journey

P1I used to preach Buddhist enlightenment too much, because that's what helped me. But an old childhood wound could still hit, like if someone accuses me of being lazy, and then suddenly it's like I'm that same little kid again, even tho the spiritual part of me felt free as a bird.

P2From what I've seen, most people (like me) who chase spiritual freedom, as valid as that is, are, at least at first, trying to get away from something painful in themselves, usually childhood trauma of some kind. That’s spiritual bypassing. Plenty of gurus do it too; I’ve written about the Dalai Lama as one example.

P3The way I think this works is simple. Imagine you’re broke and starving, and your baby is starving too. Understandable: there is no food. Then a food bank opens nearby and suddenly you’ve got lots of nutritious munchies. But you’re eating all of it yourself while baby you is still starving. Unforgivable.

P4Nobody leaves a baby starving on purpose. I just didn’t believe there was a baby. Inner-child stuff sounded silly to me until I could actually feel him. Then there was this little me actually hurting, and I could send some of the love I’d already found his way.

P5Spending your whole life processing your wounded inner child isn’t freedom either. Spirituality opened a much bigger horizon for me—love and happiness beyond the conditions of my life story. Maybe ordinary life already gives you enough love and meaning. Great. It didn’t for me. Conditional love felt radically insufficient, so I kept looking for something deeper.

P6I was well-loved as a child, but from age twelve to twenty-six I was depressed and cried every night from feeling the pain of the world. Loving-kindness (metta) meditation healed that depression almost instantly for me, so it became my regular practice automatically.

P7I also found more love in the rest of the Buddhist teachings—which, it turns out, also include inner-child self-love! and later in loveyhuasca.

P8But it didn’t reach everything. A lot of the child stuff around my attachment to women was still there. When I later tried self-directed inner-child therapy, it felt like I was making it up at first. That didn’t stop me from practicing, but I did stop “pushing” it to be a certain way, and I’d stop trying to feel it was realer than it was. I would go more into listening and waiting mode rather than imagining mode.

P9At one pivotal point, I used San Pedro cactus and CBD tea with sublingual mapacho, and that cracked it open.

P10Years later, I found a warm, intuitive inner child therapist, named Daja, randomly, while in Kenya. I figured I already knew this stuff, but I gave it a shot. She did actually help me see some things more clearly. For example, she had me write down how many people I could fully trust as a child (lots), then how many people I could fully trust as an adult (zero). That was difficult, and it’s actually the opposite of what you might expect from inner-child therapy, since usually that’s for people who had problems trusting as a child.

P11What it showed me is that I was ignoring a lot of things that were really important to look at, and that was one of them. I wouldn’t have even thought to ask the question she asked me if I were doing it on my own. It’s also an example of how therapy can sometimes make you feel worse than before, when you become more aware of problems. But personally I prefer to see things as they are, so I can figure out how to work with them, rather than just hide in delusions.

P12It also showed me that I really do need to count more on myself than I assumed. I need to be much better as an inner adult for my inner child, because my childhood was actually so nice that I kind of just assume I can trust everyone, and that gets me into trouble. So it got me curious to learn more about inner-child therapy, which is a process I’m still in the midst of as I test this guide with people.

Don’t Give the Inner Adult Away

P1If you still feel like a kid inside, somebody who seems grown-up can feel like salvation, and letting them do some of the grown-up jobs for you can actually help for a while. P3Even Pema Chödrön, who inspired parts of this guide, comes from a tradition where devotion to a teacher is a big deal, and she still says a teacher should “wean us from dependency altogether” and help us grow up.

P4Say you grew up learning that what you wanted didn't matter. Now you're only just finding out what you actually think, maybe even learning to say no. Then somebody tells you there is no self. That might be freeing at another point in your life; right now it can sound a lot like the message you already know: what you want doesn't matter. Or you tell a therapist, “No, that's not right for me,” and they call it resistance. Maybe they're right. But maybe they're wrong about you, or taking you somewhere you don't want to go. You still have to be able to say so.

Signs of Manipulation

P1Some gurus really do demand surrender, and they're the opposite of the Guide: they point you at themselves (so, more or less, they'd like to be your God). And sometimes they never have to ask. Someone seems to get your little one like nobody ever has, or treats it like it's special, and it starts thinking, "They know me better than I know myself."

P2Some manipulative people are too smart to act perfect. They lead with their wounds, or with how brutally honest they are about their flaws (very disarming, which is sort of the point), and every warning you hear about them just becomes more proof of how deep they are. Your Protector has to catch the moment compassion starts explaining away what you've already seen. "I have honesty issues and I'm working on them" may be a perfectly honest thing to say. It's also telling you there's an honesty problem right now, and knowing about a problem isn't fixing it.

P3You can't spot love bombing by how much affection there is, so watch what it's doing to your head (are the red flags from last week starting to look kind of cute?). A group can do it too with praise, until someone's an exception to rules you'd use with anybody else. I'd take a few days to check the facts before deciding anything big.

P4A joke can carry a request, like "Ha, I'll just borrow your card, kidding... unless?" If saying yes would cost you money, I'd answer it like they'd asked you straight out.

P5Borrow adulthood. Do not surrender it.

Before You Try to Go Deep

P1How much of the grown-up you is actually here right now? Try looking around the room, pausing before you act, making a choice and following through on it. If you can't do that because you're basically the frightened kid, leave the deeper conversation until later. You may need somebody steady there with you, or to do whatever helps you come back a bit before you try to talk to the child.

P2And if the grown-up you is there, and it's just how deep you're going that's too much, I'd go shallower. Like the flight attendants say, it's good to know where the exits are. 😅 So can you stop in the middle and come back to the room? If you can't, or going in at all keeps making it worse, I'd come all the way back out first. Some people try one technique after another instead, to prove to themselves they can take it. Your little one may have had to just take it back then, though. Now there's a grown-up around who can say, "Okay, that's enough for today," and try something gentler, like saying hi to them with your eyes open.

P3If you need a break from the communion, simply jot down the reason and the progress made so far. Then come back later, once that reason has changed and you feel comfortable.

P4If being alone was a big part of what hurt during your childhood, I'd call somebody now, mess and all, since reaching toward people is part of taking care of your little one too. Even if you don't have someone you know who wants to listen, there are warmlines for this.

Your Body Might Need Some Love First

P1If you notice tension in your solar plexus or abdomen, or anywhere else that seems to be keeping you from relaxing into that heart chakra love, try some non-forced, slower exhales and a little gentle self-massage. I love using one foot to massage the other foot like a yin-yang symbol, but you can start with some nice circular or spiral massage right on the tense spot.

P2If that's not enough, there's deeper somatic stuff like Shaking, tapping (EFT), EMDR, Brainspotting, Somatic Experiencing, Water Therapy, Massage, Energy Healing, or some other bottom-up approach.

When Somatic Work Brings Things Up

P1Some somatic work can feel pleasant, and some can feel like a raging storm got unleashed. It can bring up grief and fear, and sometimes nightmares later on as the memories begin to unwind. It’s a bit like going to the gym. You want to make gains, and sore muscles the next day can be good, but proper range of (e)motion here is also key to avoid injury.

P2Keep track of how you feel over the next few days, like how refreshing your sleep is, and how easy it feels to get through your day. Do you want to go back toward what came up, or is some part of you saying no? If you can’t quite tell yet, I’d just make the next one a bit easier, one change at a time, like a science nerd, so you learn what made the difference. You can play around with variables like shorter session length, or shallower diving, time of day, keeping eyes open, having a friend around, or holding a teddy bear as a stand-in for your little one.

P3And what about those hard dreams, or images that pop up during a session? They can show what feels important or frightening right now. Sometimes they show what feels missing, or what feels needed. They aren't evidence that what you saw in them happened, though, and shouldn't be used to confirm a memory or rebuild one. Or to suggest one.

You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue

P1If you don’t hear a little kid talking in your head, don’t invent one. Actually, this is one place where the usual inner-child prompt to “ask your little one what they need” can get confusing, because “ask” sounds like you’re supposed to ask a question and then wait for a second voice to answer. Maybe that’s what happens for some people, but you might not be some people (hehe). If talking into an empty room feels silly, small figures on a table can help (so that you feel even more silly!?), like one for your little one and one for the rest of the gang that shows up. Put whichever one’s running the show on a throne. Both ideas come from sections 12.2 and 13.1 of a book called Schema Therapy for Children and Adolescents, which I’ll call ST-CA.

P2You might barely have spontaneous words in your mind and yet still be able to think a sentence on purpose. Try it and see. If words are there, speak or write them. You might feel more comfortable that way than just staying in imagination land.

P3Then again, maybe what’s actually requiring your attention now is a knot in your stomach. Or perhaps you’re in a place you want to leave. Sometimes nothing “answers” and you just know what would be nice to do for yourself. And if you don’t know, it’s fine to guess. Maybe it’s some genuine human closeness, or figuring out some way to have more say in your own life.

P4Life is always switching up on us. You could have a feeling before thoughts, or thoughts might come first and provoke a feeling. They say hindsight is 20/20, but you actually might not even know which happened first.

When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child

P1I've been writing this whole guide as if everybody who tries it wants to take care of the inner child. Some people get a little rush out of making somebody scared or small, though. For such folks, inner child therapy is clearly not the place to start. They need to first deconstruct the adult identity they've got.

P2When I talk with folks like that, I mostly want to know what the identity gets them and what it costs, and especially what it's really serving. Some of them even call themselves sovereign, and then somehow there's an urge they have to obey, which sounds more like slavery to me.

P3This part tends to scare the wrong people, like somebody who's horrified by their own thoughts. Everybody gets scary thoughts they'd rather not have sometimes, and they don't make you a bad person, but why would you bring your inner child into the middle of them? The nice thing about inner child therapy is that you can wait until you're calm and actually ready. A real parent doesn't get to do that, which is one reason nobody manages to be a perfect one.

P4And if somebody actually wonders whether they're malicious, they'd do better to meditate on it and find out why they're asking before they bring their inner child anywhere near it. Are they really feeling malicious, or is it some voice in their head saying so, like the inherited critic? If some of it turns out to be malice they actually want to act on, the inner child can wait.

Borrow the Adult Before You Can Be the Adult

Noticing Counts

P1"Huh, somebody in here is really scared." That might be the most adult thing you've got right now, and it's plenty to start with. Whoever's noticing isn't all the way inside the scared one.

P2It can help to use your own name. For me, instead of "I'm terrified," that'd be "Little Joel is terrified. What does Little Joel need right now?" You'll sound a bit like Elmo :) In lab studies, though, people who talked to themselves this way reacted less to upsetting pictures and memories, and their brains didn't seem to be working any harder to do it.

P3If you can tell your parts apart pretty easily, you've probably got plenty of noticing already. Don't keep drilling it just because the love isn't coming yet. A lot of meditators are great at watching. I was, and baby me was still starving.

Borrow One Competency at a Time

P1A complete ideal parent may be impossible to imagine (unless you saw The Brady Bunch). And imagination is pretty hard for adults anyway since it kinda gets stamped out of kids at an early age, but it's not just a foolish waste of time. It's actually a genius mode unlock. So since the adult is actually a baby when it comes to power to imagine, start small: Borrow one function at a time from a figure who embodies it. Perhaps a therapist or your grandmother really cared for you. Perhaps your teacher at some age was a great guide. Maybe an older sibling protected you well. And there's always your future healed self if you can imagine that.

P2For warmth, think of someone you remember fondly. How would they sit beside your little one? Just make sure it's someone who liked you the way you were.

P3For protecting, ask what a minimally competent adult would do here. Maybe something simple, like actually smelling that old food you're about to eat and then trashing it if it smells a little weird. If you're about to send an angry text, hit pause. If the room itself triggers your spidey sense, leave, or lock the door and get help. Even if this still feels like the child acting like the adult, for now, that's ok. "Fake it til you make it" isn't exactly it, but yeah. Afterward, you can tell your little one, "I did that for us. That was the Protector." If it sounds funny, laugh!

P4The Guide can be you too, just a calmer you. That's a lot easier to imagine than your future healed self. Think back to what (and how) you decided about something like this the last time you were calm. Or ask what the wise person you respect would have you practice today. A wise tradition (like "What would Jesus do?" or "What would Buddha do?") can give some direction, too, until you develop your own internal compass.

If the Love Isn't Coming Yet

P1If you weren't loved well as a child, "love your inner child" can sound like nonsense. Love them how? Maybe you really want the best for your little one. Maybe you hug them in your imagination, or miss them. And you can still feel none of the warmth people mean when they say love. That doesn't close the door, though, it just means you start smaller.

P2Maybe just imagining a caring grown-up makes you feel more threatened, or lonelier. Lonelier is a strange one, with somebody right there. Then go smaller still, to the least cruel response a decent adult could offer your little one. It's scaffolding.

P3Taking the hostility out makes room for warmth. What people most often say helped is letting their defenses down. I mean by choice, after the part guarding you has been heard. If you've never been safe being loved, your ego can act like a rigid security guard that won't let the feeling in, and being vulnerable is the opening the feeling needs. (This guard is only about letting the feeling in. People still have to earn your trust.) Sometimes the warmth is there, with a cold part standing in front of it, guarding something. That part tends to step aside once it's been heard (see the section Hear the Guard First).

Borrow Hindsight

P1I sometimes ask: "How would I respond to this once I understood it more clearly, in balance and with compassion?" It's a question borrowed from your future self, and I wouldn't take their answer as gospel. I don't think a perfect Higher Self is waiting in there with the answers, either (more on that in How This Relates to IFS).

P2That future you also knows why you can't pull this off perfectly today. What would they do for you, if they were here? Maybe they'd just make you some food. If you can't picture them, make the food yourself, and don't bother faking a feeling first. A "no" or a pause can count as the food, too.

P3You can borrow a wiser future version of whoever else is involved, too, as long as you don't assume they'll end up on your side, or sorry. What the current one keeps doing is still what you go by, however good the picture looks (the future one has never called you back).

Remember When You Already Did It

P1My father often suggested folks remember a similar-level (or even harder) challenge that they were able to handle. Maybe you survived a car crash and surgery a few years ago, but now you're having trouble with emotional eating. You can draw strength from the past. Not Every Hero Wears A Cape

P1Set one boundary. “What’s a ‘boundary’?”, I might be asking. That looks like saying no to something that’s costing you too much (money, time, whatever it is). Handle that task you’ve been putting off, or the tax man, or your DUIs in Maryland, even if all you do today is open the envelope. Keep one small promise to your little one. Clean your room! P2You can protect your little one from yourself too, like by repairing after an internal attack. “What’s an ‘internal attack’?” Calling yourself an idiot for losing your keys again, for one. Your little one was listening, so you go back and apologize to them. If they’re sarcastic about it, you hear it out instead of snapping back. They don’t trust you yet, and you don’t ask them to. They complain about something, and if they’re right, you say so. All of that is evidence they're accepted and you’re safe to be around. Love needs those to start with, but without warmth they can still feel like they’re only being put up with.

P3Plenty of people are still at the putting-up-with part. Even with little or no warmth for your little one yet, the relationship can start with the Protector’s plain acts and the warmth can come after, which feels backwards, because you’d think you have to feel it first before you could do anything for them. But yeah, bonding starts somewhere. And this is not to say you’ve never done anything to care for yourself. If not, you’d be dead right now. But we’re making additional acts of care intentional now as a base for love to grow.

Make It Intentional

P1Intentional, meaning you pick one of those acts and decide when you'll do it, and what the smallest first step is (like with the envelope). Love Doesn't Have to Wait for Trust ❤️

P1Sometimes the love is there for your little one and still feels unsafe to them, or safe for a minute and then not. Love and trust don't have to come as a pair, but they got tied together, as if one had to vouch for the other. So your love can sound like you're asking them to trust you. It doesn't need their trust, but it does need you to be honest with them, which isn't the same as perfect.

P2The love can go first, though, before their trust is done going over your track record (they've heard what you still call yourself when you mess up, sometimes out loud). Your little one says "I don't trust you." You answer, "I believe you. And I love you."

P3I was about five when my mom told me it was time for bed (or something equally outrageous to a five-year-old). I cried and screamed "I HATE YOU!" 😡 She answered, "But I love you!" 🥰

P4That completely threw me. Fights don't usually go like that. Anger is a way to get more power when you feel a lack of justice or agency. You puff yourself up so you seem frightening or dangerous, or you demand to be heard. My anger was there to get me heard, and her answer showed me I didn't need it for that. Whatever injustice I thought there was wasn't coming from a lack of love, either. So the anger seemed stupid. I saw for myself that it was only hurting me, without helping me see clearly or get what I wanted. Still, what I screamed was true right then, and what she said back was true too, so I could hate somebody who loved me and she'd still be my mom the whole time.

P5When it's your little one yelling at you, though, try not to turn "But I love you" into a line you say on cue, because then it can brush the anger off, and what it's really saying is "so stop being angry." I think you can mean it and still be doing that. Does the love stay afterward and take whatever's true in the anger seriously?

P6The love can stay, and your little one might still come back with "Big fuckity whoopty doo. I don't believe you." 🙄 You answer, "Okay. I believe that you don't believe me. I still love you. 🤗 You don't have to love me back. Tell me what feels unsafe."

P7Your little one might say it's you. You don't have to accept every conclusion they reach about you, but do listen for the concrete complaint without fighting the yelling and swearing around it. If it's something serious, answer it seriously. The complaint might only be one sentence, once the swearing runs out. Like the anger at bedtime, the swearing is there to get them heard, so once you're listening instead of fighting, they don't need it anymore.

Instead of Blame

P1Your little one might not be the only one getting accused. P2Whichever age it picks on, saving everybody was never your job. At fifteen you probably didn't even have your own money, or anywhere else to go. And actually, not to beat a dead horse here, but every age of you was stuck with whatever it had then, even the adult you were last year.

P3So instead of asking which age of you deserves the blame, I'd ask these:

1.What was that age of you trying to get, or get away from? 2. What did it understand back then? 3. What options could it actually see? Could it stand to take any of them? 4. P3"Half the time when I try to connect to baby me, I feel fake and it seems crazy."

P4I used to be all personal trainer and teach persistence on this as the remedy. Keeping at it does help, since a little one who learned to expect being dropped won't trust you right away. P3The peer doesn't become your permanent parent, though. They show you a capacity, help you get the hang of it, and give it back to you, which is borrowed adulthood in its healthiest form (a driving instructor isn't planning to drive you around forever either). Is that therapy? Well, not the mainstream kind, but yes, for sure. Professional therapy's still useful for what it does, and peers and community do something else, so a serious healing path can include both.

The Love Has to Move

P1Community can come before the big opening or after it, too. Sometimes loving people give somebody enough safety and belonging that a deeper opening becomes possible. Other times it's the other way around. Somebody has a big opening first, a love or a meaning they couldn't have imagined before, and then they start to "manifest" different kinds of people and relationships in their life. Either way, the community is what helps make it practical, real, down to earth, embodied and so on.

P2This is part of why I connect inner-child pl/ork with Hearthwork and with building community. Having a beautiful experience on the inside isn't really the point. The love has to move, and it doesn't always go the same way round (the door you came in through matters less than the loop). From a spiritual experience to your little one, then into your relationship with the grown-up you, then out to other people, and back again.

How to Forgive Without Forgetting

P1For many people, healing does get around to some forgiveness eventually, or at least to not being run by the resentment anymore. It can't be put on a schedule (nobody gets a gold star for forgiving fastest). And you don't have to forgive first to get safe, set boundaries, hold someone accountable, get justice or get better. Then again, active resentment narrows what you see, and your little one stays organized around the injury. And if someone pressures you into forgiving early, what you get is an enlightened-looking act, with the wound gone underground.

Forgiving Yourself

P1Forgiving yourself works the same way. Own what you did (only what was yours), and let yourself feel the remorse. Repair what you can, by apologizing or making amends. Changing the behavior counts too. Then you're allowed to stop holding yourself hostage, and the responsibility stays. If you're thinking "not me, not after what I did," forgiving other people tends to be harder too. Every so often, come back to how whoever did it got to be someone who could do that, until you understand it well enough that it changes how you feel.

When One Life Has Ended and the Next Hasn’t Begun

P1Your little one can get really loud in the gap after one life ends and before the next one has formed. A breakup will do it, or moving to another country, an illness, losing your faith or a role, your youngest moving out. Maybe you've left a family or community, maybe you've stayed on new terms. A therapist or helper might step back because you're doing so much better, and suddenly your little one is wailing. For a while the old you is gone and the new you hasn't shown up yet. Tibetan Buddhism calls an in-between state like that a bardo.

P2Your little one might be sure everything's over. Is it? Usually one real thing ended, and there's other stuff you're still trying to keep going, maybe on autopilot. Some of it just isn't known yet, and unknown isn't the same as gone. Ask your little one what they're scared will happen in the meantime (worst-case scenarios are kind of their specialty).

P3Something basic can mark the ending, like boxing up the old keys. Nobody needs to know what door will open next. But when the not-knowing feels too heavy, a new identity can sure look tempting. Like a borrowed coat that doesn't fit but at least it's warm. But you can't go far with a borrowed coat anyway, so don't force the future on. Keep taking care of yourself, and grieve what ended with your little one beside you during the metamorphosis.

Missing Someone

P1When you miss someone, let the ache be a cue for love. It doesn't have to become one more problem to solve, and pushing it away won't help. If love's there, "I miss you" becomes "I love you." Or there's the loving-kindness wish: "May you be safe. May you be held in love." You can picture them. Or don't, and just feel the care going their way.

P2All of this happens in you, so it doesn't matter whether they get any of it (nobody's checking their inbox). And if it starts making you more compulsive, or a bit unreal, so you can't get back to your life, make it simpler: "I miss you, and I care." Some of that same care can go to the part of you that's still here.

Finding a Therapist to Borrow Adulthood

P1We all need some support, especially when taking our first steps. First and easiest is to start with finding a somatic therapist, if that resonates, as per my Somatic Therapies Guide.

P2Inner Child Reparenting therapists do exist, and you can find them, although they often are not licensed, and I don't know of any central directory, nor of any protocol they'd have in common. There are none which work with my guide yet. I can consult, and if you like the guide, you can ask a therapist to check it out and see what they agree with or disagree with as a great way to vet them.

P3The best licensed, listed therapists you'll find that come close will be doing Internal Family Systems (IFS), or Schema Therapy. I'll talk more about those in the appendix, but Schema Therapy comes closest to my approach. Open Path Collective (or a similar resource in your country) lists thousands of affordable therapists. You can filter them by Schema or IFS. You can often find even more Schema therapists at the official site.

P4Before choosing a therapist, get to know the strengths and pitfalls of their approach, including any modifications they make to the standard protocol. I'd especially want to interview them regarding how they treat spiritual experiences. You want someone who won't tell you what yours really mean, downplay them, force them, or treat them as a symptom. I'd also want one who doesn't put ideas in your head, and who understands dissociation. What have they found too challenging to deal with so far, and where have they seen the best results? A good therapist won't charge you an arm and a leg just for this kind of interview, and they should already have this information on their website to avoid wasting too much time. A certificate on the wall can't tell you any of that.

P5Regarding AI therapy, I've tried all the top AI therapy apps, and they're all pretty bad. I'm trying to make my own based on this guide, but I'm becoming more convinced that unsupervised AI therapy is just a toss up. That doesn't mean you can't get a lot of great insights sometimes when talking to AI, though, but just expect it to go sideways sometimes.

Appendix

How This Relates to Schema Therapy

P1Of the clinical approaches I know, schema therapy is the closest to this guide. Its therapists do what they call limited reparenting: within professional limits, they give you some of what a good parent would, with warmth and firmness and some playfulness too. Over time you take that in, and it becomes your own "Healthy Adult," who looks after your "Vulnerable Child" and stands up to the punishing parent in your head. It sets limits, too. And it gets on with adult life, like work and the things you've committed to. Sound familiar? That's a Nurturer, a Protector and a Guide by other names, and since the therapist's version becomes yours bit by bit, it's borrowed adulthood too.

P2Where it really differs is spirituality. Schema therapy doesn't build it in, though the preface to its main textbook calls blending it with meditation or religious practice an important development. That's not so surprising, since it grew out of cognitive therapy. Some people do blend them, though. Tara Bennett-Goleman's book Emotional Alchemy, for one, fits a Buddhist loving-kindness wish to each schema. Her book barely mentions gratitude, and for me that's the door to the bigger love back in Your Loan is PRE-APPROVED! IFS goes overboard the other way, in my opinion (more on that in the next section).

How This Relates to IFS

P1Richard Schwartz, who developed Internal Family Systems (IFS), called one of his books No Bad Parts. Honor, understand and love every part, he says, and the protective ones are no exception, so "meet the protector first" would fit right in over there. That's a lot of the overlap with this method, right there in the title.

P2My reparenting method diverges from IFS at an important point. The IFS Institute's outline of the model says "everyone has a Self," and that the Self "can and should lead," once your parts stop "blending" with it. I emphasize the inner adult you build and practice occupying: Nurturer, Protector, and Leader, Guide, or Guru. That's still one grown-up with three jobs, like back in The Chicken-and-Egg Problem.

P3Can't find a calm, compassionate Self in there? Then borrow. One grown-up job at a time, from a friend who'll play Protector for a night, or from something you do, like letting the phone ring when you know how that call goes (The Parent You Inherited again), until it's yours.

P4Is a perfect Higher Self already waiting in there to be your guide or parent? I'd call that claim spiritual bypassing. I've had the Higher Self perspective myself, though. It's a somewhat less subjective way of seeing things as working perfectly in reality, despite the ego's illusions, which matches what people say about it. Where I part ways with IFS is the wisdom part. I don't think the Higher Self has any specific wisdom. It's a vantage point, where more wisdom and compassion can come up, but problems can also look like they were never problems at all from up there, and that can ironically lead instead to more foolish indifference.

P5You can see both sides of this see-saw in the guru types who supposedly embody their Higher Self. Anthony de Mello) is an excellent example. He was a Jesuit priest who mixed stories from all kinds of traditions into his teaching on seeing through the ego's traps and coming back to the Perfect Present. He also had a background as a psychotherapist who understood trauma-informed healing methods. He seemed to drift between the two opposite paradigms in a way that contradicted itself. And that bizarre contradiction is itself cited as "crazy wisdom" by apologists.

P6So inner child therapy draws on those higher vantage points more concretely. The inner Guide is the closest thing here to a Higher Self, and it has to work with the Nurturer and Protector and come after them. It can't act like some transcendent authority that waves everything away. Most reparenting doesn't do enough with the Guide, which is the opposite extreme from IFS, so I'm trying to bring together the best of both. IFS isn't exactly one system, so I may be simplifying it here. It has its own branches and disputes, and that's one more problem I'm trying to remedy by laying all this out systematically in this guide.

P7IFS calls these wounded young parts “exiles.” I find that pathologizing. I prefer to speak of a child who needs parenting and integration, rather than naming them by their wounded or excluded condition.

P8And the parts just keep coming: managers, firefighters, exiles, polarized parts, legacy burdens, Self-energy, guides, new subparts... At some point it’s a maze, and as my friend Pink, who has been healing her Dissociative Identity Disorder, explained, while it can be useful to have validation of the parts that one experiences, and ideas for how to work with them, it can also get overwhelming. People who are already overwhelmed can thus become more split up than before. I've seen many others echo this same evaluation, and it makes sense to me.
