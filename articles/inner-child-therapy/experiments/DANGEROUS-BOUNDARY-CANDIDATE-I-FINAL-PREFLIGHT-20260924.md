# Before You Try to Go Deep

How much of the grown-up you is actually here right now? Try looking around the room, pausing before you act, making a choice and following through on it. If you can't do that because you're basically the frightened kid, leave the deeper conversation until later. You may need somebody steady there with you, or to do whatever helps you come back a bit before you try to talk to the child.

## When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child

Being adult enough to do the exercise is not the same as being safe enough to do it. And safety here is a narrower question than whether somebody has disturbing thoughts or beliefs. Religion or unusual beliefs, intrusive thoughts, anger, charged language, unwanted fantasies, or a harmful act in the past don't tell you by themselves what the person intends now. Somebody can have any of those things and not endorse harming vulnerability; somebody else can be calm, present, perfectly capable of making choices, and still want or enjoy frightening, humiliating, or exploiting it. That's the line that matters for this exercise. If the present-day adult is still on the second side of it, don't bring the child forward to find out what happens. A frightened reaction only tells you the child is frightened, and if that fear is rewarding to the adult, using the child as a test has already become part of the harm.

The child can stay out while the adult gets worked with directly. Whatever brought you here is still there, and you can look at what the identity or stance is actually doing in your life instead of asking the child to change it for you. Has it given you the life and happiness you expected? What are you getting from it now, what has it cost, and who else does it serve? If sovereignty is part of how you understand yourself, this is where the word gets concrete. An urge or an authority can feel absolute—a doctrine, a group, a spirit, a master—but can you stop long enough to question it, refuse it, or change your mind? If you can't, then the problem is still adult agency, whatever label you put on it. Somebody can care about you while you work on that without praising cruelty, turning the conversation into a theology fight, or helping you become better at exploiting people.

The same evidence standard applies when you say you won't harm the child. The promise matters, but it doesn't prove safety on its own. Protective credibility is what repeated non-harmful choices start to build, especially when exploiting vulnerability would have felt rewarding and you choose not to. Until then, using a different route still puts the same child in contact with the same intent. Inner dialogue can do that; so can childhood photographs or hypnosis. Imagining a borrowed parent or a spiritual figure making contact for you doesn't get around it either. For some people, sustained care from actual people may be more useful at this point than another internal exercise or one done with a bot.

## You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue

“Voice,” “ask,” and “answer” below don't have to mean a literal conversation in your head. If you don't hear a little kid talking in your head, don't invent one. Actually, this is one place where the usual inner-child prompt can get confusing, because “ask” sounds like you're supposed to ask a question and then wait for a second voice to answer. Maybe that's what happens for some people, but you might not be some people.

You might barely have spontaneous words in your mind and yet still be able to think a sentence on purpose. Try it and see. If words are there, speak or write them. You might feel more comfortable that way than just staying in imagination land.  And if that starts feeling like homework you're doing for a child who isn't answering, drop it. Maybe what's actually requiring your attention now is a knot in your stomach. Or perhaps you're in a place you want to leave. Sometimes nothing “answers” and you just know what would be nice to do for yourself.
