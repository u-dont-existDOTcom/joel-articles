# How to organize a guide's contents

These rules are for any long guide or article whose headings readers use to find their way (on Substack, the narrow
contents sidebar lists levels 1 to 4). They decide where each section goes and at what level. Naming the headings is a
separate step. Work in the four passes below, in order, and write each pass down before the next.

## Pass 1. The parts and their jobs

Read the whole piece. List its top-level parts and, for each, the one job it does for the reader (for example: what
this is and why; getting safe and ready; building the skills or roles; how to practice; when practice gets hard;
special situations; carrying it into everyday life; closing and appendix). If the piece is built around named roles,
tools, stages or steps, list each of those too, with the work it does in one line ("does the emotional repair",
"holds the spiritual side", "sets limits"). Keep the author's parts where they already have one clear job.

## Pass 2. A home for every section (a card sort)

Go through every section, one by one, and decide its home without looking at where it sits now: the part, and the role,
tool or stage, whose job it does. Ask what the section does for the reader:
- teaches a skill or kind of work: its home is the role, tool or stage that does that work, wherever the section was
  written (in a cooking guide, a knife-sharpening tip written inside a recipe goes with the tools; in a coaching guide,
  a way of handling a player's fear goes with the role that looks after morale, not with the drills);
- gives step-by-step practice: the practice part, after the concepts it uses;
- deals with a problem: next to the general problem it is a case of, or the practice where it comes up;
- tells a story or example: with the point it illustrates, even if that point is in another part;
- is about one kind of situation (a state of mind, a medium, a setting, relationships with other people): with the
  other sections about that situation.
Write the home next to each section id. Then list every section whose home differs from where it sits now: those are
the moves.

## Pass 3. Order and levels inside each home

What a reader needs first comes first: readiness and foundations before techniques, the general before its special
cases, the common case before the rare one. Keep the author's order where nothing above calls for a change.
- A section that is a case, part, tool or example of another sits one level below it; siblings are the same kind of
  thing.
- A heading with only one section under it, or over a single line or punchline, is a sign to merge it.
- A grouping heading with no text is fine with two or more sections under it; an empty heading with nothing under it
  goes.
- Only levels 1 to 4 show in the contents; level 5 hides a heading from it.

## Pass 4. Check the moves

For every move, say in one line why a reader looking for that section would look in its new place. Drop moves you
can't say that for. List the text pointers ("above", "further on", "in the next section", another section's name)
that a move makes wrong, so they can be fixed.
