### Also Look Outward

After one terrible conversation, I can see why you'd want to make a call about the person right then. Then sometimes they come back later and actually talk about what you were trying to say. They still disagree. Their boundary is still exactly the one you hated. But you're talking about the thing now. So what, exactly, did the first fight prove? Other times the subject changes but the ending feels strangely familiar. [Lindsay Gibson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) writes about that kind of repeated pattern in her work on emotional immaturity: you bring up something that hurt you and somehow your part of it is gone by the end.

You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.

If you really did hurt them, there is something to repair. The weird part is how easily that can spread. You start with “I shouldn't have done that,” and somewhere along the way you're apologizing for having had the complaint in the first place because they're still disappointed in you. Maybe you were wrong about that too. But the guilty feeling isn't what tells you. It also isn't proof that they're manipulating you.

Sometimes you explain yourself and the other person still thinks your no is unfair. So you explain it another way. Then you find yourself explaining why the last explanation didn't come out right. That can go on for a while. You may already have said what mattered.
