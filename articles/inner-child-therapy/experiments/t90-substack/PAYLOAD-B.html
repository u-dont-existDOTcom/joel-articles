Altered States Can Deepen the Therapy

I’d learn to do inner-child therapy sober first, in your ordinary mindstate, since that’s the one you’ll be living in. Cars don’t start at 60mph, that’s not the way the universe seems to work. First gear leads to second leads to third, etc. Second gear, in this case, may refer to gentle meditation or self-hypnosis. When you’re ready, my self-hypnosis guide and lucid-dreaming guide can take those deeper, into third gear. Low doses of entheogens can also synergize compatibly with various gears: cactus, CBD tea with sublingual mapacho, Loveyhuasca, iboga, and so on.

Remember those guards from The Urge to Escape, above? Same deal here, so get enough Nurturer and Protector in the room before you go deep. Borrowed is fine, as long as it’s from someone steady who won’t try to run your trip for you. And yes, those caution signs still count. But feeling attacked by a demon on a psychedelic is not necessarily paranoia. It can be real, and it’s also not bad unless you’re unprepared. It can be supremely empowering when you can send love and see how it fixes the situation.

Now, the beautiful stuff. Say one night (on a psychedelic, in a dream, ten days into a meditation retreat, whatever) you felt more love than you knew was possible. Wonderful, and it matters! Keep it as a reference point, and chase it playfully, because it is already a part of you, and you’ll be like a cat chasing its tail, which can be cute and fun, or dizzy and absurd!

Jack Kornfield has a whole book on what comes next, After the Ecstasy, the Laundry. The title kind of says it all. That night can't be the bar for every morning after, or regular life starts to feel like failing. And gorgeous as it was, it doesn't mean you're done healing. The laundry will tell you more, and it's the same test as in Timing Is Individual, further down.

One reason the peak is so momentary is because we often don’t need to truly give anything up to get it. Say you’re still pretty angry, and you want enlightenment, but it has to come with the money and the partner in one package. Ketamine can get you to a peak anyway. Then, you might now have some kind of transcendental “understanding” glimpse that seems like it could fix everything, if only you could be more aware of it, which often leads into a cycle of taking more and more medicine over time. I’d prefer to ask if any of the discontent has been let go, or the anger? If not, that peak was less the love we’re after here than “I’ll be grateful once I’ve got it all,” the gratitude with conditions from Ask God for a Loan.

And if your little one still can’t take the love in? Hold off on going for bigger, crazier breakthroughs, however tempting it is, or at least know that they are a double-edged sword. Something’s in the way, and getting to know it is your next job. Often it’s a protective part that’s seen love come with strings before. Hear it out first, the way you did in Hear the Guard First.

When Escape Leaves Reality Behind

All kinds of psychoactive drugs can mess with your sense of what’s real, and that’s often the point of them, so you can break free of all your old patterns completely for a bit. But in general, try to select the healthiest ones and use slow and gentle protocols. I’d say one of the most abused psychedelics (aside from ketamine, if you call that a psychedelic) is 5-MeO-DMT. I have a guide on that. People very often believe you need a huge dose of it for a “full release.” They go through all kinds of screaming and shaking, nearly killing themselves, with absolutely zero inner-child work being done. It’s all spiritual bypassing.

Your own drug history matters too. If you go manic, psychotic (by which I mean losing your sense of reality to the point of posing a risk to yourself or others), get super paranoid, or end up worse afterward than before, those are signs to reconsider trying the same thing again, and try to figure out why that happened. Don't try again until you understand what would make it safe. I’d look at the drug and the dosage, as well as mindset and setting etc.

Paranoia during a rough trip can be a warning sign for psychosis, but it doesn’t automatically lead there, since it can be valid. It tends to go that way when it isn’t balanced by practicality. “The CIA is mind-controlling me!” is paranoia. “The CIA, marketers, and other forces are conspiring to mind-control me, the way they do to everyone else! But since I can see that, I can prevent it to some extent, just by being aware of how it operates” is paranoia too, but it’s practically useful, and less likely to lead to psychosis. “...which means I can prevent it by standing on my head and counting backwards from 33, since 33 is their magic Freemason number!” is leaning psychotic. Some drugs are known for causing paranoia, like THC, so I’d avoid those. If you can’t clearly understand what happened yourself, get someone qualified to help. And if you have an idea of what happened but aren’t sure, ask someone who knows you well.

Any of those reactions, from mania to paranoia, can also point to a vulnerability in you, so I'd be careful with other drugs that mess with what's real too, though I don't mean every drug's dangerous for you. And any predisposition toward mania or psychosis is a warning sign too, whether it's family history, a bad environment or a bad mindset. I can't tell you exactly what to do about it, and nobody can tell you for sure what's best for you. There are just caution signs, and when you see them, you take more precautions of every kind. You have to figure out what they mean for you, and if you can't, get help from trusted advisors.

By trusted, I mean someone you know is trustworthy from a long relationship with them, not someone people on Reddit said was super magical and healing. That's called a crapshoot. It might turn out well, it might not. It might seem like it did, and then later you realize you were duped, as happened to me with Sidney the iboga shaman. A new therapist is not as trusted as an old one. As they say, "new friends are silver, but old friends are gold."

Milder reactions afterward count as caution signs too, like getting confused or spaced out (dissociating) again and again. Get professional help right away if any of this is severe or risky, and especially if it keeps going or comes back while you're sober. If you can't tell whether you're safe, I'd treat that as risky. Don't suddenly quit a prescribed medicine, or anything your body is dependent on, just because it might be part of the problem; have a doctor help you stop safely.

Be careful what you mix, too, and not just prescribed medicines. Even some basic things like cinnamon can be dangerous (and helpful) with ayahuasca, and doctors don't know about a lot of this. I also learned that mixing acacia confusa with mimosa hostilis is very dangerous. I'd never recommend anyone risk it, since it's highly destabilizing and challenging. I have a list of things to be cautious about with ayahuasca in particular.

Gentle meditation or self-hypnosis is a different story. It can really calm your nervous system down. A lot of people find that a mantra they feel some heart connection to, repeated in their head or out loud, especially with a nice melody, can lower the walls enough for the heart to open up. It's not something to lean on all day as a way of coping, but used as therapy it can be super effective. Altered States Can Require Reparenting

Have you ever been attacked by a literal demon? Maybe during sleep paralysis or meditation or on a psychedelic trip? A lot of modern folk laugh at this, yet it’s really quite a common experience. I was being psychically attacked by ghosts in my home as a child. Because I was literally a child, I was an easy target. And since most people never actually grew up, they are easy targets for every kind of influence, including spirit influence.

Those ghosts knew my weakness: dares. So they would dare me to jump out the window. I wasn’t suicidal, but I hated to seem like a “wussy.” Luckily, I trusted my parents with this information, and instead of taking me to a psychiatrist and pumping me full of anti-psychotic drugs, they put bars on the windows. Many kids are not so lucky in modern societies. They may get pumped full of anti-psychotic drugs for the rest of their life once diagnosed. That said, it'd make sense to get a professional to weigh in for some situations. I still played around with half-hanging out the windows, but I wasn’t quite dumb enough to jump out, and I really appreciate that my parents took it seriously without catastrophizing.

As a semi-adult, age 26, I discovered meditation. After I began meditating deeply for a while, it seemed like my astral portal opened. I started getting attacked by spirits during sleep paralysis. They would sit on me, prevent me from breathing, and instill tremendous fear into the core of my being.

Yep. Exactly like that.

Sleep paralysis is a trance-like state, and trance can be "more scary" because you're trapped in it, but it's also less actually dangerous. You can't ruin your life at all in trance (unless a bad hypnotic suggestion gets deeply implanted, perhaps), while without trance you can "freak out" in a way that might end up getting you committed to a mental hospital, thrown out of your housing, going to jail, possibly even killing yourself. In trance you can basically work with the fear. You recognize the fear has no real world significance and it's purely just fear, so it's super useful that way. The thing to fear is fear itself, since it actually opens us up to being manipulated by demons, which feed on it directly. So it's not really that they have no power. We give them the power.

After multiple episodes of this I decided to stop giving in to the victim mentality, and to start taking it seriously as a part of spiritual practice. My inner Guide saw it as a perfect opportunity to demonstrate the power of love over fear. The next time it happened, despite how scary it was, I sent unconditional love to this demon on me. It took some minutes, but once that love was seen as enduring, the demon left, and it never returned.

I’ve also trained other people to do this during spiritual attack while on psychedelics. It works a lot better if you are already trained in loving-kindness meditation to the point where it’s natural. I’d say it works way better than exorcism techniques (like Julian Palmer’s torping, which he also admits doesn’t work as well as love).

The astral plane is a bit different from the 3D. In the astral realm, unconditional love is a true, ultimately powerful physical force. Like a river, or a moat. There’s no evil that can trespass that moat. But don’t assume it will always work like that in the physical plane. It definitely has worked for me sometimes when facing some thugs, but definitely not with others. Loving someone does not mean you leave the front door unlocked with a sign saying “take whatever you want.”

If you’re not able yet (or in the moment) to summon real love for an enemy, you can first send love to your frightened little one from your adult Nurturer. Many people also find they can borrow support from God, Jesus, angels, devas, or whatever loving spiritual presence fits their tradition. If I were to do that, I would still be framing it as “Please help this situation with love” rather than “Strike down the enemy!” But if a presence seems to enjoy your fear, it's not one of your grown-ups, borrowed or not, whatever it says it is. Same goes if it demands you hurt or use anybody, yourself included.

Borrowing support like that is basically borrowed adulthood. At first you may need to feel protected or loved by something bigger than you, before that love starts to feel like yours, and then bit by bit it starts to become your own. Send some of it down to your frightened little one, and when that's possible, toward whatever you're afraid of. Continuing to relate to benevolent spiritual powers/beings is still perfectly fine, but they would surely be happy if they rubbed off on you!

If a voice or urge is directing you towards harm, and you feel at risk of acting on it, as with myself and those ghosts at home, move away from that open window and get some outside support. Know the limit of what you can handle, but don't turn it into a prison for life.

Non-altered states can feel like this too

Even social media algorithms will turn your life into hell if you don’t have the loving, protective inner parent developed. When you do, social media actually becomes useful and feels nice to interact with now and then.

Many people think reparenting sounds silly, or like the kind of ridiculous therapy people do when they have too much time or money. But you know what’s even sillier? Wandering the world aimlessly and getting manipulated by all kinds of evil forces. 💀

My Psychedelic Experiences Came A Bit Too Soon

The first time I took San Pedro cactus, at a medium-low dose, it made me super emotional, and I cried a lot. It was very therapeutic, actually. But I still squandered that opportunity: with a better understanding of reparenting, it could have been much more therapeutic.

Ayahuasca was often much more challenging, because at first I was taking huge doses for ego death. The ayahuasca by itself wasn't exactly spiritual bypassing for me, though. It gave me a connection to divinity, and inner-child reparenting does work best with a little of that. But then I squandered the opportunity to bring that love down from the heavens to my inner child, which led me to be more like a preacher than a fully well-rounded individual who could lead by example.

When you’re deeply entranced, you can’t freak out: no matter how scary something is, it’s just like, “Oh, this is scary, OK, oh well.” That’s not a good excuse to take crazy doses tho. I went psychotic once on ayahuasca made with pure THH (an alkaloid from the ayahuasca vine) and Mimosa hostilis bark, which has DMT in it. THH alone isn’t hypnotic (trance-like) enough to keep people safe with higher doses of DMT. I was very lucky that my landlord, a Quaker family friend, was super understanding and talked me down gently. Otherwise the cops might have been called, and I could still be in jail, perhaps, since ayahuasca was illegal there at the time, not to mention I had a bunch of mimosa bark in the house that they might have claimed was for distributing. In general, don’t call the cops and expect it to go well. That’s a real last resort.

Out of hundreds of times I've taken iboga, there was one time just a small amount of male iboga root bark got me a bit hypomanic, to the point where a thought came: "Everyone should listen to me!" Luckily I was trained in Buddhist meditation, so I immediately challenged it with "Why should anyone listen if they have no interest?" I stopped using iboga for a while after that, and I warn people about that potential issue with male iboga specifically.

Physical Pain Makes It Harder

Physical pain can bring on the urge to escape too. Say an attack has you curled up on the bathroom floor, and whatever knocked the pain out last time (say, fent, or ketamine) starts looking real good, though you know it’ll leave you worse if you keep resorting to that quick fix.

By now, you may have already been through a million doctors who shrugged their shoulders, or even alternative treatments that were too costly or hard to grade. Sometimes it really is so simple that the best doctors and scanners can’t figure it out, like your house is moldy. It is worth going through all the basic checks first that you can do yourself, and trying the things that make most sense. For more on that, check my detox guide.

And when the symptoms come and go randomly, the best time to get diagnostics done could be right at the actual time of the crisis, rather than waiting until you feel well enough to make an appointment. But what if the attacks come almost every day? You can’t move into the ER. And normal test results don’t prove the pain is imaginary, just as a treatment that helps doesn’t explain what’s causing it.

I’d ask for a chronic pain specialist and a written plan for the flare-ups you can see coming, including the new or severe signs that mean you go to the ER right away, but don’t let amelioration of symptoms be the end of the investigation.

Listen To Your Body, Or Else!

Sometimes, a physical illness is really partly or wholly an emotional issue causing “somatic symptoms,” as happened to various friends of mine, as well as to Lucia Capacchione, PhD., and many of her clients and friends. She wrote one of the first comprehensive inner child therapy guides, Recovery of Your Inner Child, in 1991. While I don’t doubt that many people do recover from mysterious physical illness via meditation, inner child therapy, and somatic therapy, I also don’t want to claim, as some folks do, that all illness is entirely rooted in emotional issues. That is clearly not true. We don’t need to be gaslit to consider all the possibilities.

Being ready to do anything to end the pain isn’t a suicide plan, so I wouldn’t present it as one to get relief. But if you’re scared you’ll actually hurt yourself, or can’t tell whether you’re safe, get someone with you right away.

If You Want to Kill Yourself

Maybe you're reading this about to act, or with the means close by. Then before anything else, get another human being involved, and put some distance between you and the means. A crisis line counts, and the person on the other end can be a total stranger. Do the same if you can't stop yourself, or you're badly disoriented or intoxicated. Can't tell if that's you? Treat it like it is. None of that is me answering whether life is worth living. It just keeps the question open long enough to look at.

Then, before getting all philosophical, make sure there's a real, live grown-up in the room or on the phone, if that's possible. And as for the internal psychology of it, when the part that wants to die feels like all of you, go ahead and notice who's doing the noticing (as we discussed in Noticing Counts), or borrow an adult from the adult lending library in your head :). Any part of you that can help keep your body safe, or would rather wait on a decision you can't undo, is welcome. And if none of them shows up yet? No problem, the questions can wait.

I try not to proselytize about spirituality, because I've made that mistake before, and I now see that it's better to let folks find it when they are ready. But when someone tells me they want to kill themself, I consider the topic to be brutally opened by default, regardless of whether they meant to do that, because if they actually do kill their body (which they might mistake to be their self), that's a very powerful spiritual challenge right there, even if they didn't realize it would be.

Then ask: what is the self you want to kill? Is that self the same thing as your body? Why? Do you have the same body in dreams? What exactly do you expect your body's death to accomplish? If the problem is a state of mind, like self-hatred or despair, shame or loneliness, what makes you so sure that destroying the body ends it?

In Buddhism, death isn't an off switch, and it isn't a free new-age get-out-of-jail card back to the source. Repeated thought and intention shape the mind, and that keeps going through death and rebirth. Yes, that can include hell realms. A human birth is a rare chance to understand suffering and end its causes. You don't have to believe that, of course.

But if you're still thinking of staking your life on the opposite claim, that death is just nothing, look into that claim first (and if you're at the edge right now, the first step above comes before any of this). Researchers at the University of Virginia have been collecting cases of small children who talk about a past life for about sixty years, and there's a big pile of near-death accounts too. I don't think any of it can tell you what dying will be like for you, but I'd read a few of those cases before betting my life on "nothing."

Even in this one life, the more you hate yourself, the better your mind gets at it, and being alone tends to deepen the despair. Physical life keeps handing you redirections, though, like other people and things you have to do, and help when you reach for it. If the many traditions and the accumulating scientific evidence are right that the mind's causal process goes on after the body dies, killing the body may take away some of those redirections and leave the suffering right where it was.

My father used to tell suicidal people, "Oh, you want to kill yourself? That's wonderful. Now you can change what you've always been too afraid to change in your life, and then see if you still want to end it!" His point wasn't that dying is wonderful. If everything's already on the table, what's the change you still can't bring yourself to make? Leaving? Quitting? Moving? Telling your family who you really are? Those aren't free, and you can't always take them back, but they're smaller than dying, and they can wait until you're calmer and have some help. Look at that life you've been afraid to change while you still have a human body.

This human life is the most precious opportunity

As I mentioned in my nibbana article, I’ve had my most profound spiritual peak experiences and realizations when I had the least interest in continued existence. And the Buddha taught in MN 129, attaining a human birth is as rare and precious as it would be for a one-eyed turtle to pop his head up through a hoop on the surface of the vast ocean. You won the cosmic jackpot, so don’t throw away the ticket before you open the envelope to find it!

Sometimes There Isn't a Clear Child Yet

Reparenting assumes there's a clear little one in there to love, and for a lot of people there is. But if you grew up somewhere you only belonged as long as you played somebody else, like a really controlling family or a strict church, the real one may never have had room to form. All this stuff about your little one could have left you wondering who I'm even talking about.

Maybe you started playing that part so early, and it took over so completely, that "What do I believe?" or "What do I want?" has no clear answer of your own. They sound like such easy questions, too. Back then, asking might have gotten you in trouble, or laughed at. Or there was just no way to ask. That can make "love yourself" feel pretty empty (love what, when the part you've been playing doesn't feel real?).

From Survival to Experimental Play

If your childhood meant avoiding pain and keeping the people you needed close, your attention got used up reading danger (was that sigh about me?) and meeting their expectations, and when curiosity or mistakes got you punished, or it felt like they loved you less, you got better at the part. That left much less room for experiments that show you what you like when nobody's grading you. People grow into who they are by trying things on and then changing their minds, from what they wear down to the ideas and roles they take up, like a teenager who starts spelling her name with an "i" at the end and later changes it back.

Some of this is developmental pl/ork you get to do late: making it safe enough for your curiosity and experimenting to finally grow, and your preferences too. It might feel wrong somehow to like something before you've checked anyone's face. You can usually reparent and build who you are at the same time.

As you tell the part you've been playing apart from the self that didn't get much practice, notice whether it makes you more curious about both, and better able to relate to them. Or does it have you thinking, "That one's fake, and I'm getting rid of it"? You're after a relationship between them inside you that works better, and that's hard to have if there's a new split between an acceptable self and one that has to disappear.

When you ask what your little one feels, maybe all you get is a guess, which isn't proof there's no little one in there. Maybe you hear yourself answer, "Scared? I mean, it would make sense if it was scared."

So early on, the practice is finding out who's actually in there, through long reflection with no outside input, and journaling just for yourself, without picturing a reader. You might catch yourself explaining who everybody is anyway. Time alone in nature helps too, and so does getting back the preferences that got buried: the foods you really like and what feels good to do, and what you believe and feel when nobody's around to take your cues from. Focusing (Eugene Gendlin's method of listening to a vague, felt sense in your body) can especially help you notice feelings and preferences the part you've been playing learned to overlook. Any words you put to them can stay tentative ("I hate cooking... or just for everybody?") while you look at what they seem to mean and revise how you read them.

Untangling the Belonging Bargains

Part of building who you are is untangling yourself from whatever pulls you away from your own knowing in return for belonging. A political tribe might expect consistent positions and predictable reactions, while a family or religion that's kind to you might have expectations that just feel like being good. Most groups expect some conformity, which is ordinary. Your family might want a particular kind of son or daughter, and your friends might have rules about what you can joke about or get upset about. But if going along means betraying yourself, that's the bargain. It can help to write down the rules your family ran on, including unspoken ones like "Boys don't cry." Then write down the role you got handed, like the stand-in parent, or the one who got blamed for everything (ST-CA has lists of both, in sections 15.2.3 and 15.2.4).

Then there's another bargain, where you have to earn your worth: when you don't feel your value inside as a human being, you can become a "human doer," working for it in some arena outside yourself. That might be beauty, athleticism, sexual desirability, intelligence or grades. For some people it's money or status, and for others it's a sophisticated spiritual identity or becoming indispensable to everybody else's problems. It can feel really good to be the one everybody calls. In the more shadowy presentation, when you really need to feel needed, you might be like the firefighters who become arsonists to keep themselves employed.

Even with good parents, you could have learned unhealthy self-valuation when people repeatedly praised what you did rather than your inherent worth. That wasn't your flaw. Self-differentiation is learning to notice when you're making that trade, and to keep your own ground instead.

Some families run on a quieter bargain: "If I'm happy while someone I love is suffering, I'm abandoning them." You might really owe them help, and that's worth sorting out. Being miserable alongside them isn't help, though it can feel like loyalty. I'd try a small good thing, like a nice meal, and see what actually happens. Someone getting upset about it doesn't by itself mean you hurt them.

Four Pairs That Commonly Hook the Child

Pema Chödrön has a quick map for finding the bargain. It's four pairs, starting with pleasure and pain, and gain and loss. The other two are about what people think of you: praise and blame, and good reputation and disgrace. Your little one can panic when something hurts, and also when something good might disappear, like praise, romance, money, status, belonging or a spiritual high. You might still be enjoying one of those when your little one starts asking how long this is going to last.

When that happens, you can ask yourself these, once you've caught your breath:

- What do I think I'm about to get, or lose?
- Whose approval suddenly feels like I can't live without it? It isn't always who you'd guess.
- Is this a value I'm standing up for, or a high I'm trying to keep?
- Would this still be something I'd feel is worth doing if at the end of the day, I didn't get praise from anyone for it?

You don't have to stop enjoying pleasure, praise, gain or belonging. The question is whether losing one of them would feel like losing yourself.

Differentiation can happen right inside a family, religion, community or relationship. You'd still belong, just selectively. As a differentiated adult you can stay deeply embedded, free to question things or disagree. If a parent used to go quiet and hurt whenever you disagreed, that might be hard to believe. A family where everyone gets to be their own person is healthier than an enmeshed one, and a faith that's your own is healthier than religious compliance. It can be the same religion either way. And two differentiated people have a sturdier relationship than two who've been absorbed into each other.

Day to day, differentiation looks something like this:

- Caring about somebody's feelings without automatically adopting their opinions or sacrificing your own ground. Their reasons can still change your mind, though.
- Noticing when you agreed in the moment and disagree later: "Wait, why was I nodding along?"
- Noticing when what you thought you knew suddenly feels like water dripping through your hand because of praise or pity, fear or attraction, or something that really resonates with you.
- Disappointing people and withstanding social pressure, and still being recognizably yourself wherever you are (maybe louder in some places). That one can take a while.
- Questioning what you were programmed to believe while holding the discomfort, and without having to stop loving anyone over it.

Differentiation is slow, and it goes on your whole life. If a family, group or relationship won't let you do this while you're still part of it, temporary distance may be necessary. Some people come back later on different terms. Sometimes the leaving turns out to be permanent, because going back would mean disappearing again. You don't have to know yet which one yours is. As you differentiate, you learn to tell the difference.

Key developed this identity and differentiation portion of the method, drawing on Murray Bowen's family-systems work and her own self-therapy.

Let the Child Be Bad at Things

Reflection can show you what got sMothered (typo?) or suppressed. For identity you also need some experiments out in real life, so go try something new just for you. Maybe a foraging group, or a painting class. Stargazing can be awe-inspiring, and you might even see a UFO! Notice what holds your attention when nobody is impressed.

The old parent in your head may demand immediate talent, though, and then the first time you're awkward at it, that's "evidence" that you're stupid or clumsy. Or you hear, "Come on, you're not really the type." I think your little one is listening to see if anybody's going to stick up for them. The grown-up you're building has to be the one who does: "It's okay if we're bad at this, nobody's on trial. We're just seeing if we like it."

Give a new interest enough tries to get past the humiliation of being a beginner, when you keep telling people, "Sorry, I'm new." Maybe after that you still genuinely dislike it, and then you may quit. You may also discover a part of yourself that never had permission to exist. Either way, that's pl/ork in its most literal form: play becomes part of forming your identity. You might catch yourself wondering when you became someone who does this.

The Inner Guide

As the Guide, you choose what your little one learns and what it gets to experience, and you hold standards, the way a good parent would. Your parent's harsh tone doesn't come with the job, though. If you hear it coming out of you, keep the standard and drop the tone. You ask hard things of it so it can grow, at its own pace. That's what I was just asking of you, with that new interest. You also widen its world with art and music, and you point out beautiful things when you see them. Nature is part of that, and so is the wider world past the end of the street you grew up on.

Maybe you've let a small child win at Go Fish and never found a good time to start playing for real. Who wants to be the grown-up who finally wins? Without the Guide, you can slide from looking after your little one into spoiling it the same way. Sweets and screens can pass for loving yourself, and so can getting it out of anything hard. Comfort does better at set times, where it can't get out of hand. It shouldn't get canceled as a punishment after a bad day, either (ST-CA, section 17.1.4).

After all, sometimes “I want it NOW!” is just that: a rung on the desire treadmill. No hidden guard or old trauma required, because all creatures have endless desires naturally anyway, especially when immature. You can hear that want without shame about it. Hearing it doesn’t require giving into it, especially if getting your way would hurt you or somebody else.

I think the opposite trap can feel a lot like finally having your act together. It's forced adulthood, and it sounds like "Nope, all good!" from somebody with a fever who's cleaning the whole kitchen anyway. Not a lot of fun in that kitchen, and nobody gets very close to the person in it. If that's you, clamping down harder is going to feel like the fix, but it's just more of the same. I'd try croaking to someone you trust, "Could you pick up some soup?" Or maybe, "I'd really like to just watch a movie with you." If you keep swinging between spoiling and clamping down, try staying warm while you say what happens next, like "Not now, we'll go after lunch," and if you need a minute, say when you'll be back.

Once a need does show, after all that hiding, it's okay to want a lot of closeness. So then someone you love goes for a walk alone, and you've texted "hello??" before they're at the corner. When they get back, you sulk all night. Wanting them close is fine! The texts and the sulking aren't, and the Guide can help you find something that leaves room for both of you, like a hug at the door and a "see you in an hour." But hear your little one out first. I think it just wants to know they're coming back.

The Guide doesn't need to know everything for sure (that would be an OCD Guide, and then you'd need a Guide for the OCD Guide). It chooses the next sane step that fits your values based on the info available, and that's how it helps your little one grow toward a whole life. Waiting to be sure first is sometimes proper & prudent, but if it's a pattern, it can keep life on hold, and then your little one's world stays small. Sometimes something really is stuck on someone else's end (you've asked, and followed up, and there's nothing left for you to do yet), and waiting on that isn't avoidance. The Guide can let it wait, keeping an eye out for when it frees up, and keep the rest of your life moving, so your little one keeps growing anyway.

Forced adulthood can get into your head too, where it wants everything as spotless as that kitchen. At your grandpa's funeral, some dumb jingle starts up in your head and you nearly laugh during the eulogy. That doesn't mean you didn't love him, or that something's wrong with you. Minds come up with ugly and embarrassing stuff all the time, some of it the opposite of what you believe, and bodies do their own thing too. The Guide isn't there to police every thought. It notices the jingle and knows you didn't mean it, and that it isn't who you are. Then it goes with what you'd stand behind, which here is probably squeezing your mom's hand and listening, and lets the jingle go.

I think this is closer to Buddhist practice too. The point is not an impossible performance in which nothing unskillful ever appears. In one discourse (sutta), AN 6.63, the Buddha defines karma in terms of intention (so if you didn't mean to cause harm, your mind is not poisoned). In DN 2, he explains that in the training to purify the mind, one first recognizes a fault, makes amends, and then restrains themself to prevent that kind of thing in future. So it's not like, "you're either pure, or you're not," which is where a lot of folks stay stuck. If you're ashamed about something you chose to do, that might be a signal to give some attention to that. If it's shame just because of an intrusive thought, then it's probably not adaptive and can be simply let go of.

The Guide also points beyond itself, toward a belonging deeper than any group, one that doesn't ask you to give up being yourself. That might be nature, or existence itself. It might be whatever your tradition sees as ultimate (that part is optional). Love can come from any of these places, and you also belong there, which is a little different. Maybe you've felt that outside somewhere, like you didn't need anybody's okay to be there. The way in is time in nature and contemplative practice, like meditation.

With that under you, belonging with people can get a lot less desperate. Not all at once, but the Guide keeps watching for the moment your little one starts thinking, "If they stop liking me, I won't belong anywhere," and keeps reminding it that a group's approval isn't the deepest belonging there is. By the way, this job doesn't have to be called the Guide. The Wise One works, or your Inner Teacher, and "Guru" does for some people, though for cult survivors it can be a trigger word, which is understandable.

Timing Is Individual

Timing is different for everyone, but most of us need enough Nurturer and Protector first. Otherwise the Guide just overrules your little one in the name of something higher (spiritual bypassing, dressed up as direction). Like a shepherd who's beating sheep to make them move. So warmth and safety come first for a while, and the direction can grow once there's a clear little one in there to guide.

Spiritual openings keep their own schedule, too. Yours may have come long before you had much of a Nurturer or Protector, and it may have been completely real. The question afterward is whether it made your love deeper or wider. Has it reached your little one yet? And does it show with people, in being more honest and dependable, more protective and compassionate, better at relationships?

How Much Happiness Do You Know Is Possible?

What do you picture when you wish someone well? Probably them fed and safe, with people around who like them, stuff like that. Wanting that for somebody is love (babies get this part already), and practical love matters a lot. The horizon of what I can wish for someone is partly shaped by the happiness, peace, and freedom I know are possible.

But what if you've had a big spiritual experience, and it showed you a kind of peace, or a love, you didn't know existed? Then "I want you to be happy" means something much bigger, almost overnight. Loving-kindness meditation can sound comically simple to someone who's never been near that, which makes sense, and so powerful to someone who has. Repeating "may you be happy" isn't some automatic shortcut to profound love, of course. It's one way to find that love and grow it.

Curious about that bigger happiness? Then follow the curiosity, and skip pretending to believe. Read people from different traditions, and if you get the chance, spend time around someone who's happy in a way that doesn't add up, and see how they treat people when their own day is going badly.

Sometimes care isn't the main thing missing. A meal can help, and so can a boundary or a kind sentence, but is that actually targeting the deeper existential question underneath? Life shouldn't be just something you endure, like a hamster wheel or a rat race. What would you need to bring zest to a life of discovery?

Curiosity can be enough here, something like "Other people say they've felt a depth of love or peace that I never have. I don't know if I believe their explanation. But what if the experience is possible for me?" Don't assume the limits of what you've felt so far are the limits of what's possible.

Somatic Heart Opening

Rumi says you have to keep breaking your heart until it stays open. Some people find that deep grieving gives way to warmth, or to feeling connected. Some feel held, in the body. Being loved can open grief from the other side, too. Someone takes care of you, and you think, "Why does this make me sad?" It can be sadness for the care that was missing, and that doesn't mean the love failed. While you're present and still choosing, stay with it. Crying that deep can overwhelm you, too. It can retraumatize, mostly when nobody's with you or you've disappeared into the sadness. Once it's swamping you instead of opening you, slow down (like turning the song off for the night).

When the Practice Feels Real but Stays Shallow

Some people have the opposite problem. It feels like you're connecting with your little one, but the contact is staying near the surface. Meditating for a long time and then sleeping can open up much more emotionally direct stuff, especially in a second sleep, after you've already slept once. What showed me that the daytime pl/ork had stayed shallow was the difference at night. So say you wake up from a dream with far more feeling and need in it than the session came anywhere near. That's the sign this is you (a session that was good and just didn't stick around is something else). Then ask what that dream let you feel that the session didn't, and how you understand it now. And if nightmares start wrecking your sleep, do less of the practice, earlier in the day or for a shorter time, before you write off the whole thing.

Borrowed Adulthood in Relationship

Reparenting is usually talked about as something you do alone, or with a therapist. But your little one got hurt by people, so some of the healing has to happen with people too. Often it's mostly one person carrying a lot of it, maybe the one who drives you to your appointments and says it'll be okay. Maybe you're picturing them right now. Having someone like that while you're struggling can be an enormous blessing.

Say they're the one who always remembers your appointments. Over time, reparenting can help you take in some of what they've been doing for you, and start doing it yourself, even that calendar bit. What you're after is care your little one can count on from more than one place, while the inner relationship stays precious and important. Other jobs can go to friends or community, outsource to the tools or pros, whatever works. You'll find the balance is dynamic between inner & outer reliance, and going too far in either extreme isn't generally the healthiest.

Maybe you’d rather that your support person were a lover, as well. Lots of people open a dating app in the middle of the night, hoping someone will make it all stop hurting. 🙈 If that’s you right now, dating can wait while your friends and the steady people around you do their part. That doesn’t mean you have to be completely healed before you date, but romance should follow when two people really love each other and want to build a life together. It won’t rescue you from the pain or reward you for healing, and it sucks to mess up a beautiful relationship because of unhealed trauma, believe me, I know. I wrote a whole romance guide, mostly due to this.

First Social Connections, Done Right

Then there are people you’re just getting to know. Meeting them can turn into a kind of test--”First impressions are everything,” as they say, but it could be even worse than that, if you need them to prove you’re lovable. Nobody’s a reassurance machine. I’m giving you permission now to quit school and flunk the tests. Get curious about who this person is, and who you are, and let both of you have your own reasons for being there in the beautiful present. Leaning on each other is still a big part of relationships, and it goes both ways. In a real crisis, getting a safe person involved can be exactly the right move.

I can't tell you where to look for people who fit you, but I'd bet on more than one place. Keep the good people you've already got. Then maybe try a class, or a spiritual community if one really fits you. Not every one of them has to click (nobody's taking attendance). And if any group, or any one person, starts acting like they should be your only support, that's where I'd want the Protector paying attention.

The same goes for doctors. If a hospital held you against your will, or a treatment hurt you, no wonder asking for help feels scary. Bring someone you trust to speak up for you (it doesn’t have to be the relative who never respected your wishes), and ask what a treatment’s for.

Opening up honestly to the right people at the right pace

And reaching out doesn't mean telling strangers everything. Privacy isn't fakery. People can earn more of you by how they handle the smaller stuff, like whether that thing you told them about your weird fear of birds turns into a joke at the next party 🙈, and how they take it when you say no. But if a relationship is getting deep and you're still keeping up a fake normal, happy version of yourself to hold onto it, I'd either bring more of the truth in, carefully, or let it be less deep for now. (If telling could put you in real danger, like violence, or being outed where that isn't safe, protect yourself first.) And when someone finally accepts you, it can feel amazing. It still doesn't prove you're meant for each other, or that they've earned your trust yet.

Then there’s the stuff that’s hardest to tell anyone, and of course that's exactly what you need help with. Maybe it’s an urge that scares you, or something you did that could get you in legal trouble. You know that DM you deleted? It can still be sitting on their phone as a screenshot 🙈, and whatever’s on a company’s server can get handed over if a court asks. Just because Mr. Boopaloopa was your name on WhatsApp doesn't guarantee you're anonymous, by the way. And some of the people you’d tell, like therapists, teachers, and doctors, can be required to report certain things (a few laws cover everybody, too). Which things depends on where you live, and it’s nowhere near every crime you might mention. The cops are hopefully the obvious ones to be careful with, even if they pretend to care.

So I’d ask them, before handing over any tricky specifics: “What does the law require you to report?” That's a normal and expected question. Although personally I wouldn't even bother with that. You can always rephrase things as a dream narrative to get the emotional content across without triggering alarm bells (although I actually got in trouble with TalkSpace therapy bot even for a dream!). Just don’t hold back a danger that’s happening now from people who can help, and get anyone in danger safe before worrying about the rest. Sometimes telling on yourself is the thing that keeps someone safe, whatever it costs you legally.

Other Folks Also Have Hooks

Once you're in your happy place, look at the other person too, and don't go only by the one fight where they crossed your boundary, or, the other way around, by the one "no" or boundary of theirs that made you mad — after all, they're allowed to say no to you. Does it keep ending the same way, where you bring up something that hurt and then still end up reassuring them they're not a bad person, and the talk never comes back to what happened to you? (The psychologist Lindsay Gibson wrote the book on this: Disentangling from Emotionally Immature People.) Or can they hear you disagree and look at their own part?

If it's the first, it can seem like you haven't explained yourself well enough yet. You probably have, and it makes more sense to count on what they keep showing you they can do than on the right words finally getting through to them. Maybe what they can do is text you something kind the next day and not bring any of it up.

You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.

If a relationship keeps making you feel less safe, meditate on why it makes you feel unsafe, and what keeps you in it. Might it be due to a lack of the healed inner child/inner adult relationship? (If so, you're reading the right guide!) But if they've actually hurt or threatened you, even once, old stuff or not, get safe first, which is not a small thing at all, I know.

If you really did hurt them, repair it. Also easier said than done, but...

You can mean the apology for a start. Then notice what happens next. Are you sorry for what you did, or are you starting to take back the complaint too because they're still upset? Maybe your first explanation sucked. Try again. 😅 And if it fails again, and you've said what you mean, well, you can't guarantee they'll understand it. Guilt may keep shouting at you. That doesn't tell you the whole conflict was your fault, nor does it prove they're manipulating you.

Write It. Don't Send It Yet.

If you've got something burning you up that you wanna say, write it out as a reply. There. That got it out. Ahh. Breathing easier. 😌 But don't send it. Take that breather you earned and then come back to it from a cooler place.

It doesn't mean you need to hide your upset, or apply for a permit to protest, but choose your battles and execute them wisely, so you don't end up regretting how you communicated later. As Lao Tsu might have said (if I could ever have read that whole boring Art of War book), a war with no casualties is the best possible war. When high IQ & EQ communication hits a writer's block, sometimes the best thing is to simply say, "I need some time to think about my response." And if the conflict really requires a response right now, then obviously just do your best rather than wait for the perfect timing.

And yeah, going back to the writer, which is often my inner child when emotions pop up, is part of the process. It's like I'm holding his hand and showing him a better way, not like I just stole his pencil because he wrote something embarrassing. So what was he actually trying to say? A lot of the time it's just "that hurt."

Other times it's "I did this too." My friend Tyler saw a guy smoking in front of a baby and asked him if he thought that was wise. The guy asked if he thought it was wise to mind his own business, and Tyler said, "Well, I believe in Christ, so I thought you might care about life too." Afterward he wasn't sure he'd handled it right, or whether he should have thrown the first stone at all, since he'd smoked in front of a baby himself.

But Jesus also said to take the log out of your own eye first, and then "you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's eye." That's exactly what made Tyler the right person to say something. If you actually have the problem they have, and you want to call them out for it, call yourself out for it instead. What we came up with was something like, "Hey man, when I was younger I smoked in front of an infant, not realizing how selfish that was, and I notice you doing the same thing. I'd ask you to please have some compassion for him. I have a young child now, so this really touches me." Tyler's an evangelical Christian who preaches on YouTube, so the fact that he really appreciated this take on Jesus' message is pretty important to me.

It's also how your voice sounds, though. You can say all the right words, but if the tone is accusatory, it doesn't matter.

Before sending anything, I'd read it once as if I were them, sitting there with my phone. What felt scared when I wrote it can read like a threat from over there. I still won't know how they'll take it, but it might change a word or two.

Speak Toward What You Are Building

It's not so hard to understand that words have power. They point your attention somewhere, so aim them at what you want more of, whether that's something you'd like to be able to do or a relationship. Say you tell yourself "I'm not dependent." Well, now you're thinking about being dependent. Being totally independent also isn't realistic nor ideal, and can even seem like just a reaction to the original problem. We can often strike a nice nuanced poetic balance, like: "I'm provided for by the universe, like the birds & lilies Jesus reminded us of." With your little one, and in hypnosis especially, make it easy to picture: warmth, protection, steadiness, rest, whatever's really needed to melt into your happy place. You'll save a ton of money on vacations!

But let's also make sure we don't hide from shadow stuff with toxic positivity. Grief is grief, and letting tears flow can be immensely therapeutic, as long as they don't hijack your perspective with a cemented story of what will always be true. If your little one says "he hurt me," that's harm, and it gets called harm, even if it was also a learning experience. Same for anger, a no, who's responsible, what's possible in practice, all that. Then once the difficult experience is acknowledged by the Nurturer, the Protector & Guide can lead the way up to more safety and maturity.

Before giving anyone advice, your little one included, it can help to find out what kind of company they want: "Do you want me to mostly listen, help you untangle this, give you ideas, or some mix?" Not that you have to make a ritual of asking every time. If it's obvious what they want, go with that. If it isn't, that one sentence can keep you from advising somebody who's thinking, "I just need you to hear this." Or from just listening while they're asking for help thinking.

What they tell you is worth remembering, too, until they say something different. (And don't guess it from their gender or some other stereotype.) Same with advice you've already given: if nothing new has changed it, a quick "like I said" and moving on works better than offering it again like it's new.

Hearthwork: Taking Turns

The basic premise of Hearthwork, my peer-counseling framework, is: Equal time. Attention without interpretation, advice, or fixing. You speak. I listen. Then we switch. Presence, Warmth, Discipline, Confidentiality. It's kind of a third type of relationship, alongside ordinary friendship and professional therapy, and one your little one might really need, because modern life rarely gives you anything like it.

Some of what the three grown-up jobs do can come from outside you, too. When you take turns listening with a good peer, as equals, their warmth and the way they stay with you lend you the Nurturer for a while. Keeping what you say private, and stopping when the time's up, lends you the Protector. If you say no, they don't push. But they don't act as your Guide, or decide what your experience means. Maybe they're thinking, "Oh, I know what this is about," or "Here's what you should do." They keep it to themselves. A question from them at the right moment can help you hear your own Guide, and what you answer is up to you. Then you trade places, and it's your turn to keep "Here's what you should do" to yourself.

The peer doesn't become your permanent parent, though. They show you a capacity, help you get the hang of it, and give it back to you, which is borrowed adulthood in its healthiest form (a driving instructor isn't planning to drive you around forever either). Is that therapy? Well, not the mainstream kind, but yes, for sure. Professional therapy's still useful for what it does, and peers and community do something else, so a serious healing path can include both.

Community can come before the big opening or after it, too. Sometimes loving people give somebody enough safety and belonging that a deeper opening becomes possible. Other times it's the other way around. Somebody has a big opening first, a love or a meaning they couldn't have imagined before, and then they start to "manifest" different kinds of people and relationships in their life. Either way, the community is what helps make it practical, real, down to earth, embodied and so on.

This is part of why I connect inner-child pl/ork with Hearthwork and with building community. Having a beautiful experience on the inside isn't really the point. The love has to move, and it doesn't always go the same way round (the door you came in through matters less than the loop). From a spiritual experience to your little one, then into your relationship with the grown-up you, then out to other people, and back again.

How to Forgive Without Forgetting

For many people, healing does get around to some forgiveness eventually, or at least to not being run by the resentment anymore. It can't be put on a schedule (nobody gets a gold star for forgiving fastest). And you don't have to forgive first to get safe, set boundaries, hold someone accountable, get justice or get better. Then again, active resentment narrows what you see, and your little one stays organized around the injury. And if someone pressures you into forgiving early, what you get is an enlightened-looking act, with the wound gone underground.

The understanding that helps me is seeing how a person's conditioning, history, pain, and capacity shaped the harmful move they made. Picture everything that had made them who they were by that moment, what happened to them and what they learned, the pain they carried and what they could and couldn't handle. From in there, it was the only move they had. Hurt people often pass on pain they never worked through. You may have heard that from somebody telling you to forgive. When you see it for yourself, though, you might find yourself forgiving them, and if you've held them uniquely to blame, the rage can soften into something more accurate.

They still have to answer for what they did, and it still hurt. Seeing how they got there doesn't make it okay. It's just that punishing them now can't reach back and give them what they were missing then, the sense to see it or the help. Or the skills. What's left is fixing what can be fixed, and making it easier for something different to happen now. So a boundary can still be right. So can the law, if it comes to that, or telling them plainly it was wrong (or telling yourself, if they'd never hear it). Keeping away from them for good can be right too, and you can still forgive them from over there. Understanding somebody's limitations should make my expectations more realistic, not make those limitations less consequential. "I understand how you became this way" can sit right next to "I now understand why I shouldn't rely on you for this."

Forgiving yourself works the same way. Own what you did (only what was yours), and let yourself feel the remorse. Repair what you can, by apologizing or making amends. Changing the behavior counts too. Then you're allowed to stop holding yourself hostage, and the responsibility stays. If you're thinking "not me, not after what I did," forgiving other people tends to be harder too. Every so often, come back to how whoever did it got to be someone who could do that, until you understand it well enough that it changes how you feel.

When One Life Has Ended and the Next Hasn’t Begun

Your little one can get really loud in the gap after one life ends and before the next one has formed. A breakup will do it, or moving to another country, an illness, losing your faith or a role, your youngest moving out. So can leaving a family or community, or staying in one on new terms. A therapist or helper stepping back can do it too, after you've grown a lot. Tibetan Buddhism calls an in-between state a bardo. You don't have to buy what it teaches about death and rebirth to use the everyday part: endings leave a stretch where the old you is gone and the new one hasn't shown up.

Your little one might be sure everything's over. Is it? Usually one real thing ended, and there's other stuff you're still trying to keep going, maybe on autopilot. Some of it just isn't known yet, and unknown isn't the same as gone. Ask your little one what they're scared will happen in the meantime (worst-case scenarios are kind of their specialty).

Something basic can mark the ending, like boxing up the old keys. Nobody needs to know what door will open next. But when the not-knowing feels too heavy, a new identity can sure look tempting. Like a borrowed coat that doesn't fit but at least it's warm. But you can't go far with a borrowed coat anyway, so don't force the future on. Keep taking care of yourself, and grieve what ended with your little one beside you during the metamorphosis.

When you miss someone, let the ache be a cue for love. It doesn't have to become one more problem to solve, and pushing it away won't help. If love's there, "I miss you" becomes "I love you." Or there's the loving-kindness wish: "May you be safe. May you be held in love." You can picture them. Or don't, and just feel the care going their way.

All of this happens in you, so it doesn't matter whether they get any of it (nobody's checking their inbox). It doesn't stand in for grieving, though, or for getting in touch when that's right, and your boundaries still apply. Same with the practical stuff. And if it starts making you more compulsive, or a bit unreal, so you can't get back to your life, make it simpler: "I miss you, and I care." Some of that same care can go to the part of you that's still here.

Finding a Therapist to Borrow Adulthood

We all need some support, especially when taking our first steps. First and easiest is to start with finding a somatic therapist, if that resonates, as per my Somatic Therapies Guide.

Inner Child Reparenting therapists do exist, and you can find them, although they often are not licensed, and I don't know of any central directory, nor of any protocol they'd have in common. There are none which work with my guide yet. I can consult, and if you like the guide, you can ask a therapist to check it out and see what they agree with or disagree with as a great way to vet them.

The best licensed, listed therapists you'll find that come close will be doing Internal Family Systems (IFS), or Schema Therapy. I'll talk more about those in the appendix, but Schema Therapy comes closest to my approach. Open Path Collective (or a similar resource in your country) lists thousands of affordable therapists. You can filter them by Schema or IFS. You can often find even more Schema therapists at the official site.

Before choosing a therapist, get to know the strengths and pitfalls of their approach, including any modifications they make to the standard protocol. I'd especially want to interview them regarding how they treat spiritual experiences. You want someone who won't tell you what yours really mean, downplay them, force them, or treat them as a symptom. I'd also want one who doesn't put ideas in your head, and who understands dissociation. What have they found too challenging to deal with so far, and where have they seen the best results? A good therapist won't charge you an arm and a leg just for this kind of interview, and they should already have this information on their website to avoid wasting too much time. A certificate on the wall can't tell you any of that.

Regarding AI therapy, I've tried all the top AI therapy apps, and they're all pretty bad. I'm trying to make my own based on this guide, but I'm becoming more convinced that unsupervised AI therapy is just a toss up. That doesn't mean you can't get a lot of great insights sometimes when talking to AI, though, but just expect it to go sideways sometimes.

You Are Worthy of Love. Always.

It's the same love as back in Your Loan is PRE-APPROVED!, the Divine Love that can start as a little spark of … something when you give thanks. That kind, whether you'd say it comes from God or the universe itself or whatever, was there before you could earn anything. Love from a person is more conditional than that. Somebody's love running out on you still says nothing about what you're worth.

Be patient with yourself, and gentle, including with the part of you that thinks you should be over it by now. You are more than your pain and your past. That's easy to lose sight of when you've spent so long looking at them. And you aren't alone. There's help, and lots of other people are out there learning to look after their own little ones.

Appendix

How This Relates to Schema Therapy

Of the clinical approaches I know, schema therapy is the closest to this guide. Its therapists do what they call limited reparenting: within professional limits, they give you some of what a good parent would, with warmth and firmness and some playfulness too. Over time you take that in, and it becomes your own "Healthy Adult," who looks after your "Vulnerable Child" and stands up to the punishing parent in your head. It sets limits, too. And it gets on with adult life, like work and the things you've committed to. Sound familiar? That's a Nurturer, a Protector and a Guide by other names, and since the therapist's version becomes yours bit by bit, it's borrowed adulthood too.

Where it really differs is spirituality. Schema therapy doesn't build it in, though the preface to its main textbook calls blending it with meditation or religious practice an important development. That's not so surprising, since it grew out of cognitive therapy (Jeffrey Young, who developed it, did his postdoctoral work with Aaron Beck). So a loan of love from God, like back in Ask God for a Loan, isn't part of the standard method. IFS goes overboard the other way, in my opinion (more on that in the next section). Past that, a lot depends on who you're seeing.

How This Relates to IFS

Richard Schwartz, who developed Internal Family Systems (IFS), called one of his books No Bad Parts. Honor, understand and love every part, he says, and the protective ones are no exception, so "meet the protector first" would fit right in over there. That's a lot of the overlap with this method, right there in the title.

My reparenting method diverges from IFS at an important point. The IFS Institute's outline of the model says "everyone has a Self," and that the Self "can and should lead," once your parts stop "blending" with it. I emphasize the inner adult you build and practice occupying: Nurturer, Protector, and Leader, Guide, or Guru. That's still one grown-up with three jobs, like back in The Chicken-and-Egg Problem.

Can't find a calm, compassionate Self in there? Then borrow. One grown-up job at a time, from a friend who'll play Protector for a night, or from something you do, like letting the phone ring when you know how that call goes (The Parent You Inherited again), until it's yours.

Is a perfect Higher Self already waiting in there to be your guide or parent? I'd call that claim spiritual bypassing. I've had the Higher Self perspective myself, though. It's a somewhat less subjective way of seeing things as working perfectly in reality, despite the ego's illusions, which matches what people say about it. Where I part ways with IFS is the wisdom part. I don't think the Higher Self has any specific wisdom. It's a vantage point, where more wisdom and compassion can come up, but problems can also look like they were never problems at all from up there, and that can ironically lead instead to more foolish indifference. You can see both sides of this see-saw in the guru types who supposedly embody their Higher Self. Anthony de Mello is an excellent example. He was a Jesuit priest who mixed stories from all kinds of traditions into his teaching on seeing through the ego's traps and coming back to the Perfect Present. He also had a background as a psychotherapist who understood trauma-informed healing methods. He seemed to drift between the two opposite paradigms in a way that contradicted itself. And that bizarre contradiction is itself cited as "crazy wisdom" by apologists.

So inner child therapy draws on those higher vantage points more concretely. The inner Guide is the closest thing here to a Higher Self, and it has to work with the Nurturer and Protector and come after them. It can't act like some transcendent authority that waves everything away. Most reparenting doesn't do enough with the Guide, which is the opposite extreme from IFS, so I'm trying to bring together the best of both. IFS isn't exactly one system, so I may be simplifying it here. It has its own branches and disputes, and that's one more problem I'm trying to remedy by laying all this out systematically in this guide.

IFS calls these wounded young parts “exiles.” I find that pathologizing. I prefer to speak of a child who needs parenting and integration, rather than naming them by their wounded or excluded condition.

And the parts just keep coming: managers, firefighters, exiles, polarized parts, legacy burdens, Self-energy, guides, new subparts... At some point it's a maze, with a little one somewhere in the middle of it. A gentle practitioner can walk you through it fine. With others, people seem to come out more fragmented than they went in. Having a big institute with certifications behind it doesn't make a model any truer, either. Or safer, for that matter.

Related Clinical Work

This framework developed through the teachers and collaborators credited below and my own practice. Later revisions also compare notes with schema therapy, compassion-focused therapy, attachment repair, motivational interviewing, mentalizing, metacognitive therapy, behavioral activation, radically open DBT, emotion-focused therapy and spiritually integrated psychotherapy. I borrow particular distinctions and practices rather than presenting the whole synthesis as their consensus or claiming that their research validates it as a package.
