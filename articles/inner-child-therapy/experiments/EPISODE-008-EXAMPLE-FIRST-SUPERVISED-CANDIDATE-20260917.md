### And Then There's the Other Person

Have you ever come out of a fight convinced you'd just learned something terrible about someone, only to have them come back later and actually talk about the thing you were trying to say? When that happens, the first fight looks pretty different in hindsight. Maybe they needed time, or maybe you hit a boundary of theirs you really don't like; they can also just disagree with you. What's harder to explain away is seeing the same strange ending again and again: you bring something up, the conversation changes shape, and later you notice the original thing never really got dealt with. [Lindsay Gibson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) writes about emotional immaturity in terms of repeated behavior, which is a lot more useful here than trying to turn one awful fight into a diagnosis.

You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.

There's also a messier version where you really did hurt them, so there is something here you genuinely need to repair. Have you ever started with a real apology and then, because the other person was still disappointed, found yourself slowly apologizing for the whole disagreement? You're trying to repair something real, but somehow the apology keeps getting bigger until you're backing away from the thing you originally came to say too. Here there really was something to apologize for, but you know that from what happened, not from the guilt itself, and the guilt doesn't tell you the other person is manipulating you either.

Have you ever explained the same thing one way, then another, then caught yourself explaining why the last explanation came out wrong? It can go on like that for ages because what you're waiting for isn't another sentence. You're waiting for the other person to say, “Okay, I get it. Your no makes sense.” They may listen to every word and still never say it. Sometimes you've simply reached the end of what you have to say while they still think you're wrong, and you still have to decide what you're going to do.
