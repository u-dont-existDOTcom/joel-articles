## 1. *Emotional Alchemy*: a genuine spiritual–psychological integration

### It adapts loving-kindness to the particular wound

The most directly relevant passage is an exercise following chapter 15, headed **“If You Want to Blend Your Spiritual Practice with Emotional Work.”**

Bennett-Goleman suggests using a calm, clear period after meditation for a reparative wish. She starts with loving-kindness toward benefactors, oneself, loved ones, difficult people and all beings, then **adapts the wish to the schema**. That is considerably more specific than “mindfulness can complement therapy.” It is a method for bringing spiritual cultivation into contact with the exact emotional difficulty.

It is not identical to your **gratitude → experience of divine love → bring the little one into that love** sequence. But I would no longer imply that combining spiritual practice with emotional repair is itself the distinguishing feature of your guide.

### It includes a caring relationship with younger vulnerability

More directly, chapter 15 describes an inner dialogue between the **nurturing, compassionate aspect** and a hurt younger girl who tried to earn love through perfection. ## 2. *Mindfulness and Schema Therapy*: more than observation, but differently framed

This is a structured training manual rather than a broad spiritual synthesis.

Its theoretical chapter explicitly discusses the **Buddhist Psychological Model**, including attachment and aversion, impermanence, suffering, not-self, and spiritual enlightenment. However, the practical program is clinically framed; I did not find a corresponding method of drawing love from God or another divine presence. **“No spiritual content” would be inaccurate, but “a divine-love-based therapy” would also be inaccurate.**  
*Source: chapter 3, “The Buddhist Psychological Model (BPM) of mindfulness.”*

The actual exercises are important:

| Exercise | What it teaches |
|---|---|
| **7.1 — Mindfulness of the Healthy Adult and the Happy Child** | Attend to an adult capacity offering kindness, comfort, protection and calm boundaries, then to the child’s security, confidence and connection. |
| **7.3 — What Do the Healthy Adult and the Happy Child Need?** | Identify **what and whom** you need to function in those modes. Developed adulthood does not mean needing nobody. |
| **8.1 — Mindfulness of Yourself as Child and Adult** | Imagine a safe setting, approach manageable difficult material, express unmet needs, receive an imagined supportive response, and reflect on what happens. |

*Source: participant workbook, sessions 7–8.*

These exercises alternate **being-mode**—observing without immediately fixing—with **doing-mode**, which includes reflection and constructive response. Thus the program does not ask someone to remain a passive witness indefinitely.

On automaticity, its follow-up section says:

> “You cannot expect your newly learned skills to work automatically after just eight training sessions.”

The qualification matters: **after just eight sessions**, not *never*. It emphasizes continued, individually appropriate practice. Compared with *Emotional Alchemy*, it gives more space to organized practice and maintenance, but the Happy Child and positive experience are explicitly present.  
*Source: first follow-up session, “Continued development of a mindful orientation.”*

## 3. What this changes for your guide

**The main correction is to the comparison with other approaches, not to your core method.**

Your current appendix has already replaced the old “leaves spirituality out” wording. These books now let us say something more informative than merely pointing to Young’s preface: there are concrete schema-focused integrations involving Buddhist spiritual development, schema-tailored loving-kindness, and compassionate work with younger vulnerability.

I would describe your distinction as **the particular emphasis on the ongoing inner-parent relationship, gratitude, and bringing accessible spiritual love into that relationship**—not as the presence of spirituality where schema-focused approaches have none.

The clearest candidate for a small teaching addition is:

> **Match the form of care to what is actually needed, rather than repeatedly increasing the intensity of a generic loving message.**

That is my proposed application of Bennett-Goleman’s schema-specific practice, not her exact wording. Someone caught in perfectionism may need acceptance without performance; someone afraid to disagree may need support for honest expression. The guide already teaches individualized care, so this needs a placement check before becoming an addition.

I would **not** add more generic material about play, borrowing care, spiritual bypassing, or everyday integration merely because these books discuss it. Your guide already develops those themes.

Nor would I import every exercise unchanged. Imagining a caring parent does not establish that the actual person is safe; a painful phase is not automatically evidence of progress; and changing a schema should not become a demand that the little one disappear.

## 4. What the books cannot tell us

They demonstrate that substantial spiritual integrations **exist**. They do not establish how common those methods are among schema therapists.

They are also older sources: *Emotional Alchemy* was originally published in **2001**, and the mindfulness manual in **2014**. This was a focused examination of their relevant contents, not an updated efficacy review or a survey of contemporary practice.

**The strongest result is that your approach has closer precedents than we initially recognized—especially in *Emotional Alchemy*. Those precedents help clarify your method without requiring us to replace it or manufacture another backlog of additions.**
