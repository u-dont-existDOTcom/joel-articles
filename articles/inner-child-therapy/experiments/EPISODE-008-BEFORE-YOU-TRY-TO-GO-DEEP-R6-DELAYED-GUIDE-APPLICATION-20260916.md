# Before You Try to Go Deep

If the scared kid has taken over so much that you can't pause before acting or choose something you can actually follow through on, don't go deeper right now. Get somebody steady around you, or do whatever helps you come back enough to make a choice. You can come back to the child later.

## You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue

If you don't hear a little kid talking in your head, don't invent one. Some people barely have words running through their mind but can still think a sentence on purpose. Use words if and when they're actually there.

When I say “ask the child,” it doesn't have to turn into a conversation. Say the question out loud or write it down. Draw if that makes more sense. A picture or song may get closer than a sentence. Maybe all you notice is a sensation or an urge. Maybe nothing answers and you simply do something caring. You don't have to make vivid pictures appear just because the words aren't there. Quiet can just be quiet.

There isn't a right order for the feeling and the words either. Sometimes the feeling comes first, sometimes the words do, and sometimes you can't tell. Leave a feeling wordless if that's how it is. If words show up later and help you work out what happened, compare what might explain it, or decide what to do, use them then. You don't have to distrust the later thinking just because the feeling got there first.

## Maybe Calm the Body First

Sometimes you have plenty of words and your body is still too wound up for the conversation.

If your solar plexus, abdomen, or somewhere else feels clenched and it's hard to relax into that heart-chakra love, try a slower, non-forced exhale or some gentle massage. I love using one foot to massage the other like a Yin/Yang symbol. Circular or spiral massage over the tense area can be nice too.

Sometimes you need more bottom-up help than that: Shaking, tapping (Emotional Freedom Technique), EMDR, Brainspotting, Somatic Experiencing, Water Therapy, Massage, Energy Healing, or another approach that works from the body upward.

### Catch the Hook Before the Story Takes Over

Sometimes the first thing you notice isn't “child” or “protector” or anything you can name. It's just the hook. Your jaw clenches or your belly pulls tight; maybe heat comes up, or you suddenly want out. Then you notice half the text is already written, your hand is going for a drink, or you've started scrolling and explaining everything. Another time you're attacking, disappearing, making a promise, or deciding what the whole relationship means before you even know how you got there. [Pema Chödrön](https://pemachodronfoundation.org/product/taking-the-leap-book/) uses the Tibetan word *shenpa* for that tightening and urge before the whole story has finished forming.

Once you catch it, look around and say, “I'm hooked.” If nobody needs protection right now, take one to three breaths before acting. Those breaths aren't for solving the relationship. Feel the charge without asking it to prove anything yet. If one small adult move is available—some warmth, protection, or direction—do that. If not, wait.

For ten minutes, the text can stay unsent and the drink untouched. The thing you want to blurt out, promise, or decide can wait too. If something actually unsafe is happening, deal with that instead.

Don't use the hook itself as evidence that you're right or wrong. The other person may really have done something while old stuff is mixed into it; you may also have read the situation wrong. Work that out later.

When you're less activated, go back to the actual event. Somebody can cross a line now and wake up an old child-state or protector at the same time. Maybe what you find is a boundary, grief, something to repair, or care you never got. Start with what happened before deciding what it means. “My stomach tightened” is something you noticed. “That person is evil” is a judgment. What did they actually do? What supports that conclusion, what doesn't, and what else could explain it? If literal words were in your head at the time, keep them with the event. Words you find later can still help you understand it, but those are the later translation.

One breath is enough if that's all you can do. If turning inward makes you less oriented, less able to function, or more compelled, stop and get support from outside yourself.

### Don't Assume It's All You

If every conflict gets turned into “this is my wound,” the other person disappears from the picture. Sometimes they're actually doing something that matters. In the hard conversations, can they hear you disagree and look at their own part? Can they make room for your side, or do you end up taking care of their feelings until your own position is gone?

[Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity shifts the focus from guessing motives to what somebody repeatedly shows they can do. Sometimes you've already explained enough.

One awful conversation can just be one awful conversation. They may need time, or have a boundary too. But if the same thing keeps happening, that is the thing you actually have to live with. You may not know why they couldn't or didn't do differently that day. You still know what you couldn't rely on. If the behavior changes later, fine—change your assessment then. A promise by itself isn't that. You don't need another injury before protecting yourself.

Guilt isn't a verdict. You can really have hurt somebody and owe a repair while also giving up your own position because you can't stand their disappointment.

Before you go back, decide what enough means this time. Maybe being understood isn't happening. Sometimes saying no and ending the conversation is the whole job. Another time you only need to get one thing across. You don't have to defend yourself for forty minutes just because the other person doesn't understand.

While you're doing that, let the Witness stay with what is happening inside you. The Protector stays with what is happening between you and the other person. Then the Guide can choose the next move.

### Write It. Don't Send It Yet.

Write the response exactly as it comes—or just say it in your head. Don't send it yet.

Then read it again without treating the person who wrote it like a problem. Fix what is unfair or confusing, but don't erase a real protest, request, or boundary just because it came out angry. If you did something wrong, keep that responsibility too. Better doesn't have to mean nicer. Maybe after editing there are only three lines worth sending. Or maybe you wait and decide not to send anything. If you tell them you'll answer tomorrow, answer tomorrow. If you need protection now, do that now.

Then read the first draft again and stop fixing it. What was that person trying so hard to get across? Did they need protection, reassurance, or actual help? The first version isn't automatically childish. It can be angry and still contain the most mature protest in the whole exchange.

### When Healing Turns Into Checking

You can get hooked on healing too. The wound can be real and you can still be checking it for the hundredth time, trying to find out whether you've finally grieved it correctly.

Take care of whatever safety issue or practical thing is actually there. Then, if you feel pulled into another round, look at what changed last time. Sometimes you finally felt something you'd only been thinking about, or saw something new enough to change a decision. That's different from asking the same question again because the last answer didn't feel certain enough. Grief can come back to familiar ground without every return being a new request from the child.

If it's checking, leave the thought unfinished and go back to the person, activity, or life you chose. You don't have to argue with it, make it disappear, or wait until it feels solved. Caring for the child doesn't require a meeting every time the mind says something.

Before doing the same exercise again, know what it's supposed to help and what would actually look different if it were working. You can leave calmer and full of insight while the original problem hasn't moved. If the method keeps giving you no useful contact—or mostly makes you monitor yourself more while daily life gets worse—don't answer that by doing more of it. Keep what genuinely helps and try another way of meeting the same need.

If these exercises start feeling like a ritual you have to complete before you're allowed to live, stop there.
