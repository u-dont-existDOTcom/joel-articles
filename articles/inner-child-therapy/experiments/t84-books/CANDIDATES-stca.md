# Candidate additions from *Schema Therapy for Children and Adolescents* (ST-CA)

Christof Loose, Peter Graaf, Gerhard Zarbock and Ruth Holt, Pavilion, 2020. Mined for Joel's reader guide (`ARTICLE-READER.md`). Twelve candidates, then five care items, then short notes.

## How to read this

**Pages.** This edition's text has no printed page numbers, so every page below is a PDF page number (the first page of the PDF file is page 1; chapter 1 starts on page 19). In any other copy, search for the quoted words. Every quotation was matched against the extracted book text by script, and I re-found each one. This repository copy cuts each to a short phrase, since the repository is public.

**Guide references.** "L" numbers are line numbers in `ARTICLE-READER.md` (853 lines) as I read it. Headings are the stable reference if the file changes.

**Book words, defined once.**
- Mode: a part of you that takes over for a while, with its own feelings, thoughts and urges (close to the guide's "parts").
- Schema: a lasting pattern of belief and feeling from childhood, such as "nobody stays" or "I'm too much".
- Limited reparenting: the therapist gives some of what a good parent would, within professional limits (close to the guide's borrowed adulthood).
- Healthy Adult, also called Caring and Guidance mode: the grown-up part that cares, protects and guides (the guide's Inner Adult doing the three jobs).
- Punitive Parent and Demanding Parent: the inherited harsh voice that attacks who you are, or says you never do enough. The book also calls it the Inner Critic (the guide's "parent you inherited").
- Detached Protector: a protective part that goes numb, cool and distant.
- Flooding: being swamped by feeling so that you cannot think or stay in the present.

**Where the ideas come from.** The book trains therapists and coaches parents of children. The best material for an adult who is parenting themself is in the parent chapters (15 to 17), the play, puppet and imagery chapters (11 to 13) and the group-game chapter (18). Each entry says how the idea carries over.

**"Already in the guide?"** I searched the guide for each idea's key words and re-read the nearest sections. Ideas the guide covers well are left out (list in the notes at the end).

**Order.** Strongest first: the biggest gap in the guide, weighted by how many readers it would help. Entries 1 to 3 fit together and could be written as one new subsection of The Inner Guide.

## The 12 candidates

### 1. Good advice delivered in the old critic's voice is still punishment

- **Idea.** Sensible advice (rewards, consistency, "natural consequences") still hurts when it is delivered in the old harsh voice, and the child first obeys, then avoids. For a reader, the same thing happens when vows, routines and reviews are run by the inner critic instead of the Nurturer, Protector and Guide.
- **Book.** pp. 414–415, 447. "the attitude that the mother displays is still punitive" (p. 447, about a mother who vowed never to ground her son as she was grounded, then gave him a three-day TV ban as a "natural consequence".)
- **Guide.** Partly. "The Chicken-and-Egg Problem" (L25) says the inherited critic can take over a loving attempt; "And So That's the Way It Happened!" (L362) says to stop if the narrating voice sneers; "The Parent You Inherited" (L418–424) describes the critic's impossible ideal. None says that a correct practice done in the critic's attitude still counts as punishment, or what the little one does in response.
- **Where.** "The Parent You Inherited", a paragraph after L422, plus a one-line pointer in the "Simple Practices" opening (L324).
- **Why.** It gives a reader a test for their own practice (is the little one relaxing, or only complying and going quiet?) and explains why doing the exercises "right" can still fail.

### 2. The third way: not soothing-then-snapping, and not "the opposite of my parent"

- **Idea.** Two traps: vowing to be the opposite of a harsh parent with no picture of what to do instead (so firmness feels like the parent, you hold it back, and it returns stronger), and swinging between rushing to comfort and snapping when the upset goes on. The book's middle path is to stay in the calm adult seat with sympathy plus clear next steps, and to step away for a stated short time (say you will be back in five minutes) instead of sinking into the sadness yourself.
- **Book.** pp. 449–450, 516–519. "there is still a third way", besides swinging between sympathy and angry ranting (p. 518, from the "Ron" case, a mother whose 9-year-old cries at school.)
- **Guide.** Partly. "The Inner Guide" (L661–667) names two failure poles, spoiling and forced adulthood (the "opposite trap"), and the "hello??" sulk. It does not describe swinging between them, avoiding all firmness because firmness feels like the parent, or comfort that gets stuck in your own sadness. "Make a Simple Vow" (L294) is realistic about "never react badly" but gives no middle posture.
- **Where.** "The Inner Guide", after the forced-adulthood paragraph (L665).
- **Why.** Readers who promised never to treat themselves as they were treated often swing, and a named middle posture (warm, clear, time-limited) gives them somewhere to stand.

### 3. Firm without harsh: the Guide's voice, posture and words

- **Idea.** A serious voice is not the same as a harsh one: in the book a mother notices her voice turning into her father's strict tone, says what she needed from him (calm words, an explanation, what to do next time), and finds a different tone for the same message. A second example has the helper ask which posture and body language fit the calm adult seat.
- **Book.** pp. 495–496, 518. clear guidance "even with a serious voice, but without these harsh words" (p. 495; the therapist's line to a mother whose voice had become her father's "strict derogatory tone".)
- **Guide.** Partly. Tone appears only for dealing with other people ("Write It. Don't Send It Yet.", L757: "if the tone is accusatory, it doesn't matter"), and "Let the Child Be Bad at Things" (L653) gives one Guide sentence ("nobody's on trial"). "The Inner Guide" (L657–677) is about direction, standards and belonging; it does not cover how a firm sentence should sound or how to catch the parent's tone in your own voice.
- **Where.** "The Inner Guide", right after the opening paragraph (L659).
- **Why.** Readers who dread sounding like their parent often drop firmness altogether (entry 2); a way to hear and replace the tone lets them keep the limit and lose the cruelty.

### 4. Protection comes in degrees, not on or off

- **Idea.** The book first shows that everyone needs some protection (clothes, helmets, speed limits), then offers a range of covers: mosquito nets of different weights, blinds with adjustable slats, sunglasses, headphones. You ask which cover fits a given situation and when you could peek out, so the guard is treated neither as an enemy nor as all or nothing.
- **Book.** pp. 283–286. "there are more than the two options of protection or no protection." (p. 283; the book's example is the numb, distant guard, with a dark net for days when parents argue and a lighter one most days, p. 285.)
- **Guide.** Partly. "Hear the Protective Part First" (L454–462) says the part "doesn't have to move out" and "can keep watch while you take one small step", with the roommate image; "When to Change the Strategy" (L464) covers changing course. There is no range of settings and no asking the guard which one it wants for the situation.
- **Where.** "Hear the Protective Part First", after the "keep watch" paragraph (L462).
- **Why.** When a guard will not step back fully, the reader has something to offer: a lighter setting for now, and a one-minute peek out to test.

### 5. Reject the message, not the parent

- **Idea.** Give the harsh voice a name of its own, such as "Inner Critic" or "Punisher", instead of a name that includes "Parent"; reject what it says while speaking of the person with respect; and keep a few "islands of good memories" of times the parent met your needs. The book says children stay loyal and will side with their family if pushed to side against it.
- **Book.** pp. 290, 297. The messages are rejected "without rejecting the parents themselves" (p. 297).
- **Guide.** Not covered. "The Parent You Inherited" (L418–424) labels the voice a parent script, and "How to Forgive Without Forgetting" (L781–789) separates understanding from excusing. The guide's own phrase "inherited critic" (L25, L129) comes closest. Nothing offers a name that sidesteps the loyalty clash or suggests keeping a few good memories in view.
- **Where.** "The Parent You Inherited", a paragraph after L420, with a pointer from "How to Forgive Without Forgetting".
- **Why.** A reader who feels disloyal when answering the critic can disagree with a sentence without accusing a person.
- **Limit.** Make it optional, and skip it for readers whose parents were dangerous. The book assumes living parents who are coached alongside the child and who had some real good moments.

### 6. A menu of what a child needs, and the need you over-serve

- **Idea.** A short list of needs: attachment (loving relationships, belonging), autonomy (self-reliance, control over your surroundings), self-esteem, play and joy, and an overarching need for consistency (structure, boundaries, predictability). It is common to meet one need strongly and neglect the others, and the list makes that visible.
- **Book.** pp. 36–37, 129–132. Often "an emphasis on one need to the neglect of other needs" (p. 130; in the "Chiara" case, closeness and self-chosen staying home scored high while self-esteem and play scored low.)
- **Guide.** Not covered. The guide asks what the little one needs (L362, L386) but gives no list to choose from, and "You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue" (L113) leaves a reader with nothing to go on if no answer comes.
- **Where.** "You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue" (a fallback: pick from the list when nothing answers) and "One Intention in the Morning, One Review at Night" (L364; add the question "which need did I meet today, which did I skip?").
- **Why.** Readers who hear no answer get a concrete list to choose from, and the evening review can show lopsidedness such as all comfort and no play.

### 7. Recognition lists: family rules and family roles

- **Idea.** Two lists that help a person see what they were handed. One is a list of statements about how families run ("Boys do not cry", "Children should be seen and not heard") to tick off; the other is the roles a child can be handed: stand-in partner, the parent's idealised self, scapegoat, whipping boy (takes the anger meant for someone else), stand-in parent.
- **Book.** pp. 418–419, 428–430. A list of family statements: "which ones apply to your family?" (p. 430; follow-up questions ask about unwritten rules, which values to keep and what to do completely differently.)
- **Guide.** Partly. "Untangling the Belonging Bargains" (L616: a family may "want a particular kind of son or daughter"; L618: the "human doer") and "Sometimes There Isn't a Clear Child Yet" (L598: belonging only by playing somebody else) touch the roles, and "The Parent You Inherited" (L420) gives one example rule. There are no named roles, no list of rules to recognise, and nothing on taboos (what could not be talked about).
- **Where.** "Untangling the Belonging Bargains", after L616, as a short checklist.
- **Why.** Many readers cannot name what was expected of them until they see it written down, and recognising a line on a list is quicker and safer than reflecting from scratch.

### 8. Write out the critic's actual lines, with who said them and when, and draft replies

- **Idea.** Write the mean things you were told on slips of paper with who said them and when, then draft replies that are firm and funny but not insulting, and write what you needed to hear instead. In the book's role play the wise side comforts the hurt child first and only then answers the critic.
- **Book.** pp. 563, 569, 573. replies to each phrase that are "not aggressive or insulting, but funny and assertive" (p. 563, a children's group game; the words-you-would-rather-hear list is on p. 569, and the comfort-first role play on p. 573 ends with each player stepping out of the critic's role.)
- **Guide.** Not covered. "Journal With Each Hand" (L344–348) has the little one write and the adult answer, and "Catch the Hook" (L326) notices the hook, but neither uses a list of the critic's own sentences or drafts replies.
- **Where.** "The Parent You Inherited", as a short practice after L422, or a new entry in "Simple Practices".
- **Why.** Specific sentences with names and ages can be answered, which a vague bad feeling cannot, and the comfort-first order keeps the exercise from turning into a debate.
- **Limit.** Pair it with "Close the Session and Return to Life" (L396), and keep the replies aimed at the sentences, not at a person.

### 9. Indirect ways in: figures on a table, a throne and a weather report

- **Idea.** Pick small figures for your parts (the book says at least five, each named: a protective part, a hurt child, a wise child, a critic, a caring parent), line them up, put the part in charge of a situation on a "throne", and ask who could move it off. Start a session with a weather or colour report instead of "what are you feeling?", which can set off the avoidant part.
- **Book.** pp. 317, 335, 339, 368–370, 536. "adults readily adapt to finger puppets for therapy and self-awareness" (p. 335; the figures cost about $2 each and the book suggests choosing from 20 to 40; the weather report is on p. 536.)
- **Guide.** Mostly not covered. A teddy bear stands in for the little one (L109, L306), the baby-doll comparison appears at L32, and L117 offers speaking or writing to readers with no inner voice. There are no figures for the other parts, no arranging them and no weather check-in.
- **Where.** "You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue", as an option for readers who hear nothing, or "Start With Whatever Showed Up" after L434.
- **Why.** Readers who feel fake talking to an empty room get something they can see and move, which also suits the guide's pl/ork.
- **Limit.** Table-top only. The book's chair version is therapist-led (care item 1).

### 10. Sort guilt into four piles, and apologise from the adult side

- **Idea.** Sort guilt into four piles: real facts, guilt imposed by others, guilt built so you would not feel helpless, and guilt learned from the critic; each pile needs a different response. When you apologise to the little one, do it from the adult side and do not hand them your distress to carry.
- **Book.** pp. 480–483. "Each individual component of guilt needs a different therapeutic response." (p. 480; the helplessness pile is the book's "rather guilty than powerless", and the apology advice is on p. 483.)
- **Guide.** Partly. "Love Doesn't Have to Wait for Trust" (L271–282) covers blame as a way to feel in control and gives six questions; "Not Every Hero Wears A Cape" (L243) covers apologising to the little one after an internal attack; "How to Forgive Without Forgetting" (L789) covers forgiving yourself. Not covered: sorting by source (especially guilt handed to you by people who needed someone to blame, and guilt that is only the critic's), or apologising without leaving the little one holding your distress.
- **Where.** "Love Doesn't Have to Wait for Trust", after the six questions (L282), or "How to Forgive Without Forgetting" at L789.
- **Why.** It shows a reader which guilt is theirs to repair and which was never theirs, so repair and self-forgiveness stop blurring together.

### 11. Joy and comfort as standing appointments, never a penalty

- **Idea.** Keep small, frequent pleasures (they beat big rare treats), start a session with something good (about four good items to one painful one), and never cancel comfort as a penalty. If part of you fears that comforting will make you soft, give that part a fixed pampering slot so it cannot "get out of hand".
- **Book.** pp. 375, 500, 512–513. "Without joy, other skills or learned competencies will not be kept" (p. 512; the "acting like a baby" hurdle and the fixed slot are on p. 500, and the advice not to withhold bedtime comforts as punishment is on p. 513.)
- **Guide.** Partly. Play (pl/ork, L428, L602–606), "enjoy them for a minute" (the Borrow Love section, L195), appreciating the good in the evening review (L378) and the warning against spoiling (L661) cover some of it. Not covered: opening a session with something good, keeping comforts safe from the critic's penalty system, and a fixed slot for the part that fears comfort will make you soft.
- **Where.** "Close the Session and Return to Life" (L396; add "end on something good") and "The Inner Guide" (L661, the spoiling paragraph; add a fixed pampering slot).
- **Why.** Readers who cannot comfort themselves for fear of going soft, or who cancel treats after a bad day, get a rule that makes comfort safe to give.

### 12. A trial run before imagery

- **Idea.** Before imagery, run a 30 to 60 second trial with a neutral image (a sunflower, a tree, a cake) and check that you can engage, zoom in and out on request, and stay free of flooding and of confusion afterwards. The book adds a safe place visited before and after, an imagined vault for distressing images (you choose who holds the key), and a "channel change" to switch the programme.
- **Book.** pp. 380–384. Is the child "able to engage without becoming overwhelmed emotionally (flooding)"? (p. 383; the case on p. 381 shows a safe-place relaxation exercise bringing up an abuse the family had not reported to the therapist.)
- **Guide.** Partly. "Before You Try to Go Deep" (L89–95) checks that the adult is present, asks whether you can "stop in the middle and come back to the room", and suggests starting with eyes open; "Your Body Might Need Some Love First" (L107–111) says to judge by the next few days and ease off; "Close the Session and Return to Life" (L396) gives the Present Time ending. Not covered: a short test of the imagination itself, or the vault and channel-change tools.
- **Where.** "Before You Try to Go Deep", after L93.
- **Why.** A two-minute, low-stakes test shows whether imagery suits today, and the exits get practised before they are needed.
- **Limit.** See care item 1.

## Care items (conflicts or clinician-only material)

1. Chair role-play and imagery are therapist-led in the book: imagery is an advanced method that "should only be used by experienced and trained therapists" (p. 390), talking to a parent in an empty chair "can be an intimidating and scary experience" even for adults (p. 353), and conditions needing extra caution are listed on pp. 380–381, so entries 9 and 12 should stay in their smallest, eyes-open form.
2. The book says harsh inner parents are "disempowered or banished" (p. 491) and argues against them, while the guide hears protective parts first and makes no enemies; apply it only to the inherited script's sentences (entries 5 and 8), never to guards (entry 4), and heed the book's warning not to sit in the critic's chair for long (p. 491).
3. The book lets anger vent ("Angry Child modes should be allowed to vent anger", p. 250) and its group games urge children to "Let go of all the anger that you are feeling" (p. 554), which can flood and differs from the guide's habit of listening for the one-sentence complaint under the swearing (L265); use the book's idea that anger covers hurt (p. 564), not its venting games.
4. Child-management tools that would become self-punishment if aimed at the little one: losing an attractive activity until an apology is done (the child may "miss out on an attractive activity", p. 514, which sits oddly beside the book's own ban on withholding comforts, p. 513), holding a violent child (p. 509), and the "trickster" story (p. 508) where a boy says "you're hurting me" to be let go, which could be misread as a reason to doubt the little one's pain (compare L763).
5. Two framings could read badly to survivors, in "small doses", negative events can have a "resilience-building and empowering effect" (pp. 515–516, meant to ease parents' perfectionism) and "islands of good memories" (p. 297), and the whole book assumes an outside therapist who models the Healthy Adult (limited reparenting), where the guide uses borrowed adulthood and peers.

## Notes

**Left out because the guide already covers them well.** Treating a skipped practice as information and asking with curiosity ("When to Change the Strategy", L464–472); repairing after an internal attack, and the vow ("Not Every Hero Wears A Cape", "Make a Simple Vow"); hearing a protective part in the Nurturer's voice ("Hear the Protective Part First"); blame as a grab for control (L271); appreciating the good in the evening review (L378); coming back after being rebuffed (book p. 511; guide L300, L462); exits and a gentle return (L89–95).

**Seen, not given a slot (not counted above).**
- Ask "how old does it feel?" when a feeling arrives (p. 332).
- Skin thickness: the same hit lands harder depending on how rested and looked after you are (p. 321).
- Glasses and an old film as images for how past hurts colour the present, including turning down the volume and brightness of the film (pp. 277–280).
- Signs that a numb, distant guard is in charge: saying everything is fine, "I don't know", sounding very rational, boredom, wanting to play instead of talk (p. 281).
- The order rule: comfort the hurt part first, then deal with the angry part (p. 61).
