# Possible additions from "Schema Therapy with Couples" (2015)

**Book:** Chiara Simeone-DiFrancesco, Eckhard Roediger and Bruce A. Stevens, *Schema Therapy with Couples: A Practitioner's Guide to Healing Relationships* (Wiley-Blackwell, 2015). It is written for therapists. Where the book gives a step as therapist-led and I have reshaped it for a reader, it is marked "Adaptation (mine)".

**Short answer:** Yes, mainly for the romance guide. Ten of the twelve candidates fit the romance guide and two fit the inner-child guide. The book's one-person ideas (an inherited inner critic, a grown-up self who protects the hurt child) are mostly in the inner-child guide already, under other names. What the book adds is what happens between two people: the loop they build, how to stop it, and how to repair.

## How to read this file

- **IC** is the inner-child guide (ARTICLE-READER.md). **RO** is the romance guide (master.md). Headings come first; a number after IC or RO is a line number in that file as it stands today.
- Page numbers are the book's printed pages. Every quote from the book was checked word for word against the extracted text. This repository copy cuts each one to a short phrase, since the repository is public.
- "No hits" means none of the search words I name matched anywhere in that guide. It does not prove the idea is missing under other words.
- The book often passes along other authors (Gottman, Hendrix, Heru and Hamel, Barnish). Check those at the source before stating them as fact.

## The book's words in plain terms

- **Schema:** a lifelong pattern, set by needs that went unmet in childhood, that makes some situations hurt more than they should.
- **Mode:** the part of you that is in charge at a given moment.
- **Healthy Adult:** the calm, caring, sensible you. Same job as Joel's Inner Adult (Nurturer, Protector, Guide).
- **Vulnerable Child / Angry Child:** the little one when scared or sad / when angry.
- **Punitive Parent / Demanding Parent:** the inherited critic or warden (*you are bad* / *you must be perfect*).
- **The four coping moves:** *Compliant Surrender* = give in. *Detached Protector* = go numb or cold. *Detached Self-soother* = escape into something soothing (screens, drink, work, food, sex). *Overcompensator* = come out on top and fight (attack, control, boss).
- **Mode cycle:** the loop two partners' coping moves create, each move setting off the other's.
- **Mode cycle clash-card:** a worksheet about one fight, with one column per partner.
- **SUDS:** a 0 to 10 self-rating of how upset you are.

## Candidates, strongest first

### 1. ROMANCE GUIDE: See the repeating fight as a loop the two of you build together

- **Idea:** Each person's coping move (attack, give in, go cold, escape into soothing) sets off the other's, so the two moves feed each other round and round. The book names five common loops, says couples often run a chain of them (p.123), and tells the pair to treat the loop, not the partner, as the enemy.
- **Book:** pp. 23, 72, 91-93, 123. Quote (p.23): "The cycle is the problem. No cycle—no hurting."
- **The five loops (pp.91-93):** both fight; one fights and the other escapes into soothing; one fights and the other goes cold; both withdraw ("living together apart"); one fights and the other gives in.
- **Already in the guides?** Only a sliver. RO 22 (in the opening section, What we mean by “love”) describes the both-pull-back loop in one paragraph, and RO 369 asks what "I shut down" means. Nothing names the other four loops or says the loop is the enemy. IC has nothing like it ("A Heart-to-Child Loop" is a different thing, inside one person). Search words in RO: loop, cycle, vicious, spiral, escalat, pursu, withdraw, shut down.
- **Where:** RO "Are you ready for the crucible?" (387), right after the "five years old" paragraph (RO 413); a pointer from RO "If you're already in it" (823); one line in IC "Other Folks Also Have Hooks" (729).
- **Why it helps:** It turns "you always" and "I always" into "we keep doing this", which lowers blame and gives both people something concrete to spot.
- **Mind:** The book calls fight-and-give-in "stable" (p.92). Stable is not the same as fair or safe (care item 1).

### 2. ROMANCE GUIDE: Agree a stop signal and a break with a promised return

- **Idea:** While calm, agree that whoever first notices a fight starting shows a sign (the book's example is a raised hand forming a T, like the sign that stops a basketball game). Both stop at once without another word and go to separate rooms for an agreed time, and the one who called the break comes back at that time and checks that both are calm enough to talk.
- **Book:** pp. 22, 165-166, 170, 213, 241. Quote (p.22): "Whoever is the first to notice … shows the sign"
- **Detail:** The book's example couple used a 0 to 10 distress rating. Whoever hit 6 first called the break and promised to return in 30 minutes, and they talked only once both were under 6 (p.170). **Adaptation (mine):** in the book a therapist coaches this first; a reader would set it up alone with a partner. Saying the return time out loud is also mine, because a break with no time attached can feel like leaving.
- **Already in the guides?** Partly. IC 745-749 ("Write It. Don't Send It Yet.") has "I need some time to think about my response"; RO 453 says to schedule alone time, RO 433 gives breathwork, and RO 803-807 suggests setting a time soon to talk in a kinder way. No hits in RO for: time-out, cool off, cool down, take a break, stop sign, pause, walk away, leave the room, separate room. Nothing sets a signal, a length and a promised return.
- **Where:** RO "Are you ready for the crucible?" after RO 433; a cross-reference in IC "Write It. Don't Send It Yet." (745).
- **Why it helps:** A break only calms things if the person who leaves does not seem to have walked out, and agreeing the sign, the length and the return in advance makes leaving a kindness.
- **Mind:** Not for a relationship where one partner is afraid of the other (care item 1).

### 3. ROMANCE GUIDE: A concrete safety plan, and the extra danger around leaving

- **Idea:** For someone who feels unsafe, the book lists practical steps: memorize emergency numbers, build support, open a separate bank account if possible, rehearse an escape plan, leave spare keys, money, clothes and copies of papers with someone trusted, and know where refuges are. It also warns that violence is more likely in the six months after a separation, so leaving needs a plan.
- **Book:** pp. 197-199 (checklist on p.198; Goldner on trusting your fear, p.197). The book (p.199), citing Barnish (2004): violent episodes are likely to increase "during the six months after separation".
- **Already in the guides?** Only one-line warnings: RO 393 ("think about safety first"), RO 895 ("unless it's unsafe for you to stay"), RO 903 (names abuse and coercion), IC 739 and IC 440 ("get safe first"). No steps, and nothing says leaving is itself a risky moment. RO 887 ("If leaving feels right, then just go") has no exception for a frightening partner. No hits in either guide for: hotline, safety plan, refuge, shelter, escape plan, restraining, protective order, emergency (the hits for bank account, escape plan, crisis line and police at IC 191, 478, 574 and 671 are unrelated).
- **Where:** Expand RO 393 (in "Are you ready for the crucible?"); add to "Ending consciously" (881) before RO 887; a line in "After leaving" (901); one pointer at IC 739.
- **Why it helps:** A reader who is scared of their partner gets something to do today and is told when the risk is highest.
- **Mind:** The checklist is second-hand from 2005 to 2007 (Heru; Hamel), is addressed to women, and mentions payphones. Check it against a current national domestic-violence hotline's own safety-planning guide before publishing. I would treat this one as top priority whatever its rank.

### 4. ROMANCE GUIDE: Sort the want from the need under it, and ask for a want as a wish

- **Idea:** A fight about a concrete want (move to Alaska or to Arizona) often hides a need both people share (both needed to feel safe). Name the need and the two of you have room to find a third option; the book also says to ask for a want as a wish, which leaves the other person free to choose (p.106).
- **Book:** pp. 106, 215-224, 231; the Paul and Carina example is on pp.221-223. Quote (p.231): "from a concrete want to the more general need"
- **Detail:** Paul saw safety as money and Carina saw it as family nearby; they ended up in a third place that was neither Alaska nor Arizona (p.223).
- **Already in the guides?** Pieces only. RO 421 says expecting a partner to know what you need is entitled; RO 746-752 has a "say the actual thing" jealousy script; RO 803 has an "unappreciated" script. No hits in either guide for asking for a want as a wish, want versus need, or "underlying need" (the hits for "wish" are unrelated uses).
- **Where:** Next to RO 421 in "Are you ready for the crucible?", or in "If you're already in it" (823).
- **Why it helps:** It gives two people stuck on opposite answers a way out that is not "who wins".
- **Mind:** The book says needs are non-negotiable and that disputing one is bullying (care item 4). Take the sorting step, not the label.

### 5. ROMANCE GUIDE: "Rewind the video", a redo with agreed words, plus ready-made repair phrases

- **Idea:** After a moment goes badly (the book's example is the greeting after a day apart), the person who walked in goes back outside and replays the scene the way they meant it, and the other answers in a new, edited way. The book also passes on short repair phrases from the Gottmans, such as saying you missed what the other was trying to do (p.61).
- **Book:** pp. 61, 163-164. Quote (p.164): "goes back outside" and "returns to replay the scene"
- **Already in the guides?** No. RO 365 asks "How did you repair afterward?" as a question for judging a past fight but gives no method. IC 741-743 says "repair it" and "Try again", which is about your own apology, not a joint redo. No hits in RO for: rewind (RO 778 is an unrelated imagination prompt), replay, redo, do-over, start over, try again.
- **Where:** RO "If you're already in it" (823); a cross-reference at IC 741-743 (in "Other Folks Also Have Hooks").
- **Why it helps:** It gives a bad moment a light, doable ending that is easier than a long talk.
- **Mind:** Skip the book's story where a husband cites the wedding vows to demand a warm greeting whatever his wife felt (p.164); it cuts against RO 720-732 (honest vows).

### 6. ROMANCE GUIDE: Strong chemistry can be familiarity, not proof of a good fit

- **Idea:** The book says couples often choose each other because the partner feels familiar (it re-creates childhood feelings), and "chemistry" then gets read as proof of love. Two people can fit together well and still have no room to grow or leave (p.52), so a strong pull is a reason to look at your history, not just a green light.
- **Book:** pp. 51-54. Quote (p.51): "“Chemistry” is often seen as a proof of love"
- **Already in the guides?** Weakly. RO 213 ("a bit like coming home"), RO 231 ("attracted to the inner-child part"), RO 247 and 263 (benefit of the doubt; recognize your pattern), RO 365 ("do you keep falling for the same stuff?") and RO 419 touch it. None says a strong pull can come from familiarity, or that a perfect fit can lock two people in place.
- **Where:** After RO 213 in "The window of clarity, and how it closes" (207), or in "Beware idealization in either direction" (265).
- **Why it helps:** It lets a reader take a strong pull seriously without treating it as proof.
- **Mind:** This is the authors' clinical theory (they borrow Young's "schema chemistry"), not a tested finding, so say so. Avoid their examples that pair people with abusive partners (pp.51, 53).

### 7. INNER CHILD GUIDE: The give in, blow up, feel guilty, give in again see-saw

- **Idea:** Someone who gives in for a long time stores up anger until it comes out as an outburst, then the harsh inner voice brings guilt, which pushes them back into giving in. The way out is to let the angry part's energy fuel a small, calm "no" that the adult actually carries out, instead of an outburst.
- **Book:** pp. 134-135, 145, 156. Quote (p.134): "the energy of Angry Child to avoid excessive submission"
- **Already in the guides?** Parts only. IC 241 (set one boundary), IC 259-265 (anger at bedtime), IC 460 (saying no and not texting an hour later to take it back), IC 474 ("saying yes to everything again"), IC 640 ("why was I nodding along?"). Nothing names the full swing or says to use the anger as fuel. No hits in IC for: see-saw (IC 837 is an unrelated use), blow up, outburst.
- **Where:** IC "Two Common Protective Patterns" (448), with a cross-reference in "When to Change the Strategy" (464).
- **Why it helps:** A reader who swings between doormat and explosion sees one pattern, not two separate failures.
- **Mind:** The book's example, Tim, also sexually pushes on his wife (p.135); take the pattern, not the case. P.156 says it the way IC does: the anger gives the energy, but the action comes from the adult.

### 8. ROMANCE GUIDE: Draw one fight on paper, then swap cards

- **Idea:** After a fight, each person alone fills in a short card: what set me off, the critic's sentence I heard, what I did, the hurting part underneath, the need, the wish, what a calm adult would do, and what happened. Then you swap cards and read the other's as if standing in their shoes.
- **Book:** pp. 122-126 (the card is on p.122; Tom and Betty on pp.124-125; couples fill in their own cards and compare them when calm on p.126) and pp. 165-166 (the order: stop, separate rooms, fill cards alone, compare, suggest fixes together). The book (p.122): the way out of the loop is to find the blocked feelings and "relate them to unmet core needs".
- **Detail:** In the book's example the fixes were small and concrete: five minutes to explain before she reacts, and a text before the agreed time (p.125). **Adaptation (mine):** the book teaches the card in sessions first; my plain-word rows drop the mode labels, so check them against p.122 before publishing.
- **Already in the guides?** No. Nearest are IC 745 (write the reply out, don't send it) and RO 813 (write down what each of you learned). The card games at RO 217 and 718 are for choosing a partner. No hits in either guide for: clash-card, worksheet, swap, exchange cards.
- **Where:** RO "If you're already in it" (823); a link from IC 745.
- **Why it helps:** Writing moves the fight from the body to the page and shows each person what the other was feeling.
- **Mind:** Skip it if one partner is afraid of the other, and watch that it does not turn into point-scoring.

### 9. ROMANCE GUIDE (also IC "Borrowed Adulthood in Relationship"): A card for what helps when I'm like this, written while calm

- **Idea:** Each person writes, in their own voice, what they want from the partner when each hurting part shows up: a long hug for the scared part, a short vent with a limit for the angry part, a sharp word against the inner critic. The partner keeps the card and can try it, and the book's couple also asks for small, time-boxed things such as five minutes of listening (p.89).
- **Book:** pp. 89, 169-170. Quote (p.170): "Just give me a hug and don’t let go for five minutes"
- **Already in the guides?** Partly. IC 665-667 give two examples of asking someone for something specific (soup, a hug at the door), and RO 421 says expecting a partner to know what you need is entitled, but neither gives a way to say it ahead of time.
- **Where:** RO after line 421 in "Are you ready for the crucible?"; one sentence near the start of IC "Borrowed Adulthood in Relationship" (705).
- **Why it helps:** It replaces "you should just know" with something the partner can actually do.
- **Mind:** Keep it two-way and small. The book itself warns that a partner may feel treated like a child (p.149), and RO 459-465 warns against permanent parent and child roles.

### 10. INNER CHILD GUIDE: Turn "I have to" into "You have to", then ask whether you were born with that belief

- **Idea:** A rigid rule like "I have to be perfect" feels like plain truth. Say it as "you" and it sounds like someone else's voice, usually a parent's; then ask whether you were born with it, which obviously you were not, so it was learned (pp.117, 156).
- **Book:** pp. 117-118, 155-156. Quote (p.118): "“I have to be perfect” is turned into “You have to be perfect.”"
- **Already in the guides?** In part. The inherited-voice idea is there (IC 418-424 "The Parent You Inherited", and IC 243), but not the flip or the born-with question. No hits in IC for: born with, whose voice.
- **Where:** IC "The Parent You Inherited" (418), after the last sentence of IC 420 ("They might still be doing it.").
- **Why it helps:** It is a one-line move that lets a reader hear a rule as an old voice, without a therapist.
- **Mind:** In the book this sits inside therapist-led chair work (talking to each part in its own chair); on paper, alone, I judge the flip itself low-risk. P.117 has a typo ("Where you born"); p.156 words it cleanly.

### 11. ROMANCE GUIDE: Small daily deposits chosen from the partner's own list

- **Idea:** Each person lists five small things the partner has done that made them feel loved, and the partner picks one and does it at least five days a week. The point is that it comes from the receiver's list, not from what the giver thinks is loving. The book adds a monthly surprise, a date night, and fixed greeting and parting rituals (p.252).
- **Book:** pp. 242-243, 252 (the list is from Hendrix 1988; the rituals are from Gottman and Schwartz Gottman 2009). Quote (p.243): "not based on what Mark believed to be loving actions"
- **Already in the guides?** Partly. RO "Affection and the simmer" (49-59) covers touch without an agenda (RO 51) and flirty texts (RO 53). It has no list-from-the-receiver method and no rituals. No hits in RO for: date night, surprise, love language, greeting (RO 807 mentions a ritual only for setting up one hard talk).
- **Where:** RO "Affection and the simmer" (49), or "If you're already in it" (823).
- **Why it helps:** Many people try hard in the wrong currency, and asking for the list fixes that without a long talk.
- **Mind:** A standard therapy exercise, so credit Hendrix for the list.

### 12. ROMANCE GUIDE: Agree early-warning signs and a call-for-help pact while things are good

- **Idea:** When things are calm, each person names their own early-warning sign, you pick a marker (a date night that keeps getting cancelled), and you agree that either of you can ask for outside help and the other has already said yes. The book's version is returning to the therapist for one session, and it suggests check-ups every three to six months (p.237).
- **Book:** pp. 236-237. Quote (p.237): "to return for a session at the request of the other"
- **Adaptation (mine):** Swap the therapist for a trusted friend, a peer counselor or a one-off session.
- **Already in the guides?** Partly. RO "Outside help can sometimes break the loop fast" (841-845) treats outside help as normal, not a last resort; RO 857 says you cannot make the other person look; RO 897 says to recruit friends honestly; RO 817 mentions scheduled check-ins. There is no pre-agreed warning sign and no standing "yes".
- **Where:** RO "Outside help can sometimes break the loop fast" (841); also as a card in "What are you actually choosing together?" (700) or "Two Pillars Don't Hold The Roof Up" (674).
- **Why it helps:** It removes the hardest step, being the first to admit trouble, by making the yes arrive in advance.
- **Mind:** Keep it two-way and withdrawable; a standing yes must never be used to pressure someone who feels unsafe (care item 1).

## Care list: things in the book that conflict with the guides or need care

1. **Safety framing.** The book treats hitting and sexual coercion as a loop both people share ("Neither of you is fully to blame for the cycle." p.23; "Domestic violence is a mode cycle, too, maintained by submissive behavior!" p.197; the Ned and Suzzie case, p.200, says she "contributed to the problem") and disagrees with Gottman and Jacobsen's caution about couple therapy where there is violence (p.197); that clashes with RO 393 and IC 739, so every loop, card or break exercise above needs a "not if one of you is afraid of the other" screen.
2. **Leaving and affairs.** The book says "When the other modes push leaving, that reflects immaturity and unhealthy avoidance." (p.213), keeps an affair disclosure to a "good enough" single session (p.187), and asks a hurt partner not to be "prosecutor and judge" (p.188); this cuts against RO 887 ("If leaving feels right, then just go") and could push a betrayed reader to stay or forgive too soon.
3. **Therapist-led, memory-risky techniques and parent and child roles.** Imagery rescripting (re-imagining a childhood scene with a caring adult stepping in), chair-work, infant-mode work and SUDS drills (pp. 86-89, 140-150) need a trained guide, and p.148 has the watching partner "soothe the working partner as if the working partner were their child"; this clashes with IC 388-392 (guided imagery can plant memories) and RO 459-465 (no permanent parent and child roles), and the book itself warns the partner may feel "one down" (p.149).
4. **Needs language.** The book calls needs universal and non-negotiable, lists being able to count on a partner's faithfulness as one (p.219), and says disputing a partner's need "is abuse or bullying" (p.216); that can turn disagreement into accusation and clashes with RO 700-732 (exclusivity is a choice to negotiate), and in the Nick case "The therapist said I have a right to get my needs met!" is used to excuse betrayal (p.118).
5. **Appendix claims and labels.** The book describes scripture-based prayer (Lectio Divina) as an ongoing reparenting source for some couples (p.234) and gives the Healthy Adult a "moral compass" job (pp.249, 251), which qualifies IC 827 ("Schema therapy leaves it out") and IC 839 ("Most reparenting doesn't do enough with the Guide"); also, labels like "Bully and Attack" and "Self-aggrandizer" name people, not moments, and the claim that only 23 percent of people are relatively free of personality-disorder traits (p.1) over-pathologizes, so rename in Joel's terms.
