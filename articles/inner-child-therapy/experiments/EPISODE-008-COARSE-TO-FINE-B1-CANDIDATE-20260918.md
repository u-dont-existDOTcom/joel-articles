```markdown
### Also Look Outward

One thing I like in [Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) is that it keeps dragging the question back to what the person actually does. You tell somebody they hurt you, and somehow by the end you're comforting them while the thing you raised has disappeared. That can happen in one horrible conversation. People need time, they disagree, and sometimes the thing you're furious about is simply a boundary of theirs. If the same basic disappearance keeps showing up under different arguments, that starts to matter more than whether you can explain perfectly why it's happening.

You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.

And sometimes you really did hurt them, which makes the whole thing harder to sort. There is something real to repair. But while you're doing that, you may notice yourself slowly giving away the rest of your position because their disappointment is hard for you to tolerate. The apology gets bigger and bigger. Maybe now you're apologizing for things you still believe, or agreeing to things you don't actually agree with. Feeling guilty doesn't tell you whether that extra surrender is part of the repair, and it doesn't tell you whether the other person is manipulating you either.

Sometimes you do all the explaining and still don't get the scene where both people understand each other. They may understand you perfectly well and still think you're wrong. Your no can remain a no while they think it's unfair. At some point you notice you're defending the same thing again. The disagreement can still be there when the conversation ends.
```
