# Before You Try to Go Deep

If you're basically the scared kid right now, don't dig deeper. Get somebody steady around you first and come back to this when there's more grown-up you around.

## You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue

If you don't hear a little kid talking in your head, don't invent one. See whether you can make one silent sentence on purpose even if sentences almost never pop up by themselves. If you can, fine—words are available sometimes.

When I say “ask the child,” say it out loud or write it down if that works. If it goes nowhere, maybe a drawing, picture, or song gets closer. Sometimes the contact is just a body feeling or urge; sometimes the caring thing you do next is all there is. Some minds are simply quieter in words and pictures, and that isn't proof you're resisting anything.

Feelings and words can come in either order, and sometimes you won't know which came first. Words that come later can still help you check whether your first explanation fits and decide what to do; the feeling getting there first doesn't automatically make it truer.

## Maybe Calm the Body First

If your solar plexus, abdomen, or somewhere else feels clenched and it's hard to relax into that heart-chakra love, try a slower, non-forced exhale or some gentle massage. I love using one foot to massage the other like a Yin/Yang symbol. Circular or spiral massage over the tense area can be nice too.

Sometimes you need more bottom-up help than that: Shaking, tapping (Emotional Freedom Technique), EMDR, Brainspotting, Somatic Experiencing, Water Therapy, Massage, Energy Healing, or another approach that works from the body upward.

If talking still doesn't work because your body is too charged, stay with the body-first work in the roadmap below until dialogue becomes useful again.

### Catch the Hook Before the Story Takes Over

You may feel the hook before you know whether it's the child, a protector, or something old. Maybe your jaw clenches, your belly pulls tight, heat comes up, or you suddenly want out. Sometimes the clue arrives late: half the text is already written or your hand is already going for the drink. If the momentum keeps going, you can find yourself attacking, disappearing, making a promise, or deciding the whole relationship before you even know how you got there. [Pema Chödrön](https://pemachodronfoundation.org/product/taking-the-leap-book/) calls this tightening and urge before the story has finished forming *shenpa*.

Once you catch it, look around and say, “I'm hooked.” If nobody needs protection right now, take one to three breaths before acting. Stay with the charge for those breaths instead of using them to decide the relationship or whether you're right. See if you can do even one adult thing—give the kid some warmth, protect somebody, or find enough direction for one next move. Do the smallest version. If none of that is available, don't fake it; wait, and if the charge drops, get on with your day.

For ten minutes, don't send the text, take the drink, blurt out the thing, make the promise, or decide the relationship. If something is actually unsafe, deal with that now.

When you're less activated, go back to the actual event. The hook itself doesn't tell you what the other person did. What did they actually do, and what old material got pulled in? Somebody can cross a line now and wake up an old child-state or protector at the same time. There may be a boundary to set now; the older part might be carrying grief, something unfinished that needs repair, or care you never got. Start with what happened before deciding what it means. “My stomach tightened” is something you noticed. “That person is evil” is a judgment. Does what they did really support that conclusion, or could something else fit? If literal words were in your head at the time, keep them with the event. Words you find later can still help you understand it, but those are the later translation.

One breath is enough if that's all you can do. If turning inward makes you less oriented, less able to function, or more compelled, stop and get support from outside yourself.

### Don't Assume It's All You

If every conflict gets turned into “this is my wound,” the other person disappears from the picture. Sometimes they're actually doing something that matters. When you disagree, do they ever look at what they did and make room for your side, or does the conversation keep ending with you managing their feelings and dropping your own position?

[Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) on emotional immaturity is useful here because it pushes you to stop guessing motives for a minute and watch what the person does over and over. Sometimes you've already explained enough.

One terrible conversation isn't a pattern; somebody may need time or be holding a boundary of their own. Look at what happens next time, then the time after that. You may never know whether they can't do something or just won't, but if it wasn't there today, you couldn't rely on it today. Update your judgment when the behavior changes, not just because they promise it will, and don't wait for another injury before protecting yourself.

Guilt isn't enough to tell you what happened. You may really have hurt somebody and owe a repair while also giving up your own position because you can't stand their disappointment; the guilt doesn't prove they're manipulating you either.

Before you go back, ask what this conversation actually has to accomplish. Maybe it only needs to get one thing across. If that's not possible, saying no and ending the exchange may be enough; you don't have to spend forty minutes defending yourself. While you're doing that, the Witness keeps track of what's happening inside you and the Protector keeps track of what the other person is actually doing. The Guide only has to choose the next move.

### Write It. Don't Send It Yet.

Write the response exactly as it comes—or just say it in your head. Don't send it yet.

Read it again as the adult who's on the writer's side. Fix what is unfair or confusing without erasing what is true—including what you did wrong, what hurt, what you're asking for, or what needs to stop. Better doesn't necessarily mean softer. Then decide whether any of it is worth sending. It may end up much shorter; it may wait; maybe nothing needs to go out. If you say you'll answer tomorrow, answer tomorrow. If somebody needs protection now, don't postpone that for the exercise.

Then read the first draft again and stop fixing it. What was that person trying so hard to make somebody understand? What did that part need from the adult in you right then? Maybe they needed you to protect them; maybe they just needed reassurance or some actual help. The first version isn't automatically childish. The angry draft may be the first place you actually said what was wrong or what needed to stop.

### When Healing Turns Into Checking

You can get hooked on healing too. The wound can be real and you can still be checking it for the hundredth time, trying to find out whether you've finally grieved it correctly.

Take care of whatever safety issue or practical thing is actually there. Before another round, ask whether the last one moved anything. Maybe a feeling finally became real, or you saw something that actually changed a decision. If you're asking again only because the same answer still doesn't feel certain enough, that's checking. Grief can come back to familiar ground, but repetition by itself doesn't mean the child has a new request.

If it's checking, leave the thought unfinished and go back to the person, activity, or life you chose. The thought can stay there while you do that; it doesn't have to be settled first. Caring for the child doesn't require a meeting every time the mind says something.

Sometimes you keep leaving these sessions calmer and full of insight while the original problem doesn't move, or you start monitoring yourself more and everyday life gets worse. If that keeps happening, stop repeating the same exercise. Keep the part that genuinely helps and meet the remaining need another way. And if the exercise itself has become something you have to finish before you can get on with the day, stop there too.
