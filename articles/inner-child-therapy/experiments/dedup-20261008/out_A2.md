# Repeats in the inner child article: whole-article check

What I checked: every block from B2 to B173 (B0 is the title, B1 a reviewer note). Each block in "Before You Try to Go Deep" (B32–B77) was compared with every other block there and with the rest of the article; then the rest of the article was compared with itself.

Found: 40 repeats, 8 contradictions or pulls in opposite directions, and 10 callbacks that rely on something sections back (listed separately after the repeats, not counted as repeats).

Each entry gives: the blocks; an exact quote from each instance (each under 25 words); the shared point; what the later instance adds, or whether a reader would feel told the same thing again; and which instance carries the point better or sits in the more natural place. Strength: "strong" = a reader will very likely feel told again; "moderate" = many readers will; "minor" = a careful reader might.

## 1. Repeats, in article order

### 1. B10, B21, B76: the work isn't meant to last a lifetime
- B10: "Our lives can become small and like a never-ending therapy session when we focus only on caring for ourselves"
- B21: "Spending your whole life processing your wounded inner child isn't freedom either."
- B76: "Real healing is ultimately measured by reduced need for the therapy, just like real growing up is not something you do forever."
- Shared point: inner-child work shouldn't become your whole life.
- Later instances: B21 adds the bigger horizon spirituality gave him; B76 adds a yardstick (needing therapy less, daily life improving). By B76 the core point has been made twice already. Moderate.
- Better: B76, because it turns the point into a test the reader can apply.

### 2. B11–B12, B25: the chicken-and-egg problem, told twice
- B11: "What if you’re so far inside the child-state that you can’t become the adult?"

- Shared point: you can sink so far into the child that no adult is left, so you need an adult there before you can be one.
- Later instance: B25 adds the author's own case (he had got past a protective part first), then arrives at the same answer B12 gave ("have an adult there before I could reliably be that adult myself"). Moderate.
- Better: B11–B12, because it names the problem and gives the borrowed-adulthood answer in one place.

## 2. Contradictions and pulls in opposite directions

### C1. B33 vs B80 (and B14): start now, or wait?
- B33: "If you can't do that because you're basically the frightened kid, leave the deeper conversation until later."
- B80: "That might be the most adult thing you've got right now, and it's plenty to start with."
- B14: "use your childlike superpowers to come into the "Wow of Now""
- The pull: B33 says to put off the deeper conversation while you're mostly the scared child. B80 says noticing the scared one is enough to start, and B14's stage 1 treats being fully the child as a starting point (with enough safety around you). Nothing says how B33's test (look around, pause, choose and follow through) relates to B80's lower bar. Moderate.
- Better argued: B80, which gives a reason ("Whoever's noticing isn't all the way inside the scared one."); B33 gives only the test.

### C2. B37 vs B137 (and B154): can you wait until you're calm?
- B37: "The nice thing about inner child therapy is that you can wait until you're calm and actually ready."
- B37: "A real parent doesn't get to do that"
- B137: "Whenever your little one shows up looking for their parent"
- B137: "you go back to being the one they can rely on"
- B154: "If it sounds like your little one, though, hear it out the first time it shows up."
- The pull: B37 says that, unlike a real parent, you can wait until you're calm. B137 puts you in "my mom's place", answering whenever they show up, and B154 says to hear them the first time. Nothing says whether B137 and B154 mean a brief response in the moment and B37 the deeper session. Moderate.
- Better argued: B137, which gives the bedtime example of staying kind while still leading.

### C3. B43 vs B81: the "ask your little one what they need" prompt
- B43: "the usual inner-child prompt to “ask your little one what they need” can get confusing"
- B81: "Little Joel is terrified. What does Little Joel need right now?"
- The pull: B43 warns that this prompt confuses people who don't hear an inner voice; B81 recommends that very question with no caveat. Later scripts (B116, B120) also give the little one spoken lines. Mild.
- Better argued: B43, which explains the problem; B81 doesn't address it.

### C4. B55 vs B154: let passing thoughts go, or not?
- B55: "throwing everything transient away, as some people do with "thought-noting" practice, also isn't healthy, except as a meditation exercise."
- B154: "Let a stray thought go as just thinking, and come back to the room you're in."
- The pull: B154 recommends the move B55 warns against. B154 does separate a recurring fear (talk to it) from a stray thought (let it go), but neither passage says how to tell a stray thought from the meaningful signals B55 describes. Mild.
- Better argued: B55, which gives examples of signals worth keeping.

### C5. B58 vs B63: explained enough, or try again?
- B58: "it can seem like you haven't explained yourself well enough yet. You probably have"
- B63: "Maybe your first explanation sucked. Try again."
- The pull: B58 says you've probably explained enough; B63 says your explanation may have been bad, so try again. The situations differ (B58: a pattern where the talk never comes back to you; B63: repairing after you hurt them), which partly explains it, but the article doesn't connect the two. Mild.
- Better argued: B58, which says what to go by instead (what they keep showing you).

### C6. B86 vs B111: warmth first, or acts first?

### C8. B159 vs B172: stop now, or hear it out first?
