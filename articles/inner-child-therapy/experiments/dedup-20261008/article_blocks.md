[B6] # The Chicken-and-Egg Problem

[B7] Céline's song is called *On ne change pas*—"we don't change." I think that's both fortunately and unfortunately true for most of us. Fortunately in the sense that the sweet, innocent, curious, vivacious little one is still inside us, even if hiding. Unfortunate in the sense that we often don't truly develop the adult qualities that we might pretend to have, so we continue burdening that little one inside us with troubles they aren't made for handling. This shows up in how we may feel abandoned, helpless, impulsive, frightened, or desperate to be chosen.

[B8] And I realized that it doesn't make sense to ask the child to heal itself, which is really a disguised request for the child to abandon its own nature and turn into an adult. There was never anything wrong with the child's worth to begin with. Instead, we need to separately develop the qualities of a good inner parent for that rightly vulnerable child: the loving Nurturer, safe Protector, and wise Guide.

[B9] That's one grown-up doing three jobs, not three more people moving in, and each job keeps the others in balance. Say a party gets to be too much for your little one. The Protector gets you both out of there, and on the way home the Nurturer tells them they did great, even though mostly they hid in the bathroom. And the Guide helps them think twice about the next weird party they might go to, avoiding another cycle of bathroom vacationing. 🙈

[B10] The Guide is the one people overlook the most. I left it out of my first version of this guide until Key pointed it out, and without an inner Guide, it's easy to go looking for one in a cult. Our lives can become small and like a never-ending therapy session when we focus only on caring for ourselves rather than giving ourselves a mission as well. But then some people over-rely on their mission to avoid caring for themselves.

[B11] But here’s the chicken-and-egg problem. What if you’re so far inside the child-state that you can’t become the adult? This is much easier when you can already reach one of those adult roles. But when you can't, it's like you sit there trying to be loving while your mind is becoming controlled again by the inherited critic voice, or you start going numb, throwing a fit, or panicking.

[B12] Sometimes somebody else may have to do one part of the adult role with you or for you first. You get to feel what that is like and watch how they do it. Maybe you copy one small thing you've seen them do, or imagine them there when they're not, and try one piece yourself while they're still helping with the rest. That's what I mean by borrowed adulthood. You may still need help with almost all of it for a while, but over time there are more and more of those little parts you can do yourself.

[B13] For now, I think of it in five fairly predictable stages. You might be starting at any point here (and it may change even from hour to hour as well):

[B14] 1. You're still fully identified as the child — use your childlike superpowers to come into the "Wow of Now" (have you seen the movie Waking Life?), and make sure there's enough safety around you to continue:
2. Begin to borrow adulthood — remember watching Mr. Rogers or Sesame Street? Those adult role models are what we're looking for at this stage. This can almost feel like playing house as a kid where you imagined how you'd take care of a baby doll etc.
3. Practice adulting — do at least one part of caring for yourself while support is still there from the previous step.
4. Adult mode itself — after sufficient practice with reparenting, you become a natural at sending love to little you, protecting them, and directing them.
5. Adult and child in relationship — the adult leads. The child isn't pushed away, but doesn't have to run the whole life alone. This can actually feel really weird if you've been stuck in adult mode for a while. Our goal is to go from weird to silly and playful. Lose self-consciousness while gaining self-awareness, if that makes sense!

[B15] Whichever stage you're at, saying the right thing when you sit down with your little one helps, but you also have to start actually doing some of it in the rest of your life. Your little one starts believing the grown-up you when they catch you lying down because you're wiped out, instead of pushing through one more chore.

[B16] # My Journey

[B17] I used to preach [Buddhist enlightenment](http://mindset.u-dont-exist.com/) a lot. But an old childhood wound could still hit and suddenly I was the same little kid again, even when the spiritual part of me felt completely free.

[B18] The way I picture it is almost stupidly simple. Imagine you're broke and starving, and your baby is starving too. *Understandable*: there is no food. Then a food bank opens nearby and suddenly you've got lots of nutritious munchies. But you're eating all of it yourself while baby you is still starving. *Unforgivable.*

[B19] Nobody leaves a baby starving on purpose. I just didn't believe there was a baby. Inner-child stuff sounded silly to me until I could actually feel him. Then there was this little me actually hurting, and I could send some of the love I'd already found his way.

[B20] I think most people who chase spiritual freedom are also trying to get away from something painful in themselves, and a lot of it goes back to childhood. That's [spiritual bypassing](https://goenka.u-dont-exist.com/). Plenty of seekers and gurus do it too; I've written about the [Dalai Lama as one example](https://substack.com/@udontexist/note/c-245314772?r=5vdc6m). I did it too. It doesn't mean you're stupid or crazy.

[B21] Spending your whole life processing your wounded inner child isn't freedom either. Spirituality opened a much bigger horizon for me—love and happiness beyond the conditions of my life story. Maybe ordinary life already gives you enough love and meaning. Great. It didn't for me. Conditional love felt radically insufficient, so I kept looking for something deeper.

[B22] I was well-loved as a child, but from age twelve to twenty-six I was depressed and cried every night from feeling the pain of the world. [Loving-kindness (metta) meditation](http://love.u-dont-exist.com/) healed that depression almost instantly for me.

[B23] I wasn't lacking love. My parents had given me a lot of it. I found more in [Buddhist teachings—which, it turns out, also include inner-child self-love!](https://substack.com/profile/355021294-u-dont-existcom/note/c-297324975) and later in [loveyhuasca](http://soma.u-dont-exist.com/). Metta gave all that love somewhere to land.

[B24] But it didn't reach everything. A lot of the child stuff around my attachment to women was still there. When I later tried [self-directed inner-child therapy](https://youtu.be/yEWFjODXJbI?si=IXUqrhExojOm6Itm), it felt like I was making it up at first. I used San Pedro cactus and CBD tea with sublingual mapacho, and that cracked it open; structured sessions with an inner-child therapist deepened it.

[B25] Sometimes I'd get past a protective part and reach the child, then get pulled so far into being him that there wasn't a grown-up me around anymore. The three adult jobs from the map don't work too well when the adult has vanished, so I started looking for a way to have an adult there before I could reliably be that adult myself.

[B27] ### Don’t Give the Inner Adult Away

[B28] If you still feel like a kid inside, somebody who seems grown-up can feel like salvation, and letting them do some of the grown-up jobs for you can actually help for a while. But they can know things you don't and still be wrong about you.

[B29] Even [Pema Chödrön](https://pemachodronfoundation.org/product/taking-the-leap-book/), who inspired parts of this guide, comes from a tradition where devotion to a teacher is a big deal, and she still says a teacher should “wean us from dependency altogether” and help us grow up.

[B30] Say you grew up learning that what you wanted didn't matter. Now you're only just finding out what you actually think, maybe even learning to say no. Then somebody tells you there is no self. That might be freeing at another point in your life; right now it can sound a lot like the message you already know: what you want doesn't matter. Or you tell a therapist, “No, that's not right for me,” and they call it resistance. Maybe they're right. But maybe they're wrong about you, or taking you somewhere you don't want to go. You still have to be able to say so.

[B31] Borrow adulthood. Do not surrender it.

[B32] # Before You Try to Go Deep

[B33] How much of the grown-up you is actually here right now? Try looking around the room, pausing before you act, making a choice and following through on it. If you can't do that because you're basically the frightened kid, leave the deeper conversation until later. You may need somebody steady there with you, or to do whatever helps you come back a bit before you try to talk to the child.

[B34] ## When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child

[B35] I've been writing this whole guide as if everybody who tries it wants to take care of the inner child. Some people get a little rush out of making somebody scared or small, though. For such folks, inner child therapy is clearly not the place to start. They need to first deconstruct the adult identity they've got.

[B36] When I talk with folks like that, I mostly want to know what the identity gets them and what it costs, and especially what it's really serving. Some of them even call themselves sovereign, and then somehow there's an urge they have to obey, which sounds more like slavery to me.

[B37] This part tends to scare the wrong people, like somebody who's horrified by their own thoughts. Everybody gets scary thoughts they'd rather not have sometimes, and they don't make you a bad person, but why would you bring your inner child into the middle of them? The nice thing about inner child therapy is that you can wait until you're calm and actually ready. A real parent doesn't get to do that, which is one reason nobody manages to be a perfect one.

[B38] And if somebody actually wonders whether they're malicious, they'd do better to meditate on it and find out why they're asking before they bring their inner child anywhere near it. Are they really feeling malicious, or is it some voice in their head saying so, like the inherited critic? If some of it turns out to be malice they actually want to act on, the inner child can wait.

[B39] In one of the Buddha's own accounts of how he woke up, ill will shows up in his mind and he sees it's hurting him. If he had to figure that out by watching, it's not obvious. Ill will can make you feel powerful. Being on fire would too, and people would definitely get out of your way, but unless you're the Human Torch from the Fantastic Four, you'd be the one burning. So it's worth noticing what it's doing to you. Has it ever left anybody at ease, with nothing to regret afterward? Of course not.

[B40] The Buddha also told his son that loving-kindness is what gets rid of ill will, and compassion does the same for cruelty. I'm biased on loving-kindness, since it got me out of my depression. For these folks it starts with the grown-up they are now, though, and their little one stays out of it.

[B41] As for when their little one gets a turn, I'd say once they've had some bad days where scaring somebody would've felt good, and they didn't do it. Until then it helps a lot to have a real person in their life who likes them and won't laugh along, since loving-kindness is easier to pick up from someone who already has some.

[B42] ## You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue

[B43] If you don't hear a little kid talking in your head, don't invent one. Actually, this is one place where the usual inner-child prompt to “ask your little one what they need” can get confusing, because “ask” sounds like you're supposed to ask a question and then wait for a second voice to answer. Maybe that's what happens for some people, but you might not be some people (hehe).

[B44] You might barely have spontaneous words in your mind and yet still be able to think a sentence on purpose. Try it and see. If words are there, speak or write them. You might feel more comfortable that way than just staying in imagination land.  And if that starts feeling like homework you're doing for a child who isn't answering, drop it. Maybe what's actually requiring your attention now is a knot in your stomach. Or perhaps you're in a place you want to leave. Sometimes nothing “answers” and you just know what would be nice to do for yourself.

[B45] And it might not be the same order all the time. You could have a feeling before thoughts, or thoughts might come first and provoke a feeling. They say hindsight is 20/20, but you actually might not even know which happened first. That's ok, too.

[B50] ### Catch the Hook Before the Story Takes Over

[B51] Pema Chödrön calls the tightening and urge you get before the story has finished forming "shenpa," and she says "[hooked](https://www.lionsroar.com/how-we-get-hooked-shenpa-and-how-we-get-unhooked/)" is a more descriptive translation than the usual "attachment." You might notice the hook before you have any clue what part of you is doing the hooking. Or you might notice it embarrassingly late. The jaw and stomach are already tight, and/or half the text is written, and then suddenly it dawns on you: you're supposed to be observing yourself. 😀 Maybe forget the parts detective work for a minute. You can figure that out later.

[B52] When you catch it, look around and just take a moment to enjoy some free honesty: "Oh! I'm hooked again! That's awesome I could notice it!" If nobody is in danger, take a breath or three before you finish whatever you were doing. Sometimes human beings need to be being, not always doing. 🙃 Maybe then you'll find there's enough adult around to be nice to your little boo for a second. Or enough Protector to stop something you'd regret later.

[B53] Here's where I want to clearly depart from the new-age wizdumb that all your problems are self-created: Somebody may really have crossed a boundary. That's not just because they are your karmic partner mirroring what God wants to show you in yourself, even if that also applies sometimes. Your reaction is what you can control the most, though. If what they did hit something old in you, notice how that feels in your body. And notice how your feelings can also amplify or distort your perceptions of others. Just because your stomach tightened up doesn't mean their boundary crossing indicates “They're evil.” You can defer that judgement for when you're in your happy place.

[B54] Feelings about yourself work the same way. "I feel attracted" or "I feel ashamed" is real as a feeling, but it doesn't settle who you are, or who you love. Making too much out of something momentary is one side of the human psyche's pendulum. Give yourself time to see what patterns form more durably, and what feels authentically yours vs something planted or manipulated into being there from society, influencers, family, friends, religion, etc., etc. You may find an exceptional freedom in exactly that in-between place where you don't have to decide what your pronouns are, or whether you believe in this or that. If you stay in flux forever, though, it can seem like there's nothing solid about you. In that case, sometimes it helps to meditate or talk with a good unbiased therapist who can help you see the pattern.

[B55] The opposite extreme of our pendulum, throwing everything transient away, as some people do with "thought-noting" practice, also isn't healthy, except as a meditation exercise. Transient feelings and thoughts can be meaningful signals. For example, if you don't want to be touched, say no now and work out what it means later. Same if something might be dangerous right now, or might be a medical warning sign: step away or get checked first.

[B56] ### Also Look Outward

[B57] Once you're in your happy place, look at the other person too, and don't go only by the one fight where they crossed your boundary, or, the other way around, by the one "no" or boundary of theirs that made you mad — after all, they're allowed to say no to you. Does it keep ending the same way, where you bring up something that hurt and then still end up reassuring them they're not a bad person, and the talk never comes back to what happened to you? (The psychologist [Lindsay Gibson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) wrote the book on this: [*Disentangling from Emotionally Immature People*](https://www.newharbinger.com/9781648481512/disentangling-from-emotionally-immature-people/).) Or can they hear you disagree and look at their own part?

[B58] If it's the first, it can seem like you haven't explained yourself well enough yet. You probably have, and it makes more sense to count on what they keep showing you they can do than on the right words finally getting through to them. Maybe what they can do is text you something kind the next day and not bring any of it up.

[B59] You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.

[B61] If [a relationship](https://romance.u-dont-exist.com) keeps making you feel less safe, meditate on why it makes you feel unsafe, and what keeps you in it. Might it be due to a lack of the healed inner child/inner adult relationship? (If so, you're reading the right guide!) But if they've actually hurt or threatened you, even once, old stuff or not, get safe first, which is not a small thing at all, I know.

[B62] If you really did hurt them, repair it. Also easier said than done, but...

[B63] You can mean the apology for a start. Then notice what happens next. Are you sorry for what you did, or are you starting to take back the complaint too because they're still upset? Maybe your first explanation sucked. Try again. 😅 And if it fails again, and you've said what you mean, well, you can't guarantee they'll understand it. Guilt may keep shouting at you. That doesn't tell you the whole conflict was your fault, nor does it prove they're manipulating you.

[B64] Then there are people you're just getting to know. Meeting them can turn into a kind of test--"First impressions are everything," as they say, but it could be even worse than that. If the real reason you're asking someone out is to prove you're lovable, or that you're sure what you want, then they've become part of the test. I'm giving you permission now to quit school and flunk the tests. Get curious about who this person is, and who you are, and let both of you have your own reasons for being there in the beautiful present.

[B65] ### Write It. Don't Send It Yet.

[B66] If you've got something burning you up that you wanna say, write it out as a reply. There. That got it out. Ahh. Breathing easier. 😌 But don't send it. Take that breather you earned and then come back to it from a cooler place.

[B67] It doesn't mean you need to hide your upset, or apply for a permit to protest, but choose your battles and execute them wisely, so you don't end up regretting how you communicated later. As Lao Tsu might have said (if I could ever have read that whole boring Art of War book), a war with no casualties is the best possible war. When high IQ & EQ communication hits a writer's block, sometimes the best thing is to simply say, "I need some time to think about my response." And if the conflict really requires a response right now, then obviously just do your best rather than wait for the perfect timing.

[B68] And yeah, going back to the writer, which is often my inner child when emotions pop up, is part of the process. It's like I'm holding his hand and showing him a better way, not like I just stole his pencil because he wrote something embarrassing. So what was he actually trying to say? A lot of the time it's just "that hurt."

[B69] Other times it's "I did this too." My friend Tyler saw a guy smoking in front of a baby and asked him if he thought that was wise. The guy asked if he thought it was wise to mind his own business, and Tyler said, "Well, I believe in Christ, so I thought you might care about life too." Afterward he wasn't sure he'd handled it right, or whether he should have thrown the first stone at all, since he'd smoked in front of a baby himself.

[B70] But Jesus also said to take the log out of your own eye first, and then "[you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's eye](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%207%3A3-5&version=ESV)." That's exactly what made Tyler the right person to say something. If you actually have the problem they have, and you want to call them out for it, call yourself out for it instead. What we came up with was something like, "Hey man, when I was younger I smoked in front of an infant, not realizing how selfish that was, and I notice you doing the same thing. I'd ask you to please have some compassion for him. I have a young child now, so this really touches me."

[B71] It's also how your voice sounds, though. You can say all the right words, but if the tone is accusatory, it doesn't matter. Tyler's an evangelical Christian who preaches on YouTube, so it meant a lot to me that he liked this take on what Jesus was saying.

[B72] ### When Healing Turns Into Checking

[B73] You can get hooked on healing too. The wound can be completely real while you're checking it for the hundredth time to see whether you've finally grieved it correctly.

[B74] Going back can still matter, since realizations are a real part of life. But returning to the same wound can also become a search for certainty in an inherently uncertain existence. Spending all day asking, "Did I process it properly? Did I grieve it enough?" is clearly not what we're after here.

[B75] If you're feeling guilty because you didn't put your seat belt on, just put it on. If you still want to investigate it later, fine. Perhaps now and then you feel bad about that time you refused to put it on, broke your collar bone in a crash, and then got blamed for not wearing it. That's natural, but doesn't mean there's something new to look at. Then the background checker goes, “But are you really listening?” There it is. Seat belt's on. 😇 Where were we?

[B76] Don't be afraid to ask your therapist/yourself "What is this practice supposed to help, and how would I know if it's helping?" This sovereign, gently curious stance should apply to everything in life. Feeling calmer or having deeper insights or explanations for the trauma response are some possible signs of initial progress, but if that's the endgame, it's just a cope. Real healing is ultimately measured by reduced need for the therapy, just like real growing up is not something you do forever. And if the therapy is actually working, it should improve your daily life, and especially the health of the inner child-inner adult relationship. Going at it harder isn't as useful as going at it with more wisdom and compassion. Take the parts that work, and innovate on the rest.

[B77] If these exercises start feeling like a ritual you have to complete before you're allowed to live, stop there.

[B78] # Borrow the Adult Before You Can Be the Adult

[B79] ## Noticing Counts

[B80] "Huh, somebody in here is really scared." That might be the most adult thing you've got right now, and it's plenty to start with. Whoever's noticing isn't all the way inside the scared one.

[B81] It can help to use your own name. For me, instead of "I'm terrified," that'd be "Little Joel is terrified. What does Little Joel need right now?" You'll sound a bit like Elmo :) In [lab studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5495792/), though, people who talked to themselves this way reacted less to upsetting pictures and memories, and their brains didn't seem to be working any harder to do it.

[B82] If you can tell your parts apart pretty easily, you've probably got plenty of noticing already. Don't keep drilling it just because the love isn't coming yet. A lot of meditators are great at watching. I was, and baby me was still starving.

[B83] ## Borrow One Competency at a Time

[B84] A complete ideal parent may be impossible to imagine (unless you saw The Brady Bunch). And imagination is pretty hard for adults anyway since it kinda gets stamped out of kids at an early age, but it's not just a foolish waste of time. It's actually a genius mode unlock. So since the adult is actually a baby when it comes to power to imagine, start small: Borrow one function at a time from a figure who embodies it. Perhaps a therapist or your grandmother really cared for you. Perhaps your teacher at some age was a great guide. Maybe an older sibling protected you well. Even Mr. Rogers could help here if you felt some nice vibes from his show as a kid. And there's always your future healed self if you can imagine that.

[B85] If the love still isn't coming, you can at least stop judging your little one. Obviously no insults. Helpful advice is great, we all need advice. Let them absorb it at their pace. And don't expect a thank-you for the session, like they booked it. That's enough to start being the adult, even if it isn't enough yet for them to trust you.

[B86] Most of us wouldn't open up to somebody who'd only just stopped being mean, or let them take care of us. That takes warmth, and if there isn't any yet, borrow some. Think of someone you remember fondly. How would they sit beside your little one? Just make sure it's someone who liked you the way you were. If nobody comes to mind, you can look to examples like Mr. Rogers, or spiritual love you've felt, if you have.

[B87] For protecting, ask what a minimally competent adult would do here. Maybe something simple, like actually smelling that old food you're about to eat and then trashing it if it smells a little weird. If you're about to send an angry text, hit pause. If the room itself triggers your spidey sense, leave, or lock the door and get help. Even if this still feels like the child acting like the adult, for now, that's ok. "Fake it til you make it" isn't exactly it, but yeah. Afterward, you can tell your little one, "I did that for us. That was the Protector." If it sounds funny, laugh!

[B88] The Guide can be you too, just a calmer you. That's a lot easier to imagine than your future healed self. Think back to what (and how) you decided about something like this the last time you were calm. Or ask what the wise person you respect would have you practice today. A wise tradition (like "What would Jesus do?" or "What would Buddha do?") can give some direction, too, until you develop your own internal compass.

[B89] The thank-you you weren't waiting for might not come anyway. 😜 More like "Oh, so NOW you care?" Which, fair enough, you did take a while. Your first instinct might be "But I'm trying!", and then it's just two six-year-olds arguing. Some days, just hearing it out like a decent adult is the only thing you'll manage to borrow, and your little one might still roll their eyes at you. 🙄

[B90] ## Borrow Love—or Borrow the Perspective of Care

[B91] Sometimes love isn't the missing part. Your dog can chew up your good shoes and be forgiven before you've found the other one. Try giving yourself a fraction of that and it goes flat, or starts to feel weirdly unsafe. So let the dog go first. Or your kid, or whoever you love without trying; they don't have to be in the room. Just feel that for a minute, in your body, before you try to turn it into self-love. Then see if some of it can go to your little one, who hasn't even chewed anything.

[B92] Watch how it lands, though. "I care about you" can be patient, or it can be "I care about you, now hurry up and feel better." Getting close can be too much, too, the way kids squirm away from an aunt they barely know who goes in for the big hug, and then climb all over the uncle who's just sitting there. And if nothing moves at all, don't push it. Knowing that love is in you somewhere counts for a lot, and you're borrowing something you already know how to do.

[B93] Or put somebody you care about into your exact life, your family, your bank account and all, and then step outside it and ask what you'd tell them. It's a [cheap trick for becoming the outsider to your own mess](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21728454/). The quest for self-knowledge is a life's journey, but you don't have to wait for your life to finish to figure out your best next move. And the gap it gets around even has a name, [Solomon's paradox](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24916084/), after the king everybody brought their disputes to, who then wrecked his own life.

[B94] Then take a small piece of whatever you'd tell them and hand it to yourself. If the advice was "get more sleep," then for now just try brushing your teeth before midnight instead of at two. That's how you learn as a kid, and it works now, too. The technical term for this is "[scaffolding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instructional_scaffolding)."

[B95] If the only time the Nurturer shows up is when something's wrong, your little one can start to feel like they're your job. Sometimes show up just to say "I'm glad you're here," and then enjoy them for a minute, the way you'd enjoy a little kid who wants to show you a rock they found.

[B96] ### A Smaller Doorway: Goodwill

[B97] Sometimes "I love you" is more than you can honestly say, especially to somebody who's treated you badly. Even the usual whittled-down loving-kindness prayer, "May you be happy," can feel like rewarding them. That's why I go with Buddha's overall wish (paraphrasing): **"May you be loving, peaceful, and free,"** since if they were all of those, they wouldn't have done what they did, and then they actually would be lovable.

[B98] This goodwill isn't just for the target of it, though. You may find it bounces back and opens up a little warmth in your heart. And if "I love you" goes flat when you aim it at yourself or your little one, the same wish works there, too. 🫶🏿

[B99] ### Ask God for a Loan

[B100] The dog kind of love is real love. Some people also know a love that's a whole different size, like God's, or whatever opened up for them in prayer or on a long retreat, and I don't want to lump that in with the dog. If you've been there, don't shrink it down to a thought experiment about a friend.

[B101] Get as close to it as you really can right now, and bring your little one in with you. They might go numb, or just say "Bullshit." That becomes the conversation, and it's worth listening to. If you pour more spirituality on top instead, it's the food bank all over again, and none of it is getting to them.

[B102] ### When the Spiritual Relationship Hurts

[B103] If a spiritual teacher you trusted betrayed you, even sitting down to meditate can feel like it belongs to them now. Kids quit piano over one bad teacher, and this is way worse. But if you let an abuser control your appreciation of life, then you've given them an unearned win.

[B104] And being the grown-up doesn't mean controlling everything. That's pretty much the Serenity Prayer: change what's yours to change, and accept what isn't, which is where other people's choices go.

[B105] ## Become the Adult Apprentice

[B106] Say a friend sits with you while you fall apart. Most of us feel better and call them again the next time, and they get a little better at it every time while we don't. So steal some of it. Usually what helped was how they just sat there without trying to fix you. Ten seconds of sitting with yourself like that is good enough for a start, so try it before you call next time.

[B107] # Building Trust With Your Little One

[B108] ## Not Every Hero Wears A Cape

[B109] Set one boundary. "What's a 'boundary'?", I might be asking. That looks like saying no to something that's costing you too much (money, time, whatever it is). Handle that task you've been putting off, or the tax man, or your DUIs in Maryland, even if all you do today is open the envelope. Keep one small promise to your little one. Clean your room! Maybe your little one had grown-ups who said sweet things and then did nothing, and maybe you've done it too, so this is how they start to believe you.

[B110] You can protect your little one from yourself too, like by repairing after an internal attack. "What's an 'internal attack'?" Calling yourself an idiot for losing your keys again, for one. Your little one was listening, so you go back and apologize to them. If they're sarcastic about it, you hear it out instead of snapping back. They don't trust you yet, and you don't ask them to. They complain about something, and if they're right, you say so. All of that shows as clearly to them as the clean room did. It's evidence you're safe to be around and that you accept them. Love needs both, but without warmth they can still feel like they're only being put up with.

[B111] Plenty of people are still at the putting-up-with part. Even with little or no warmth for your little one yet, the relationship can start with the Protector's plain acts and the warmth can come after, which feels backwards, because you'd think you have to feel it first before you could do anything for them. But yeah, bonding starts somewhere. And this is not to say you've never done anything to care for yourself. If not, you'd be dead right now. But we're making additional acts of care intentional now as a base for love to grow.

[B112] Intentional, meaning you pick one of those acts and decide when you'll do it, and what the smallest first step is (like with the envelope). Some of them are only one step anyway. If it needs money or somebody's help, sort that out before the day, and if you'll have to tell someone something they won't like, say it out loud to yourself ahead of time. Then put yourself in their shoes to see how it sounds, and go back & forth like that a couple times until both sides feel good about it, or good enough, as long as what you needed to say is still in there.

[B113] After the day, look at what actually happened. If you did the Protector action(s), not feeling better right away is information to learn from. It doesn't mean failure, because the process is dynamic. And you can ask yourself again, "What is this practice supposed to help, and how would I know if it's helping?" Not doing it is harder to figure out. Fear is the first guess, but maybe you didn't know how or why. Being worn out can stop you too, and sometimes whoever said they'd help couldn't make it. Hey, we've all been there. 🤗

[B114] ## Love Doesn't Have to Wait for Trust ❤️

[B115] Sometimes the love is there for your little one and still feels unsafe to them, or safe for a minute and then not. Love and trust don't have to come as a pair, but they got tied together, as if one had to vouch for the other. So your love can sound like you're asking them to trust you. It doesn't need their trust, but it does need you to be honest with them, which isn't the same as perfect.

[B116] The love can go first, though, before their trust is done going over your track record (they've heard what you still call yourself when you mess up, sometimes out loud). Your little one says "I don't trust you." You answer, "I believe you. And I love you."

[B117] I was about five when my mom told me it was time for bed (or something equally outrageous to a five-year-old). I cried and screamed "I HATE YOU!" 😡 She answered, "But I love you!" 🥰

[B118] That completely threw me. Fights don't usually go like that. Anger is a way to get more power when you feel a lack of justice or agency. You puff yourself up so you seem frightening or dangerous, or you demand to be heard. My anger was there to get me heard, and her answer showed me I didn't need it for that. Whatever injustice I thought there was wasn't coming from a lack of love, either. So the anger seemed stupid. I saw for myself that it was only hurting me, without helping me see clearly or get what I wanted. Still, what I screamed was true right then, and what she said back was true too, so I could hate somebody who loved me and she'd still be my mom the whole time.

[B119] When it's your little one yelling at you, though, try not to turn "But I love you" into a line you say on cue, because then it can brush the anger off, and what it's really saying is "so stop being angry." I think you can mean it and still be doing that. Does the love stay afterward and take whatever's true in the anger seriously?

[B120] The love can stay, and your little one might still come back with "Big fuckity whoopty doo. I don't believe you." 🙄 You answer, "Okay. I believe that you don't believe me. I still love you. 🤗 You don't have to love me back. Tell me what feels unsafe."

[B121] Your little one might say it's you. You don't have to accept every conclusion they reach about you, but do listen for the concrete complaint without fighting the yelling and swearing around it. If it's something serious, answer it seriously. The complaint might only be one sentence, once the swearing runs out. Like the anger at bedtime, the swearing is there to get them heard, so once you're listening instead of fighting, they don't need it anymore.

[B122] You can see the same move in a dramatized scene from the movie A Beautiful Day in the Neighborhood. Lloyd, a reporter, tells Mr. Rogers, "I can't imagine it was easy… growing up with you as a father." He doesn't collapse under that or fight it, but he does answer what's worth answering in it. He tells Lloyd that until recently his oldest son never told people about his dad, and his youngest "genuinely tested" him. Eventually he and his youngest found their way, and now he's very proud of both his sons. Then he says, "But you are right, Lloyd. It couldn't have been easy on them. Thank you. Thank you for that perspective." Like the two truths at bedtime, a true complaint and real love don't cancel each other out, so you can take the true part without giving up your love or defending yourself.

[B123] Also notice what else in you answers your little one, besides what you choose to say. When they say "I don't believe you," something in you might fire back an accusation, and then they can learn your love is only there as long as they take it the right way. The resentment can be purely visceral: being doubted after you've tried feels unfair, but there's nothing unfair in them going over your track record. The part that resents not being believed might be the same one that said "I love you" in the first place, or a different one, or a bit of both. Both talk in your grown-up voice, so to your little one they're the same. Be careful not to take the accusation as proof your love really is conditional, when it could just be another part talking.

[B124] Your little one might not be the only one getting accused. When life has gone badly, part of you may try to get some control back by finding someone to blame, like "If you'd gotten it together at fifteen, you could have saved everybody." If it's somebody's fault, it didn't have to happen, and that feels like control. But figuring out which age of you is to blame doesn't give you what it would have taken back then. That part may start picking on a different age.

[B125] Whichever age it picks on, saving everybody was never your job. At fifteen you probably didn't even have your own money, or anywhere else to go. And actually, not to beat a dead horse here, but every age of you was stuck with whatever it had then, even the adult you were last year. Hindsight being 20/20 doesn't change that. You still did what you did, though, and if it hurt people or damaged a life, that may need repair.

[B126] So instead of asking which age of you deserves the blame, I'd ask these:

[B127] 1. What was that age of you trying to get, or get away from?
2. What did it understand back then?
3. What options could it actually see? Could it stand to take any of them?
4. What happened because of the choice it made?
5. What was missing? Some ability, or help/protection from someone else?
6. What can you learn or repair now, and how would you act differently in such situations?

[B128] Whatever was missing back then, you can start giving your little one now.

[B129] Your little one has their own version of that last question: "Big fuckity whoopty doo. What are you gonna do for me?" They actually want to know, though I think the first answer may be that you keep loving them, even while they don't believe you. What you do for them takes days to add up, but the moment they doubt you, you can show them right away that the love stays and doesn't turn into an accusation. Then their trust can grow from the stuff back in "Not Every Hero Wears A Cape," like hearing out the sarcasm, or finally opening that envelope.

[B130] If your stomach tightens while your little one waits for an answer, slower exhales may help you stay with them. Feeling calmer doesn't mean their complaint is wrong, though, and it won't make them trust you on its own.

[B131] ## Make a Simple Vow

[B132] If your promises still mean something to your little one, maybe because you've kept a few small ones, a quiet vow can help too. As many real-life parents know, your heart really explodes in such an unexpected and wonderful way when you hold your newborn baby for the first time (happened to me!). And if you're not yet a parent, it can really help to imagine holding a baby in your arms to make the vow feel real. Imagine you just had a baby, how would you feel? Would you look at him/her with adoration and joy? Carry that into visualizing yourself with your own inner child:

[B133] > "I know you're in there. I love you. You don't have to love me back or trust me yet. I can't promise that nothing painful will happen. Or that I'll always know what to do, or never react badly. But I can promise to tell you the truth and listen when you're angry. I'll make our life together safer where I can, and take the next step I can see to protect you. When I fail, I'll come back to make it right. You don't have to believe these words. Watch what I do."

[B134] Notice it only promises what you can keep, so your little one can actually watch you keep it.

[B135] Only say the "I love you" part if you can feel it. If you don't feel the love yet, say you care (with the goodwill prayer: "May you be loving, peaceful, and free"). That's a first step. Or even before that, just promise your little one you won't be cruel to them on purpose. Borrow the Nurturer to improve this.

[B136] Whichever you choose, your little one may still answer with "Big fuckity whoopty doo." That's allowed, and one beautiful session wouldn't convince me either. Pick one thing to do that's small enough to repeat. A big gesture you later drop would only prove them right to doubt you, but a small thing you keep doing can change their mind.

[B137] Whenever your little one shows up looking for their parent, even if they don't know that's what they are doing, you go back to being the one they can rely on, and you're kind to them while you do it. If they show up to have some innocent fun, don't "put them in their place" as the adult. But sometimes they show up as the one who doesn't want to go to bed. Now you're in my mom's place: you still go to bed, and staying kind about it shows them bedtime isn't a lack of love.

[B138] ### Remind Yourself

[B139] The vow can live somewhere you'll see it, too, like on a stuffed animal from when you were little, a photo of yourself as a child, or just a sticky note on the bathroom mirror. Whatever helps you feel connected to the Nurturer and Protector you've been practicing. The Teddy bear note may remind you to think, "When I see you, I remember to stop attacking myself and go to bed on time."

[B140] A real-life child of yours, or a calm pet, can also act as a companion that brings out the relationship with your own inner child. But those are also their own beings with their own needs as well. Let them come and go without making them responsible for the session.

[B141] # When the Adult Voice Feels Fake

[B142] A lot of people get stuck right here. You call up the Nurturer and try to talk to your little one, and you get silence or a numb feeling, and all of a sudden your phone or the laundry feels urgent. Or some voice says this is silly, and it's hard to tell which part of you is saying it.

[B143] My friend Scott said:

[B144] > "Half the time when I try to connect to baby me, I feel fake and it seems crazy."

[B145] I used to be all personal trainer and teach persistence on this as the remedy. Keeping at it does help, since a little one who learned to expect being dropped won't trust you right away. But I don't think that explains the adult feeling fully. More importantly, I think it could even be like unintentional gaslighting to the little one to ask to connect with them when you don't believe they're even real. Like when my mom pretended to listen to me when I was young, but even less excusable (from the inner child's view) because she was not asking for my time when she did that. So, it's worth meditating first on why it feels fake.

[B146] For some people it only stops feeling fake in an altered state, like on MDMA or deep in meditation. All of a sudden your little one is right there! Oh my gosh! And the love or grief that sounded like therapy talk is just obvious. Old memories might come up too. They can feel totally real, but that alone doesn't prove they happened. I'd still hang on to what you felt, even once it's gone, and try to bring a little of it into your normal life. You've felt that your little one is real now, so talking to them afterward isn't just pretending.

[B147] ## The Parent You Inherited

[B148] I'd look at the parent "script" you inherited: how you learned to treat yourself from how people treated you growing up, long before you started building your Nurturer, Protector and Guide. It can be warm, but it's often more like a critic, or a warden with rules. The rules can even sound reasonable, like "You can come back to the table when you're done with your embarrassing crying fit." If yours was like that, your little one learned bit by bit to hide that part of their authentic self which wasn't allowed, so they could keep belonging. They might still be doing it.

[B149] If it's a critic, the parent you inherited also measures whatever you show the world against an impossible ideal, and then attacks you from both sides: you're not good enough, and anything that does look good is fake anyway. That second one means nothing you do could ever count, and it's one place feeling fake can come from. When a new grown-up voice shows up saying "Trust me," of course your little one doesn't answer.

[B150] But with the borrowed adulthood trick, the new grown-up can start outside your head, as someone who likes you the way you are, or as something you do to protect your little one, like letting the phone ring when you know how that call goes. You don't have to pretend to feel anything to let a phone ring. Your little one can watch you do it many times before trusting you, while the parent you inherited calls it fake.

[B151] ## Start With Whatever Showed Up

[B152] Your little one wants to play, too, which is why I call the deeper therapy pl/ork, play + work. A lot of the best discoveries come when you're both just messing around, kind of like how kids tell you what's really going on from the back seat, when nobody's looking right at them. Play not only paves the way for therapy, but is a part of how successful therapy should be measured.

[B153] You might go looking for your little one and get mad instead, or realize you've been staring at the rug. I count that as pl/ork too, even if it feels like it's in the way. Before you decide where it's coming from, ask it what it's trying to stop, or what it wants. Maybe it just has something to say. It could be your little one, mad that you took so long to come back, or a part of you that's trying to protect you. Maybe it's that parent you inherited, or something from earlier today, or a bit of each.

[B154] How you picture all that is up to you, as long as it helps, and the simpler the better. I think a fear that keeps turning up to tell you something is worth talking to as a part of you. If it sounds like your little one, though, hear it out the first time it shows up. Let a stray thought go as just thinking, and come back to the room you're in. A worry that just keeps asking to be reassured can go the same way, unless it's your little one asking.

[B155] I wouldn't spend long deciding what to call each part, though. Every part of you learns from how the others treat it, your little one included. A part of you that's trying to protect you might warn you about something, and when you hear it out, it doesn't have to get any louder.

[B156] You can tell that part something like, "I see you. I'm not going to force my way past you. What's going on?" Or however you'd actually say that. Then wait and listen patiently. If nothing comes, that's fine for now. No need to answer for that part just because you'd set this up as a healing session. Even without the answer, you can notice what sets the reaction off, and you can understand what the reaction is propelling you toward, and away from.

[B157] Or if you do get an answer, it could come in different forms, like as a voice or just a hunch. It's often more like a feeling. Maybe grief or anger. If so just listen and be present for it if that's what it wants. If it tells you to do something, I'd only do it if it's loving and leaves you free. It should make sense, too. Even then, anything big or hard to undo can wait until you're calm and you've checked it against what you actually know (if you're in danger, get safe first).

[B158] Anxiety deserves the same kind of listening. A lot of the time it's a part of you that's trying to protect you, making a guess about what's coming: that you'll get overwhelmed, maybe, or that people will laugh at you. It can be right! It's probably been wrong before too, and you know some of those times. So when it's safe to find out, I'd hear it out and then go see. Maybe nobody laughs. (Or they do, and you survive it.) If it might be real danger, though, I'd get some distance first, and find out how right it was later. Same with something a doctor should look at.

[B159] Some of it I wouldn't test at all, even when it sounds like a prediction. If a part hesitates about being touched or about sex, that's enough to stop. And if it says no to going deeper in this work, or to an altered state, I'd stop there too. It might not be able to tell you why.

[B160] ### Two Common Protective Patterns

[B161] Two kinds of reaction really are trying to protect you, more often than not. The first one is on duty all the time, and it can sound like a skeptic: "You're just making this up." It may have spent years keeping you from being humiliated again, or left again. That's a long time to stay on guard. It might be tired by now, but it's experienced and good at what it does. It has probably watched you get excited about other healing things, then drop them.

[B162] The other one shows up when you get close to something painful, as a sudden pull to do anything but feel it (even a yawn can come with an "I'm so done with this" feeling). It might believe there's no grown-up around who could hold what's coming up. I'd want out too. That yawn might actually be the opposite of checking out, though. Re-evaluation Counseling, a kind of peer counseling, counts yawning as emotional discharge, a feeling on its way out. The skeptic and the pull both started out trying to prevent something that really hurt, and they can keep at it even when what they do now hurts you. If you treat either one like an enemy, or like proof that something's wrong with you, it'll probably just think "See? I knew it," and stay in charge. I wonder what it would do if it didn't have to.

[B163] ### Hear the Protective Part First

[B164] Whichever protective part showed up, try talking to it in the same Nurturer voice you meant for your little one. A harder voice can seem more fitting for something that's been in your way. I'd still keep it the same. If that voice isn't there in you yet, borrow it from someone who's been safe for you. At some point you can ask that part, "What are you afraid would happen if you stepped back?" It might say this will just be one more performance, and you'll fool yourself again.

[B165] That one can sting, because there could be something to it. Maybe you've fooled yourself before, and that part remembers. "Tell me what you remember, I'm listening." works well here. It puts you into the role of the Nurturer, which alleviates the instinct to argue back. Usually your little one only comes forward after that part feels heard. Peek-a-boo! If you push past it, you might get exactly the performance it was afraid of. Even if you didn't win an Emmy last year, that can look convincing, with the right words and maybe even tears, while nothing underneath feels any safer.

[B166] A new roommate doesn't win you over with a speech on move-in day (hopefully, right?). The rent just has to keep showing up. Your Protector, the grown-up you, earns that protective part's trust about the same way, mostly out in ordinary life. The things you do can be small, like keeping a promise to yourself nobody else knew about. Or saying no to someone, and not texting an hour later to take it back. That second one can be really hard.

[B167] That part doesn't have to move out. It's been living there way longer than the grown-up you. It can keep watch while you take one small step toward your little one, and I think watching is what it wants anyway, to find out if there's a grown-up here now who could call it off if things got to be too much. If it says no, I'd listen. When it lets you, keep an eye on that part too, for any change at all. If it's been calling all this stupid, it might turn out to be scared of getting its hopes up. I wouldn't want to get mine up either. I'd tell it something like, "You don't have to hope yet, I'll keep coming back even when I mess up." Sometimes it doesn't say anything back for a while.

[B168] ### When to Change the Strategy

[B169] Weeks in and nothing much is changing? I'd want to quit too, or else keep doing the exact same thing forever so I never had to find out. Before either one, I'd look for what exactly is in the way. Sometimes those weeks were mostly reading about it and getting ready, which can feel a lot like doing it (this article included). Or you did sit down with your little one, and fear got there first, or a part of you that's trying to protect you. It can be as plain as never having the place to yourself. Whatever it turns out to be, I'd work on that one thing instead of giving up on everything.

[B170] Maybe you've been trying to comfort your little one for weeks, and the words are all there, but the loving grown-up behind them isn't, not yet. Some people at that point might try saying sweeter and sweeter things, and then upon that not working, perhaps just "throw the baby out with the bathwater," and decide reparenting is useless. I'd borrow that grown-up first. Then try again, and see if your little one can tell the difference.

[B171] One more thing about telling whether it helped. A few hours of feeling lighter, with the old feeling back the same day, is a few hours of relief. I'd take that! But I wouldn't decide from it that the problem's over, or even that the exercise did it, especially if you also slept well for once. Write down what changed and how long it lasted if you want, and then leave it alone.

[B172] How many tries is enough? I really don't know, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who promises you a number, including you, since some things shift in a day and some take about as long as learning guitar. If it's making things worse, or you just can't do it right now, I'd change course sooner (stopping counts). Not wanting to do it anymore is a reason too, but if that "nope" sounds like a part of you that's trying to protect you, I'd hear it out first. If it still says no, that's your answer. And if you keep following the reparenting steps correctly without getting the expected results, consider if you have a unique case these steps don't match yet. Maybe you'll find something that works better for you, or you can ask me what I'd do for this case, or ask a therapist you work with. Either way, I'd like to hear about it!

[B173] Sometimes it's the exercise that works. You feel real care for your little one and say exactly what they needed to hear. And a few days later you're skipping lunch and saying yes to everything again. A lot of people's first guess is that they didn't go deep enough. I don't think that's it, though, and I don't think this one failed either. Maybe the grown-up just hasn't come out of the exercise with you yet. Whatever you did for your little one in there, I'd try a tiny bit of it out here, like texting a friend that you're having a rough day instead of saying you're fine. That can honestly be harder than the meditation was. One small thing like that can teach your little one more than ten more good meditations. It doesn't have to happen after every session, and once isn't proof you've got it all down.
