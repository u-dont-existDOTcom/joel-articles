### Also Look Outward

A bad fight can make you feel like you finally understand somebody. Then the next conversation ruins the diagnosis a little. They come back, actually talk about what you raised, and maybe still disagree with you. The boundary that made you furious may really be theirs to keep. The first fight can still have been awful; it just isn't doing as much explanatory work anymore. Then some other subject comes up and you find yourself doing the same emotional job again while your own point disappears. That repetition is much closer to what [Lindsay Gibson's work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) asks you to notice about emotional immaturity.

You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.

The messiest version is when you really did hurt them. You owe repair for that. But the apology can keep going just because they are still disappointed. You apologize for what you actually did, then find yourself backing away from the rest of your position too. What exactly are you apologizing for at that point? Some of the guilt may belong to something real without the guilt itself being a good measure of the whole conflict. It also doesn't tell you whether the other person is manipulating you.

And even after all that, you may not get mutual understanding. Sometimes the other person hears you and still thinks you're wrong. Your no may still look unfair to them. You try another explanation. Then another. At some point the conversation is just the two of you disagreeing, with extra sentences piled on top. It can end there.
