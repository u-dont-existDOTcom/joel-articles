# Understanding Reparenting

Welcome, lovey bunnies!

Here's the idea behind Inner Child Reparenting, in a nutshell:

Growing up, all of us picked up automatic ways of understanding situations we find ourselves in, reacting based on those understandings, and then treating ourselves partly based on how we happened to handle things. Some of those patterns are still wise in general, although even then, we are always learning from what life throws at us. Others of those patterns were built for a crisis that's long over, and some just seem to make every situation worse.

Reparenting means consciously evolving and molding a better Inner Adult inside you, comprising a loving Nurturer, a safe Adult Protector (hereafter just the Protector) and a wise Guide. This chosen Inner Adult super hero gets into a real, loving relationship with the little one you still carry. That grown-up notices the old patterns and practices better ways of responding. At first, it's deliberate and might feel difficult. Many struggle to get past the silly, awkward feeling of it. Over time, instead of shying away from the silly, we can use the silly feeling in the work, to make it more playful. I call it pl/ork (play + work)! With time, the adult/child roles become second nature, so you spend less of your life managing yourself and more of it living with purpose, loving, relaxing, and playing.

## The Chicken-and-Egg Problem

Céline's song is called On ne change pas – "we don't change." I think that's both fortunately, and unfortunately, true for most of us. Fortunately in the sense that the sweet, innocent, curious, vivacious little one is still inside us, even if hiding. Unfortunate in the sense that we often don't truly develop the adult qualities that we might pretend to have, so we continue burdening that little one inside us with troubles they aren't made for handling. This shows up in how we may feel abandoned, helpless, impulsive, frightened, or desperate to be chosen.

<!-- EMBED https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4PxC3zZ2Mus : Rosey Grier, "It's All Right to Cry" (Free to Be... You and Me) -->

And I realized that it doesn't make sense to ask the child to "heal itself," which is really a disguised request for the child to abandon its own nature and turn into an adult. I call that "forced adulthood," where the child has to deal with situations the evolved inner adult should be handling--we see this in actual children all the time when their parents are not providing care. People even wear t-shirts saying, "I don't wanna adult today." The forced adulting often goes along with a misperception that there is something wrong with the inner child. Instead, we need to separately develop the qualities of a good inner parent for that rightly vulnerable child: the loving Nurturer, safe Protector, and wise Guide.

That's one grown-up doing three jobs, not three more people moving in to help with the rent, and each job keeps the others in balance. Say a party gets to be too much for your little one. The Protector gets you both out of there, and on the way home the Nurturer tells them they did great, even though mostly they hid in the bathroom. And the Guide helps them think twice about the next weird party they might go to, avoiding another cycle of bathroom vacationing. 🙈

The Guide is the one people misunderstand the most, because a lot of the bad parenting or misdirection we originally received was likely lacking in Protector and Nurturer functions, while over-relying on harsh guidance. So I left it out of the first version of this framework until my friend Key pointed it out as crucial. Without a healed Inner Guide, our lives can become small and like a never-ending therapy session when we focus only on caring for ourselves rather than giving ourselves a mission as well. That's the Guide's job. But on the other hand, some people over-rely on their discipline or their mission to avoid caring for themselves. And many folks try to find an ideal guide via some kind of guru-led political or religious cult.

If the phrase "inner parent" calls up the wrong archetype for you, pick another one. Your future self might work, or the captain of a ship, so long as they'd do those three jobs.

But here's the chicken-and-egg problem. What if you're so far inside the child-state that you can't become the adult? In this case, you sit there trying to be loving while your mind is becoming controlled again by the inherited critic voice, or you start going numb, throwing a fit, or panicking.

Sometimes somebody else may have to do one part of the adult role with you or for you first. You get to feel what that is like and watch how they do it. Maybe you copy one small thing you've seen them do, or imagine them there when they're not, and try one piece yourself while they're still helping with the rest. That's what I mean by borrowed adulthood. You may still need help with almost all of it for a while, but over time there are more and more of those little parts you can do yourself.

## Five Stages of Reparenting

For now, I think of the whole path in five progressive stages. You might be starting at any point here (and it may change even from hour to hour as well):

1. Forced adulthood: You're still stuck identifying as the lost inner child, forced frequently into handling situations that require more maturity, even if it looks like you've got it all together. At this stage, use your childlike superpowers to come into the "Wow of Now" (have you seen the movie Waking Life?), and make sure there's enough safety around you to continue with reparenting.  
2. Begin to borrow adulthood – remember watching Mr. Rogers or Sesame Street? Those adult role models are what we're looking for at this stage. This can almost feel like playing house as a kid where you imagined how you'd take care of a baby, etc.  
3. Practice being the adult – do at least one part of caring for yourself while support is still there from the previous step.  
4. Adult mode itself – after sufficient practice with reparenting, you become a natural at sending love to little you, protecting them, and shepherding them.  
5. Adult and child in relationship – the adult leads. The child isn't pushed away, but doesn't have to run the whole life alone. This can actually feel really weird if you've been stuck in forced adulthood for a while. Our goal is to go from weird to silly and playful. Lose self-consciousness while gaining self-awareness, if that makes sense!

Whichever stage you're at, saying the right thing when you sit down with your little one helps, but you also have to start actually doing some of it in the rest of your life. Depending on your maladaptive patterns, improvements could look opposite to each other. If you're a workaholic normally, then your little one might start believing in grown-up you when they catch you lying down because you're wiped out, instead of pushing through one more chore. Or the opposite, when you stop procrastinating so much and clean your room, then they start believing in you.

Reparenting isn't an exercise you finish, even if you make excellent progress. You might feel like you've mastered the adult/child inner relationship, and then it's Friday night and your boss asks you to stay late again. You get up the nerve to tell them no, and go home feeling okay about it, and nobody had to cry on a meditation cushion for that to happen.

## My Journey

I used to preach [Buddhist enlightenment](https://nibbana.u-dont-exist.com) too much, because that's what helped me out of a long existential crisis.

From what I've seen, most people who chase spiritual freedom, as valid as that is, are, at least at first, trying to get away from something painful in themselves, usually childhood trauma of some kind. That's [spiritual bypassing](https://bypassing.u-dont-exist.com). Plenty of gurus do it too; I've written about the [Dalai Lama as one example](https://substack.com/@udontexist/note/c-245314772?r=5vdc6m).

The way I think this works is simple. Imagine you're broke and starving, and your baby is starving too. Understandable: there is no food. Then a food bank opens nearby and suddenly you've got lots of nutritious munchies. But you're eating all of it yourself while baby you is still starving. [*Unforgivable*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0dJu1Jj7VTw&t=36s).

Nobody leaves a baby starving on purpose. I just didn't believe there was a baby. Inner-child stuff sounded silly to me until I could actually feel him. Then there was this little me actually hurting, and I could send some of the love I'd already found his way.

Spending your whole life processing your wounded inner child isn't freedom either. Spirituality opened a much bigger horizon for me – love and happiness beyond the conditions of my life story. Maybe ordinary life already gives many folks enough love and meaning. It didn't for me. Conditional love felt radically insufficient, so I kept looking for something deeper.

I was well-loved as a child, but from age 12 to 26, I was depressed, and cried every night from feeling the pain of the world. [Loving-kindness (metta) meditation](http://love.u-dont-exist.com/) healed that depression almost instantly for me, so it became my regular practice automatically. I later found out that many people weren't able to do a deep metta meditation, because they literally didn't know what love felt like, as they hadn't received it as a child. My 2nd wife told me she didn't know the word for love in her native language, because her mom never said that word to her.

But even though my depression was gone, the sending-love-outward metta practice didn't reach everything in me. An old childhood wound could still hit, like if someone accused me of being lazy, and then suddenly it's like I was that same little kid again, even tho the spiritual part of me felt free as a bird. And a lot of the childhood stuff around my attachment to girls/women was still there.

At some point I guess I heard folks talking about inner child therapy, and being curious about different healing approaches, I thought I'd give it a go even if I wasn't the prime candidate. It felt like I was making it up at first, which seems quite common. That didn't stop me from occasionally practicing, but I did stop "pushing" it to be a certain way, and I'd stop trying to feel it was realer than it was. I would go more into listening and waiting mode rather than imagining mode.

At one pivotal point, I used San Pedro cactus and CBD tea with sublingual mapacho, and that cracked it open. I had a full-on audio-visual communion with my little Joelito and I heard a few things he was still sad about from so long ago. Things which most people would laugh at, because they're seemingly so minor. Like when I hit my neighbor while riding my bicycle, and even after I profusely apologized, her mom said, "Don't you have any common sense???" And I found out why I was always pushing myself to help others. It was because when I was 5yrs old, at the Zendik community in Texas that we were visiting, they held a meeting with everyone there (like 50 people) and me in the center. I was critiqued for not offering to help anyone I saw who was working. Well, sorry! I never knew I was supposed to. That's a pattern that has stuck, although it does help to remember what caused it, so I don't take it so seriously.

Years later, I found a warm, intuitive inner child therapist, named Daja, randomly (if you believe anything is random), as a friend of a friend, while in Kenya. I figured I already knew this stuff, but I gave it a shot. She did actually help me see some things more clearly. For example, she had me write down how many people I could fully trust as a child (lots), then how many people I could fully trust as an adult (zero). That was difficult, and it's actually the opposite of what you might expect from inner-child therapy, since usually that's for people who had problems trusting as a child.

What it showed me is that I was ignoring a lot of things that were really important to look at, and that was one of them. I wouldn't have even thought to ask the question she asked me if I were doing it on my own. It's also an example of how therapy can sometimes make you feel worse than before, when you become more aware of problems. But personally I prefer to see things as they are, so I can figure out how to work with them, rather than just hide in delusions.

It also showed me that I really do need to count more on myself than I assumed. I need to be much better as an inner adult for my inner child, because my childhood was actually so nice that I kind of just assume I can trust everyone, and [that gets me into trouble](https://sidney.u-dont-exist.com/)! So it got me curious to learn more about inner-child therapy, which is a process I'm still in the midst of as I test this guide with people.

# Safety and Readiness

## Dip Your Toes In Before You Go Deep

How much of the grown-up you is actually here right now? Check if you can pause before you act. Are you following through after making decisions? If you can't do those things because you're basically still stuck identifying as the frightened kid, then let's leave the deeper conversation until later. Do whatever helps you come back a bit before you try to talk to little you. And have someone steady there to support you if possible.  

And if the grown-up you is there, and it's just how deep you're going that's too much, dip your toes in the kiddie pool first. Like the flight attendants say, it's good to know where the exits are. 😅 So can you stop in the middle and come back to the room? If you can't, or going in at all keeps making it worse, I'd come all the way back out first. Some people try one technique after another instead, to prove to themselves they can take it. Your little one may have had to just take it back then, though. Now there's a grown-up around who can say, "Okay, that's enough for today," and try something gentler, like saying hi to them with your eyes open.

If you need a break from the communion, simply jot down the reason and the progress made so far. Then come back later, once that reason has changed and you feel comfortable.

If being alone was a big part of what hurt during your childhood, I'd call somebody now, mess and all, since reaching toward people is part of taking care of your little one too. Even if you don't have someone you know who wants to listen, there are warmlines for this (not hotlines unless it's an emergency).

## Your Body Might Need Some Love First

If you notice tension in your solar plexus or abdomen, or anywhere else that seems to be keeping you from relaxing into that heart chakra love, try some non-forced, slower exhales and a little gentle self-massage. I love using one foot to massage the other foot like a yin-yang symbol, as well as gentle circular or varied directional massage in the solar plexus, neck, head, abdomen, or whatever spot is feeling tense.

I'd also recommend checking into deeper somatic therapies like [Shaking](https://www.skool.com/shakingmedicine/about?ref=ee69b2178e96491f88530789dc160291), tapping (EFT), EMDR, Brainspotting, Somatic Experiencing, Water Therapy, Massage, Energy Healing, or some other bottom-up approach.

When your nervous system is flooded, your protective parts are busy throwing buckets of water out of the boat, so they're not going to let your little one come out and talk much anyway. Some people go straight into EMDR to work on desensitizing their worst memory before they can even stay in the room. ### When Therapy Stirs Things Up

Some somatic work or reparenting therapy can feel pleasant, and some can feel like a raging storm got unleashed. It can bring up grief and fear, and sometimes nightmares later on as the memories begin to unwind. It's a bit like going to the gym. You want to make gains, and sore muscles the next day can be good, but proper range of (e)motion here is also key to avoid injury.

Keep track of how you feel over the next few days, like how refreshing your sleep is, and how easy it feels to get through your day. Do you want to go back toward what came up, or is some part of you saying no? If you can't quite tell yet what feels different, I'd just make the next session simpler, one change at a time, like a science nerd, so you learn what made the difference. You can play around with variables like shorter session length, or shallower diving, time of day, keeping eyes open, having a friend around, or holding a teddy bear as a stand-in for your little one.

And what about those hard dreams, or images that pop up during a session? They can show what feels important or frightening right now. Sometimes they show what feels missing, or what feels needed. Although sometimes dreams can show you real memories and even predict the future on occasion, we need to be careful not to assume that they are doing that each time.

### Notice the Part That Came Here

Some people hear all this about loving themself and answer, "No. I *hate* myself." Like my friend Malia, who told me, "My inner child is just capricious! What's to love?" If that's what you find, just listen to it, without arguing back. But if you opened this article, something in you wanted things to be different, whatever the rest of you was saying. The same goes if you came to therapy or asked someone for help.

It might have been hope, or desperation. Maybe you were curious, or scared. Some people come here out of duty (my sister kept telling me to see therapists; she says everyone needs therapy). Or it was more like, "I'm not going to keep suffering like this." And maybe it was love. Ask that part what it hoped might happen when it brought you here, and follow that thread.

## When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child

I've been writing this whole guide as if everybody who tries it wants to take care of the inner child. Some people get a little rush out of making somebody scared or small, though. For such folks, inner child therapy is clearly not the place to start. They need to first deconstruct the adult identity they've got.

When I talk with folks like that, I mostly want to know what the identity gets them and what it costs, and especially what it's really serving. Some of them even call themselves "sovereign," yet somehow there's an urge they have to obey, which sounds more like slavery to me.

This part tends to scare the wrong people, like somebody who's horrified by their own thoughts. Everybody gets scary thoughts they'd rather not have sometimes, and they don't make you a bad person, but why would you bring your inner child into the middle of them? The nice thing about inner child therapy is that you can wait until you're calm and actually ready. A real parent doesn't get to do that, which is one reason nobody manages to get a perfect 4.0 GPA on the parent report-card their children bring home. 😛

And if somebody actually wonders whether they're malicious, they'd do better to meditate on it and find out why they're asking before they bring their inner child anywhere near it. Are they really feeling malicious, or is it some voice in their head saying so, like the inherited critic? If some of it turns out to be malice they actually want to act on, the inner child can wait.

### Ill Will and Loving-Kindness

In one of the Buddha's own accounts of how he woke up from identity, ill will shows up in his mind and he sees it's hurting him. If he had to figure that out by watching, it's not obvious. Ill will can make you feel powerful. Being on fire would too, and people would definitely get out of your way, but unless you're the Human Torch from the Fantastic Four, you'd be the one getting burnt first. Has it ever left anybody at ease, with nothing to regret afterward?

The Buddha also told his son, Rahula, that loving-kindness is what gets rid of ill will, and compassion does the same for cruelty. I'm biased on loving-kindness, since it got me out of my depression. For these folks it starts with the grown-up they are now, though, and their little one stays out of it.

As for when their little one gets a turn, I'd say once they've had some bad days where scaring somebody would've felt good, and they didn't do it. Until then it helps a lot to have a real person in their life who likes them for the good they see deep down, and won't laugh along with the mask persona, since loving-kindness is easier to pick up from someone who already has some.

## If You Want to Kill Yourself

Maybe you're reading this about to act, or with the means close by. Then before anything else, get another human being involved, and put some distance between you and the means. A crisis line counts, and the person on the other end can be a total stranger.

As for the internal psychology of it, when the part that wants to die feels like all of you, go ahead and notice who's doing the noticing (more on that in Noticing Counts, further on), or borrow an adult from the adult lending library in your head :).

### The Spiritual Side of the Question

I try not to proselytize about spirituality, because I've made that mistake before, and I now see that it's better to let folks find it when they are ready. But when someone tells me they want to kill themself, I consider the topic to be brutally opened by default, regardless of whether they meant to do that, because if they actually do kill their body (which they might mistake to be their self), that's a very powerful spiritual challenge right there, even if they didn't realize it would be.

Then ask: what is the self you want to kill? Is that self the same thing as your body? Why? Do you have the same body in dreams? What exactly do you expect your body's death to accomplish? If the problem is a state of mind, like self-hatred or despair, shame or loneliness, what makes you so sure that destroying the body ends it?

In Buddhism, death isn't an off switch, and it isn't a free new-age get-out-of-jail-free card back to the Divine Source. Repeated thought and intention shape the mind, and that keeps going through death and rebirth. Yes, that can include hell realms (and hell can be right here on Earth, also). A human birth is a rare chance to understand suffering and end its causes.

You don't have to believe that, but if you're still thinking of staking your life on the opposite claim, that death is just nothing, might that be a double standard to look into? Curiosity killed the cat, you might say, [so research could be dangerous](https://www.forbes.com/sites/startswithabang/2020/07/30/you-must-not-do-your-own-research-when-it-comes-to-science/). 😅 But despite the risk of curiosity, researchers at the University of Virginia have been busy little bees, collecting cases for 60 YEARS now, of young kids who talk about a past life, and there's [a big pile of near-death accounts too](https://iands.org). Even if every case is different, I'd read a few of those cases before betting my life on "nothing."

Right here in this one life, it's apparent that the more you hate yourself, the better your mind gets at it, and being alone tends to deepen the despair. But lucky us, because physical life keeps handing out redirections, like that guy who found me in a random park crying one time, and asked me "What's wrong?" If the many traditions and the accumulating scientific evidence are right that the mind's causal process goes on after the body dies, killing the body may take away some of those redirections and leave the suffering right where it was. That sounds like a substantially worse deal to me.

My father used to tell suicidal people, "Oh, you want to kill yourself? That's wonderful! Now you can change what you've always been too afraid to change in your life, and then see if you still want to end it!" So, what would you tell my dad? What's the change you still can't bring yourself to make? Leaving a relationship or quitting a job? Telling your family who you really are? Those might sound difficult, but they're actually considerably easier and more likely to help than dying.

### This human life is the most precious opportunity

As I mentioned in my [nibbana article](http://nibbana.u-dont-exist.com/), I've had my most profound spiritual peak experiences and realizations when I had the least interest in continued existence. And the Buddha taught in [MN 129](https://suttacentral.net/mn129/en/sujato), attaining a human birth is as rare and precious as it would be for a one-eyed turtle to pop his head up through a hoop on the surface of the vast ocean. You won the cosmic jackpot, so don't throw away the ticket before you open the envelope to find it!

# Building Your Inner Adult

## Borrow the Adult To Become the Adult

Now, don't tell anyone about this! I'm going to reveal my Magician's Secret. I call it the "borrowed adulthood trick". The new grown-up you're moulding can start outside your head, as someone who likes you just the way you are, or as something you do to protect your little sweetie pie, like letting the phone ring when you know already how that call goes. You don't have to pretend to feel anything to let a phone ring. It may take many such ignored calls before your inner child can start trusting you (and all the while, the parent you inherited calls your efforts fake).

This is the heart of the reparenting manual. Here's where you start to come into your power. Try to see the end goal as attainable right at the start here so that you:

### Don't Sell Your Inner Adult To Buy An Outer Adult

If you still feel like a kid inside, somebody who seems grown-up can feel like salvation, and letting them do some of the grown-up jobs for you can actually help for a while. But they can know things you don't and still be wrong for you to follow too much. The test is whether the relationship makes you more grounded, truthful, and free.

How do you check that? I'd keep what you feel around them in mind, and look at their track record too. Do they respect a no, or keep pushing on it, and what happens when you disagree? If something outside of them backs up what they're telling you, and it all adds up, that's a good sign.

Even [Pema Chödrön](https://pemachodronfoundation.org/product/taking-the-leap-book/) (who inspired parts of this guide), who comes from a tradition where devotion to a guru is a big deal, still says a teacher should "wean us from dependency altogether" and help us grow up.

### Signs of Manipulation

Some gurus really do demand surrender, and they're the opposite of the Guide: they point you at themselves for everything, as if they want to be your God. Others are so professional that they don't even need to ask anyone to follow them. Someone seems to get your little one like nobody ever has, or treats it like it's special, and it starts thinking, "They know me better than I know myself! Can you believe it?" 🙊 I still remember a Sadhguru video where he said anyone who follows him is an idiot, and his followers were still worshipping him in the comments, proud idiots. 🤪

Here's where I want to clearly depart from the new-age wizdumb that all your problems are self-created: Somebody may really have crossed a boundary. That's not just because they are your karmic partner mirroring what God wants to show you in yourself, even if that also applies sometimes. Your reaction is what you can control the most, though.

Some manipulative people are too smart to act perfect. They lead with their wounds, or with how brutally honest they are about their flaws (very disarming, which is sort of the point), and every warning you hear about them just becomes more proof of how profound they are. Your Protector should be called to step in whenever love bombing hides obvious red flags your more skeptical friends would love to point out if you'd listen. After all, "I have honesty issues and I'm working on them" may be a perfectly honest thing to say. It's also telling you there's an honesty problem right now, and knowing about a problem isn't fixing it.

Other types of manipulation to check for include:  
**Gaslighting**, when your perception of reality is slowly giving way to someone else's. Combat this by trusting your perceptions (after documenting or double checking).

In abusive relationships, gaslighting often turns into **D.A.R.V.O.**: Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender. This is how the abuser flips the script on accountability. If you notice this happening, extricate yourself quickly.

  
**Guilt tripping,** where you're made to feel responsible for things you shouldn't be. Like someone did something for you and then later makes you seem like a bad person because you haven't done enough to give back. Check if the guilt is deserved or not.

**White Knight**: this is when problems are created that you need to be saved from, which can then lead back into guilt-tripping, or dependence. Solve your own challenges rather than letting yourself over-rely on someone as a pattern.

**Triangulation** is where another person is brought in to the conversation so it becomes 2 vs 1. Insist on direct communication with one person if this tactic is employed.

**Silent treatment**: when someone cuts back their emotional involvement to punish or control you (can be confused with simply avoiding situations they aren't able to handle). Don't chase these people, nor reverse it onto them.

**Breadcrumbing** is when silent treatment is reduced just enough to make you hooked on trying to get their attention. Value yourself enough so this doesn't work.

That's a lot of manipulation tactics! And those sneaky sneaks are always coming up with new ones, so it's time we develop our spidey sense. We can also look inward to check if we are using those on ourselves or others.

### Borrow One Competency at a Time

A complete ideal parent may be impossible to imagine (unless you saw The Brady Bunch). And imagination is pretty hard for adults anyway since it kinda gets stamped out of kids at an early age ([along with spirituality](https://substack.com/@udontexist/note/c-355483418?r=5vdc6m&utm_source=notes-share-action&utm_medium=web)), but it's not just a foolish waste of time. It's actually a genius mode unlock. So since the adult is actually a baby when it comes to power to imagine, start small: Borrow one capacity at a time from a figure who embodies it well.

While all of these super powers should be developed alongside each other, the inner child's receptivity will naturally follow this order: first he/she wants to feel safe (Protector), then, building on that safety, feel loved (Nurturer), and then building on the safety & love, trust allows the little one to be eager to take guidance, and that's where Guide-building comes.

### Noticing Counts

Even before you build up any of these capacities, you can start with simply being present with your little one, which does a lot more than you might think to set the stage for the proper division of identity. Just saying, "Huh, somebody in here is really scared." might be the most adult thing you've got right now, but notice that whoever's noticing isn't all the way inside the scared one.

It can help to use your own name. For me, instead of "I'm terrified," that'd be "Little Joel is terrified. What does Little Joel need right now?" You'll sound a bit like Elmo :) In [lab studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5495792/), though, people who talked to themselves this way reacted less to upsetting pictures and memories, and their brains didn't seem to be working any harder to do it.

If you can tell your parts apart pretty easily, you've probably got plenty of noticing already. Don't keep drilling it just because the love isn't coming yet. A lot of meditators are great at watching. I was, and baby me was still starving.

## Protecting and Building Trust With Your Little One

Plenty of people are still at the putting-up-with part of reparenting. Even with little or no warmth for your little one yet, the relationship can start with the Protector's plain acts and the warmth can come after, which feels backwards, because you'd think you have to feel it first before you could do anything for them. But yeah, bonding starts somewhere. And this is not to say you've never done anything to care for yourself. If not, you'd be dead right now. But we're making additional acts of care intentional now as a base for love to grow.

As you may have heard, not every hero wears a cape! Maybe an older sibling protected you well at some point, so they were your hero then, even if they weren't a full adult. So whatever is troubling your inner child right now, start with asking what a minimally competent adult would do in this situation.

### Talk to the hand!✋🏿

Setting boundaries is an obvious first protector move. "What's a 'boundary'?", I might be asking. That looks like saying no to something that's costing you too much (money, time, health, or whatever resource). Handle that task you've been putting off, or the tax man, or your DUIs in Maryland, even if all you do today is open the envelope.

Maybe it's something seemingly simple, like actually smelling that old food you're about to eat and then trashing it if it smells a little weird (that's one I'm still working on). If you're about to send an angry text, hit pause. If the room itself triggers your spidey sense, leave, or lock the door and get help. Even if this still feels like the child acting like the adult, for now, that's ok. "Fake it til you make it" isn't exactly how I'd phrase it, but yeah. Afterward, you can tell your little one, "I did that for us. That was the Protector." If it sounds funny, laugh! Laughter is the best medicine.

Keep one small promise to your little one. If you promise to clean your room, then do that. Maybe your little one had grown-ups who said sweet things and then did nothing, and maybe you've done it too, so this is how they start to believe you.

### Making It Intentional

Intentional, meaning instead of waiting for crisis to hit to react, you pick one of those protector acts and decide when you'll do it, and what the first step is. If it needs money or somebody's help, sort that out before the day, and if you'll have to tell someone something they won't like, say it out loud to yourself ahead of time. Then put yourself in their shoes to see how it sounds, and go back & forth like that a couple times until both sides feel good about it, or good enough, as long as what you needed to say is still in there.

After the day, look at what actually happened. Say you did the Protector action(s) and you don't feel any better, or you feel worse. Standing up for yourself can do that at first. Not doing it is harder to figure out. Fear is the first guess. But did you know how to do it, and why? Were you worn out? Maybe whoever said they'd help couldn't make it. 🤗

### Protecting them from yourself

Even if you mean well, things don't always come out the way you hoped. You can protect your little one from yourself too, like by repairing after an internal attack. "What's an 'internal attack'?" Calling yourself an idiot for losing your keys again, for one. Your little one was listening, so you go back and apologize to them. If they're sarcastic about it, you hear it out instead of snapping back. They don't trust you yet, and you don't ask them to. They complain about something, and if they're right, you say so. All of that is evidence they're accepted and you're safe to be around. Love needs those to start with, but without warmth they can still feel like they're only being put up with.

### Protecting from the Parent You Inherited

I'd look at the parent "script" you inherited: how you learned to treat yourself from how people treated you growing up. Maybe sometimes that script feels warm, but maybe too often it feels more like a critic, or a warden with rules. The rules can even sound totally reasonable, like "You can come back to the table when you're done with your embarrassing crying fit." If your childhood was like that, your little one learned bit by bit to hide that part of their authentic self which wasn't allowed to show up, so they could keep belonging. And if so, do a double check: they might still be doing it!

If the script is mostly critical, it means the parent you inherited is busy measuring whatever you show the world against an impossible ideal. Then you can feel attacked from both sides: not only are you not good enough, but if you do seem to be good enough, it's probably fake, so it's like nothing you do really counts. When a new grown-up voice shows up saying "Trust me," with a background like that, well, of course your little one doesn't answer.

Keep in mind that while a critical voice might be that inherited parent script, or any outside influence with no concern for your welfare, it could also be trying to protect you from rejection or failure. It may be pointing to a real problem, but in a cruel way.

#### Reframing attacks as unskillful attempts to help

One of my favorite teachers, Mr. Rogers, gave us an excellent example of how to extract the sweet nectar of truth from what less advanced souls might recoil at. In the movie A Beautiful Day in the Neighborhood, Lloyd, a reporter, tells Mr. Rogers, "I can't imagine it was easy… growing up with you as a father." He doesn't collapse under that or fight it, but he does answer what's worth answering in it. He tells Lloyd that until recently his oldest son never told people about his dad, and his youngest "genuinely tested" him. Eventually he and his youngest found their way, and now he's very proud of both his sons. Then he says, "But you are right, Lloyd. It couldn't have been easy on them. Thank you. Thank you for that perspective."

We want to use the Mr. Rogers reframing trick to stay curious about the critical voice's motivations, and open to any kernels of truth it points out, without accepting its insults and cruelty. Then we send it on its way with compassion, since that voice surely needs some.

### Earning their trust, day to day

A new roommate doesn't win you over with a speech on move-in day (hopefully, right?). The rent just has to keep showing up each month. Your Protector earns your little one's trust about the same way, out there in the real world, day after day. Little things make a big difference, like keeping a promise to yourself nobody else knew about, or saying no to the 13th friend today who asked you to Google something for them (I'm still working on that one).

## The Nurturer

### Love Can Start Before It's Trusted ❤️

Sometimes the love is there for your little one and still feels unsafe to them, or safe for a minute and then not. Some researchers found a phrase for that: "[compassion-threat](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31301164/)." People in their study with anxious or avoidant attachment, or a tendency to be self-critical, reacted to compassion imagery like a threat, especially at first.

Love and trust are reciprocal and intertangled, like the Chicken and Egg problem. Without trust, the love seems fake. And without love, the trust is shallow. When you show your love, it can sound like you're asking them to trust that you love them. But your love doesn't need their trust. It does need you to be honest with them, which isn't the same as perfect. They're still going over your track record (they've heard what you still call yourself when you mess up, sometimes out loud).

When your little one says "I don't trust you," you can answer, "I believe you. And I love you." I learned this when I was 5. My mom told me it was time for bed (or something equally outrageous to a 5-year-old). I cried and screamed "I HATE YOU! I wish you were dead!" 😡 She answered, "But I love you!" 🥰

That completely threw me. Fights don't usually go like that. Anger is a way to get more power when you feel a lack of justice or agency. You puff yourself up so you seem frightening or dangerous, or you demand to be heard. My anger was there to get me heard, and her answer showed me I didn't need it for that. Whatever injustice I thought there was wasn't coming from a lack of love, either. So the anger seemed stupid. I saw for myself that it was only hurting me, without helping me see clearly or get what I wanted. Still, what I screamed was true for a part of me right then, and what she said back was true too, so I could hate somebody who loved me and she'd still be my mom the whole time.

When it's your little one yelling at you, though, try not to turn "But I love you" into a prompted line you say on cue, because then it can feel like a tactical ruse to brush the anger off. Your little one will be watching to see if the love stays afterward and takes whatever's true in their anger seriously.

But even when the love does stay, your little one might still come back with "Big fuckity whoopty doo. I don't believe you." 🙄 You answer, "Okay. I believe that you don't believe me. I still love you. 🤗 You don't have to love me back. Tell me what makes the love feel unreal to you."

Your little one might say it's you, without giving more details at first. Keep practicing your listening, and eventually you'll get more of a concrete complaint, which you can consider without getting flustered from the meanness they packaged it in. Like my anger at bedtime, their swearing is there to get them heard, so once your ear guards are off, they don't need it anymore.

And sometimes you might feel the love for them, yet there's also some other voice at the same time, besides what you choose to say. When they say "I don't believe you," something in you might resent that, and maybe even fire back an accusation, and then they can learn your love is only there as long as they take it the right way. The resentment can be purely visceral: being doubted after you've tried feels unfair, but there's nothing unfair in them going over your track record. On the other hand, don't give in and start believing your love is more conditional than it really is, regardless of the past. Apply the Mr. Rogers reframing trick here as well.

### If the Love Isn't Coming Yet

If you weren't loved well as a child, then the suggestion to "love your inner child" can sound like nonsense. Love them how? Maybe you really want the best for your little one. Maybe you hug them in your imagination, or miss them. Yet you still can't feel the warmth people mean when they say love. One direction to work with is to start smaller.

Maybe just imagining a caring grown-up makes you feel more threatened, or lonelier. Lonelier is a strange one, with somebody right there. Then go smaller still, to the least cruel response a decent adult could offer your little one. This method of finding the most feasible next challenge is called [scaffolding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instructional_scaffolding).

Taking the hostility out makes room for warmth. Sometimes the warmth is actually there, with a cold part standing in front of it. If you've never been safe being loved, your ego can act like a rigid security guard that won't let the feeling in. But it generally will stand down once it feels its concerns have been heard and addressed. But the guard is still available for dealing with people who haven't earned your trust.

If the love still isn't coming, you can at least stop judging your little one. Obviously no insults. Helpful advice is great, we all need advice. Let them absorb it at their pace. And don't expect a thank-you for the session, like they booked it. That's enough to start being the adult, even if it isn't enough yet for them to trust you. That takes warmth, and if there isn't any yet, borrow some.

Your little one might not take it well at first. More like "Oh, so NOW you care?" Your first instinct might be "But I'm trying!" Some days, just hearing it out like a decent adult is the only thing you'll manage to borrow.

### Borrow Someone Else's Love

When a good friend sits with you while you fall apart it does help you feel better. You call them again the next time, and they get a little better at it every time while you don't. So steal some of it. Usually what helped was how they just sat there without trying to fix you. Ten seconds of sitting with yourself like that is good enough for a start, so try it before you call next time.

If you don't have anyone like that right now, try remembering when you did. Perhaps a therapist or your grandmother really cared for you. Think of someone like that you remember fondly, and imagine how they would sit beside your little one.

### Borrow Your Love For Someone Else

Sometimes it's not that you can't love in general. Your dog can chew up your good shoes and be forgiven before you've found the other one. But maybe you direct that love to your little one and the attempt goes flat, or starts to feel weirdly unsafe. Just feel that love you can feel for others for a minute, in your body, before you try to redirect anything. Then as you relax into that and enjoy it more, imagine it flowing downhill, like an overflowing river of delicious chocolate. Now imagine how happy your inner child is at the bottom of the hill to play in that chocolate love that rolled down!

Or put somebody you care about into your exact life, your family, all the conditions you face, and then step outside it and ask what you'd tell them as a friend. This [psychological trick](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21728454/) has a rich history around it, and the blind spot behind it even has a name, [Solomon's paradox](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24916084/), after the biblical king everybody brought their disputes to, who then wrecked his own life.

Then take a small piece of whatever you'd tell them and do it yourself. If it's "get more sleep," that could be brushing your teeth before midnight instead of at two.

### Show Up When Nothing's Wrong

If the only time the Nurturer shows up is when something's wrong, your little one can start to feel like they're your job. Sometimes show up just to say "I'm glad you're here," and then enjoy them for a minute, the way you'd enjoy a little kid who wants to show you a rock they found.

Watch how your love lands. "I care about you" can be patient, or it can be "I care about you, now hurry up and feel better." Getting close can be too much, too, the way kids squirm away from an aunt they barely know who goes in for the big hug, and then climb all over the uncle who's just sitting there. Knowing that love is in you somewhere counts for a lot, and you're borrowing something you already know how to do.

### A Smaller Doorway: Goodwill

Sometimes "I love you" is more than you can honestly say, especially to somebody who's treated you badly. Even the usual whittled-down loving-kindness prayer, "May you be happy," can feel like rewarding them. That's why I go with the Buddha's overall wish (paraphrasing): "**May you be loving, peaceful, and free**," since if they were all of those, they wouldn't have done what they did, and then they actually would be lovable.

This goodwill isn't just for the target of it, though. You may find it bounces back and opens up a little warmth in your heart. And if "I love you" goes flat when you aim it at yourself or your little one, the same wish works there, too. 🫶🏿

### Ask God for a Loan

The dog kind of love is real love. Some people also know a love that's a whole different size, like God's, or whatever opened up for them in prayer or on a long retreat, and I don't want to lump that in with the dog. If you've been there, don't shrink it down to a thought experiment about a friend.

If you haven't been there yet (and even if you have), I have good news for you!

#### Your Loan is PRE-APPROVED!

<!-- EMBED https://www.youtube.com/shorts/4TYvq-d0-XM : A short from EndSickness.org, "Gratitude heals nervous system" -->

One practical door anyone can use to slowly experience divine love is gratitude (even after the Turkey's done & Thanksgiving is over!). Genuinely cultivated gratitude (not just empty/forced ritual) is one way to experience divine love for the first time, or to get it back when the feeling fades. I think it's essential if we want to live in that love instead of occasionally visiting it.

This is a bit tricky to put into words, but let me try anyway… You may be suffering now, but you could be suffering more. That's something to be grateful for (although please don't fall into the common trap of being grateful that you have what others don't have, as that's more like selfishness). I'm not talking just about changing your "glass half empty" perception to a glass "half full perception." I'm not pushing "toxic positivity." Being thankful shouldn't be pushed on people who just need to grieve for the moment. On the other hand, I've noticed that I can sometimes completely flip a bad psychedelic trip into a good one with gratitude alone.

If you have a moment right now to breathe, and to really feel the beauty of that breath as it goes in and out, that's something to be grateful for. I learned this many times the hard way. For example, when I got tuberculosis from a cave in Mexico at age 20, and coughed for six months straight, eventually having my throat close up, leading to a near death experience. And then in Senegal, I had a suspected bilharzia infection in 2021, and could only painfully pee blood. I promised to always be thankful if I could ever pee normally again. Miraculously, I then found some obscure ethnobotanical research (on [Google Scholar](http://scholar.google.com), before AI) which led me to drink roselle tea. Within an hour, I was cured. I don't always remember to say "thank you" when I pee now, but I try.

That may sound like a silly, quirky waste of time to some folks. But what I notice is that saying thank you for every little thing, whenever I remember to, is not about imposing some kind of "spiritual discipline." It is directly reconnecting me to something pure and sacred.

##### Mo' Money, Mo' Problems

And I'm certainly not preaching "prosperity philosophy," like the mega churches, even though it can seem to start at that. We still need to remember that if we can only be thankful for the good things we have, we can fall into the trap of thinking that what we have is happiness itself. I'm ultimately reaching for something much deeper. Because getting what you want is an endless loop, and it doesn't lead to being content with less. When you can see that less is more, at a deeper level, that's where the real gratitude flourishes.

And even (or especially) when I am suffering, I try to remember to be grateful for that also, because if not for suffering, I would not even think about how much I have enjoyed not suffering as much, and how much this tribulation will help me to develop more humility, compassion, wisdom, and gratitude in future. Most importantly, it connects me with the plight of the countless other beings suffering at that very moment, authentically bringing up a [wellspring of compassion in my heart for all of us](http://love.u-dont-exist.com). And I see clearly that it is my inner child who is feeling this pain, and what an honor it is that I can be there for him.

Of course, that's not to say you should not try to suffer less, or get away from abusers. The point of life, according to every spiritual tradition and religion, I think, is to suffer less, which first requires us to understand the cause of suffering, and what real suffering and real happiness are. If you, like Biggie Smalls, have come to understand that having money is not real happiness, that's a big deal! That's something to be grateful for. If we get a chance to meditate or pray today, while on the toilet, upon waking up, while ChatGPT is "thinking" for us, that's something to be thankful for, for sure.

Notice how, when you offer thanks (to the universe, to life, to God, whatever you may believe in), a space in your heart opens up. It might not feel immediately like you suddenly know the real meaning of Divine Love, but it's a little spark of … something.... There's a tiny bit of freedom, of peace, stillness, softness. And as that softness deepens each time, you start to understand what I mean by Divine Love. Many people have written books on this topic (like [A. Helwa's book](https://authorahelwa.com/), Secrets of Divine Love, for example). I hope you are able to experience this when you try it out for a while, and I'd really love to know how it works for you!

### Invite Your Little One In

Once you start to feel that lovely softness of the heart (which can become a very deep field of "perfect freedom"), invite your little one in with you as your spiritual integration practice. They might go numb, or call BS on that. If so, they might just need to see if you're actually humble enough, and grateful enough for them, to listen to their rejection, be patient, and be open to the possibility that your Higher View is missing the trees for the forest. If you instead retreat back into your cozy corner, it's the food bank feast all over again: You can have an enormous spiritual feast upstairs while the child is still starving downstairs.

#### A Heart to Child Loop

With a little warmth to work with, rest one hand at the center of your chest (the heart chakra) and feel for love, and the other on the solar plexus, just below, and feel for your little one. The solar plexus is the most vulnerable part of the torso. One punch there hurts a lot (as I sadly learned while getting beaten up every day at kindergarten recess by a gang organized by my best friend. Can you believe it?), so it makes sense to look for your little one there.

Picture the love traveling from your heart down to your little one. If some comes back up, let it, and send it on down again, so it goes around like a loop. That part's a gift from them, and they don't owe it to you, so if nothing comes back, just keep sending yours. If you don't feel the warmth just yet, rest your hands there and notice what you find, no faking required. Non-judgement is a place to start. It can blossom into love over time. I also enjoy massaging my solar plexus gently.

### When the Spiritual Relationship Hurts

If a spiritual teacher you trusted betrayed you, even sitting down to meditate can feel like it belongs to them now. But if you let an abuser control your appreciation of life, then you've given them an unearned win.

Being the grown-up doesn't mean controlling everything yourself, though. After a betrayal like that, part of you might want to. Spiritual support can help you act where it's yours to act, and let go of outcomes and other people's choices where it isn't. [The Serenity Prayer](https://www.archspm.org/the-serenity-prayer/) is exactly to remember that.

### Make a Simple Vow

If your promises still mean something to your little one, maybe because you've kept a few small ones, a quiet vow can help too. As many real-life parents know, your heart really explodes in such an unexpected and wonderful way when you hold your newborn baby for the first time (happened to me!). And if you're not yet a parent, it can really help to imagine holding a baby in your arms to make the vow feel real. Imagine you just had a baby, how would you feel? Would you look at him/her with adoration and joy? Carry that into visualizing yourself with your own inner child:

"I know you're in there. I love you. You don't have to love me back or trust me yet. I can't promise that nothing painful will happen. Or that I'll always know what to do, or never react badly. But I can promise to tell you the truth and listen when you're angry. I'll make our life together safer where I can, and take the next step I can see to protect you. When I fail, I'll come back to make it right. You don't have to believe these words. Watch what I do."

Notice it only promises what you can keep, so your little one can actually watch you keep it.

Whenever your little one shows up looking for their parent, even if they don't know that's what they are doing, you go back to being the one they can rely on, and you're kind to them while you do it. If they show up to have some innocent fun, don't "put them in their place" as the adult. But sometimes they show up as the one who doesn't want to go to bed. Now you're in my mom's place: you still go to bed, and staying kind about it shows them bedtime isn't a lack of love.

#### Remind Yourself

The vow can live somewhere you'll see it, too, like on a stuffed animal from when you were little, a photo of yourself as a child, or just a sticky note on the bathroom mirror. Whatever helps you feel connected to the Nurturer and Protector you've been practicing. The Teddy bear note may remind you to think, "When I see you, I remember to stop attacking myself and go to bed on time."

A real-life child of yours, or a calm pet, can also act as a companion that brings out the relationship with your own inner child. My son did that for me, and I think that's very common for parents. But those are also their own beings with their own needs as well. Let them come and go without making them responsible for the session.

### How Much Happiness Do You Know Is Possible?

When you wish someone well, what do you have in mind? Probably something like, "I want them to have enough to eat, a safe place to sleep, some people who like them, etc." Wanting this for someone is a form of love (this is the kind babies already understand). It's a good and important thing, this practical love. But the horizon of your wishes may be partly determined by how much happiness/peace/freedom you know to be possible.

Sometimes your little one already feels cared for in those normal ways, but they need help with deeper existential questions. Maybe your life isn't meant to be just something to put up with, something to slog through, a hamster wheel, a rat race. Maybe you could live it with a sense of zest and discovery. What would you need for that?

Maybe you've had some big spiritual experiences, and for a while knew a kind of peace/love etc. that you hadn't known before. Now when you wish someone well, your wishes may be bigger – perhaps much bigger – than they would have been before. You might ["love them in a language they will never learn."](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8o4jvs0s55E&t=3m10s) This is one reason why loving-kindness meditation can seem kind of silly but actually be very powerful. Saying, "May you be happy," over and over won't necessarily zap you into a state of profound love for all beings, but it's one way people discover that there is such a love and learn to expand it.

If you're curious about this bigger love and happiness, follow that curiosity. You don't have to pretend to believe anything, but you might read a few people from different traditions (try a Sufi poet or a Zen teacher). If you know anyone who seems consistently happy in this bigger way, check out how they deal with people when things are going badly. And if they still pass that test, consider, "I don't know if I believe their explanation. But what if the experience is possible for me?" That's what I did, and it was the best move I've ever made!

### The Joy In Sorrow

<!-- PULL QUOTE: in Substack the next paragraph is a pull quote block (Joel, 2026-11-03 23:32 UTC, on F4: "we can also add a 'pull quotes' substack block code to quotes like this") -->

Jelaluddin Rumi wrote: "The most secure place to hide a treasure of gold is some desolate, unnoticed place. Why would anyone hide treasure in plain sight? And so it is said: 'Joy is hidden in sorrow.'"

Another Sufi teacher, Hazrat Inayat Khan, wrote, "God breaks the heart again and again and again until it stays open."

Some people find that deep grieving gives way to feeling connected to something much deeper than what was taken away. Being loved can open grief, too. Someone takes care of you, and you think, "Why does this make me sad?" It can be sadness for the care that was missing.

[As Sister Medhanandi said](https://www.buddhanet.net/joydeath/), "When we are able to come face to face with our own direst fears and vulnerability, when we can step into the unknown with courage and openness, we touch near to the mysteries of this traverse through the human realm to an authentic self-fulfilment. We touch what we fear the most, we transform it, we see the emptiness of it. In that emptiness, all things can abide, all things come to fruition. In this very moment, we can free ourselves."

## The Inner Guide: Becoming Your Own Shepherd

As the Guide, you choose what your little one learns and what it gets to experience, and you hold standards, the way a good parent would. Your parent's harsh tone doesn't come with the job, though.

By the way, this job doesn't have to be called the Guide. The Wise One works, or your Inner Teacher, and "Guru" does for some people.

If you don't feel like the wisest apple on the tree just yet, that's perfectly fine, because we can borrow the guiding function to start with, and you can get better at it day by day. Perhaps your teacher at some age was a great Guide. Ask what the wise person you respect would have you practice today. A wise tradition ("What would Jesus do?" or "What would Buddha do?") can give some direction, too, while you develop your own internal compass.

### Borrow Hindsight

I sometimes ask: "How would I respond to this once I understood it more clearly, in balance and with compassion?" Some moments of silence then allow incredible wisdom and compassion to pour down in moments of grace. At other times, you might just have a slightly better understanding develop slowly.

Your future self probably also knows why you can't pull this off perfectly today. What would they do for you, if they were here? Maybe instead of a magic solution, they'd remind you of what my mom often reminded me: "Everything always works out in the end, somehow!". I thought that was actually pretty funny, but it consoled her. One man's humor is another's wisdom.

You can borrow a wiser future version of whoever else is involved, too, although don't assume they'll definitely end up on your side, or sorry. What the current one keeps doing is still what you go by, but I've definitely felt a lot more patience in arguments when I could imagine the future versions of us saying "that was just a silly misunderstanding!".

### Remember When You Already Did It Well

My father often suggested folks remember a similar-level (or even harder) challenge that they were able to handle. Maybe you survived a car crash and surgery a few years ago, but now you're having trouble with emotional eating. You can draw strength from the past. As my sister, a historian, likes to say, ["The past didn't go anywhere."](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/april-rosenblum-the-past-didn-t-go-anywhere) Your own challenge doesn't need to be as dramatic as a car crash, as long as it really happened. So who or what helped you get through it? Is one piece of that still within reach? If you catch yourself thinking "I survived a car crash, so why can't I stop eating when I'm upset?", that's the past turned into a stick to hit yourself with. Maybe you're more worn out now, or missing whatever helped back then. Getting hit with a stick doesn't solve the problem, unless you're a Zen student. 🙃

### A Healthy Mind Is Not a Perfect Mind

The Guide doesn't need to know everything for sure (that would be an OCD Guide, and then you'd need a Guide for the OCD Guide). It chooses the next sane step that fits your values based on the info available, and that's how it helps your little one grow toward a whole life. Waiting to be sure first is sometimes proper & prudent, but if it's a pattern, it can keep life on hold, and then your little one's world stays small. Sometimes something really is stuck on someone else's end (you've asked, and followed up, and there's nothing left for you to do yet), and waiting on that isn't avoidance. The Guide can let it wait, keeping an eye out for when it frees up, and keep the rest of your life moving, so your little one keeps growing anyway.

Forced adulthood can get into your head too, and it might set the bar too high sometimes. At your grandpa's funeral, some dumb jingle starts up in your head and you nearly laugh during the eulogy. That doesn't mean you didn't love him, or that something's wrong with you. Minds come up with ugly and embarrassing stuff all the time, some of it the opposite of what you believe, and bodies do their own thing too. The Guide doesn't need to police your every thought. It notices the jingle and knows you didn't mean it, and that it isn't who you are. Then it goes with what you'd stand behind, which here is probably squeezing your mom's hand and listening, and lets the jingle go.

I think this is closer to Buddhist practice too. The point is not an impossible performance in which nothing unskillful ever appears. In one discourse (sutta), AN 6.63, the Buddha defines karma in terms of intention (so if you didn't mean to cause harm, your mind is not poisoned). In DN 2, he explains that in the training to purify the mind, one first recognizes a fault, makes amends, and then restrains themself to prevent that kind of thing in future. So it's not like, "you're either pure, or you're not," which is where a lot of folks stay stuck. If you're ashamed about some pattern you have, that might be a signal to give some attention to how you can improve on that pattern. If it's shame just because of an intrusive thought, then it's probably not adaptive, and can be simply let go of.

### You Belong Everywhere and Nowhere

The Guide also points beyond itself, toward somewhere you belong that doesn't ask you to give up being yourself. That might be nature, or existence itself. It might be whatever your tradition sees as ultimate. Love can come from any of these places, and you also belong there. Maybe you've felt that randomly while wandering around outside somewhere, like you didn't need anybody's permission to be there. The way into "the zone" is time in nature and contemplative practice.

With that under you, belonging with people can get a lot less desperate. Your little one might still think, "If they stop liking me, I won't belong anywhere," and the Guide can remind it there's more to belong to than them. And then one day you might have the opposite problem, with people wanting to belong too much to you.

### Timing Is Individual

Most of us need enough Nurturer and Protector first. Otherwise the Guide just overrules your little one in the name of something higher (spiritual bypassing, dressed up as direction). Like a shepherd who's beating sheep to make them move.

Say you had a spiritual opening years before you had much of a Nurturer or Protector. Has your love grown because of it, and has that love reached your little one yet? Look at how you are with people, too, whether you're more compassionate and understanding. Are you getting better at relationships, including telling the truth when it's awkward and doing what you said you'd do?

### Between Spoiling and Clamping Down

Maybe you've let a small child win at Go Fish and never found a good time to start playing for real. Who wants to be the grown-up who finally wins? Without the Guide, you can slide from looking after your little one into spoiling it the same way. Sweets and screens can pass for loving yourself, and so can getting it out of anything hard. Treats and pampering do better at set times, where they can't get out of hand. They shouldn't get canceled as a punishment after a bad day, either.

After all, sometimes "I want it NOW!" is just that: a rung on the desire treadmill. No hidden guard or old trauma required, because all creatures have endless desires naturally anyway, especially when immature. You can hear that want without shame about it. Hearing it doesn't require giving into it, especially if getting your way would hurt you or somebody else.

We also need to remember that many of our desires are covering over deeper desires which we may be afraid to look at. Relationship desires, for example, can be scary because we don't have unilateral ability to satisfy them. Learning to acknowledge the unmet needs without a definite solution is a deep Guide capacity which takes maturity to appreciate.

# Practicing With Your Little One

## Start With Whatever Showed Up

You might go looking for your little one and get mad instead, or realize you've been staring at the rug. I count that as reparenting, too, even if it feels like it's in the way. Before you decide where it's coming from, ask it what it's trying to stop, or what it wants. Maybe it just has something to say. It could be your little one, mad that you took so long to come back, or a part of you that's trying to protect you. Maybe it's that parent you inherited, or something from earlier today, or a bit of each.

You can tell that part something like, "I see you. I'm not going to force my way past you. What's going on, boobaloo?" Or however you'd actually say that. Then wait and listen patiently.

## You Don't Need an Inner Monologue

If you don't hear a little kid talking in your head, don't invent one. Actually, this is one place where the usual inner-child prompt to "ask your little one what they need" can get confusing, because "ask" sounds like you're supposed to ask a question and then wait for a second voice to answer. Maybe that's what happens for some people, but you might not be some people (hehe). If talking into an empty room feels silly, small figures on a table can help (so that you feel even more silly!?), like one for your little one and one for the rest of the gang that shows up. Put whichever one's running the show on a throne. Both ideas come from sections 12.2 and 13.1 of a book called Schema Therapy for Children and Adolescents, which I'll call ST-CA.

You might barely have spontaneous words in your mind and yet still be able to think a sentence on purpose. Try it and see. If words are there, speak or write them. You might feel more comfortable that way than just staying in imagination land.

Then again, maybe what's actually requiring your attention now is a knot in your stomach. Or perhaps you're in a place you want to leave. Sometimes nothing "answers" and you just know what would be nice to do for yourself. And if you don't know, it's fine to guess. Maybe it's some genuine human closeness, or figuring out some way to have more say in your own life.

Life is always switching up on us. You could have a feeling before thoughts, or thoughts might come first and provoke a feeling. They say hindsight is 20/20, but you actually might not even know which happened first.

## Use Childhood Photographs Gently

My friend Cody had trouble imagining his inner child, so he started looking at photos of himself when he was young. That did the trick! If you have the same trouble, see if you can find some childhood photos.

While you look, notice any new feelings or urges, and any tightness or unusual feelings in your body, including the good ones. Use Pema Chödrön's "feather touch" approach to gently bring some light and love to the edges of any contracted feelings. Is there something that younger you seems to want you to understand? Some of it you'll remember yourself, and some of it you might even glean from talking to family/friends from then ("You hated that coat"). It might just be an inference you're guessing from the picture, or perhaps it triggers an image or a dream. Or just nothing comes up. Ask what your little one needs from you now, and do a bit of it.

We don't need to ask it leading questions, like "Who abused me & how?" or "What memory is my body hiding?" Grilling one photo until a story shows up is a good way to get a story, but stories aren't necessarily the truth, and even if they are accurate, the feelings beneath them are still more important. Memory is an amorphous thing, easily influenced by guided imagery and suggestions. [Look at this study, for example](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03196318), where people were shown a doctored photo of a childhood hot-air balloon ride they never took. Half of them actually started sorta remembering it. I'm sure you can relate.

A photo is just one moment that somebody picked and framed (like O.J.😅)... not necessarily representative. Flipping thru an album may give different jolts in your body.

If you start to suspect someone of something serious, like abusing you, please don't confront or accuse anyone based only on what came up here. You might be looking at the photo thinking, "That would explain so much." Journal what came up and how it came up, and see if you can get support from someone level-headed who won't lead you toward an answer. (If the glove doesn't fit, you must acquit!)

Put the photo back in its drawer when you're finished. If it starts tugging at you, so you keep going back to search it or you need to know for sure, or you're getting overwhelmed, take a break from it right then. Settle yourself in the present to close out properly. [Re-evaluation Counseling](https://rc.org), a global movement of peer counseling, always ends on this "Present Time" reminder. Can be as simple and silly as "Oh, Today is Monday, my favorite day of the week!"

## Journal With Each Hand

Lucia Capacchione, an art therapist, wrote a whole book on this one, [The Power of Your Other Hand](https://www.luciac.com/). As titles go, it's pretty literal. Your other hand, the clumsy one, writes like a first grader anyway, so it gets the scared or hurt feelings, as well as some extra creative juice. The usual hand answers, as the grown-up. Some people who get nothing but silence from their little one are surprised by what the other hand comes up with. A protective part might grab the pen first, and then it gets that hand until it's had its say.

For some people, writing might make them feel more confused, especially at first, or nothing might want to be written. Then just keep track for a while, and save the patterns for later, if they turn out to matter at all.

### Which thoughts to talk to

When journaling, you might be surprised sometimes by the myriad things that come into your mind seemingly out of nowhere. Let a stray thought go as just thinking, and come back to the room you're in. A worry that just keeps asking to be reassured can go the same way, unless it's your little one asking.

I wouldn't spend long deciding what to call each part you meet. Every part of you learns from how the others treat it, your little one included. A part of you that's trying to protect you might warn you about something, and when you hear it out, it doesn't have to get any louder.

### If no answer comes

That's fine for now. No need to answer for that part just because you had set this up as a healing session. Even without the answer, you can notice what sets the reaction off, and you can understand what the reaction is propelling you toward, and away from.

### If you do get an answer

An answer could come in different forms, like as a voice or just a hunch. It's often more like a feeling. Maybe grief or anger. If so just listen and be present for it if that's what it wants. How you picture all that is up to you, as long as it helps, and the simpler the better.

### If it tells you to do something

I'd only do it if it's loving and leaves you free. It should make sense, too. Even then, anything big or hard to undo can wait until you're calm and you've checked it against what you actually know.

### If it's anxiety

Anxiety is often a part of you that's trying to protect you, making a guess about what's coming, like that people will laugh at you. Sometimes it's right, sometimes it's useful, sometimes it's not. Anxiety is not necessarily bad by itself, but as they say, the fear of fear is worse than the fear itself. Hearing it out and taking any action that seems legitimately beneficial as a result, often helps it to pass.

## Catch the Hook Before the Story Takes Over

Pema Chödrön calls the tightening and urge you get before the story has finished forming "*shenpa*," and she says "[hooked](https://www.lionsroar.com/how-we-get-hooked-shenpa-and-how-we-get-unhooked/)" is a more descriptive translation for that Tibetan concept than the usual "attachment." You might notice the hook before you have any clue what part of you is doing the hooking. 💃🏿 Or you might notice it embarrassingly late. The jaw and stomach are already tight, and/or half the text is written, and then suddenly it dawns on you: you're supposed to be observing yourself. 🤦‍♂️ Maybe forget the parts detective work for a minute.

When you catch it, look around and just take a moment to enjoy some free honesty: "Oh! I'm hooked again! That's awesome I could notice it!" Take a breath or three before you finish whatever you were doing. Maybe then you'll find there's enough adult around to be nice to your little boo for a second. Or enough Protector to stop something you'd regret later. Or enough Guide to redirect attention to where it's supposed to be.

## Gentle Meditation and Mantra

If the hook is stuck a bit deeper in your mouth and you're flailing around like a fish out of water, gentle mantra meditation or self-hypnosis (which overlap a lot) are some tools that can really calm your nervous system down. A lot of people find that a mantra they feel some heart connection to, repeated in their head or out loud, especially with a nice melody, can lower the walls enough for the heart to open up. It's not something to lean on all day as a way of coping, but used as therapy it can be super effective. I personally love this beautiful Om Mane Pemme Hum mantra that I found in the Garchen Rinpoche documentary, For the Benefit of All Beings:

## What to Make of a Passing Feeling

If what someone did hit something old in you, notice how that feels in your body. And notice how your feelings can also amplify or distort your perceptions of others. Just because your stomach tightened up doesn't mean their boundary crossing indicates "They're evil." You can defer that judgement for when you're in your happy place.

Feelings about yourself work the same way. "I feel attracted" or "I feel ashamed" is real as a feeling, but it doesn't settle who you are, or who you love. Making too much out of something momentary is one side of the human psyche's pendulum. Give yourself time to see what patterns form more durably, and what feels authentically yours vs something planted or manipulated into being there from society, influencers, family, friends, religion, etc., etc.

You may find an exceptional freedom in exactly that in-between place where you don't have to declare your pronouns on social media, or whether you believe in this or that. If you stay in flux forever, though, it can seem like there's nothing solid about you. In that case, sometimes it helps to meditate or talk with a good unbiased therapist who can help you see if there's some avoidance of differentiation (we get deeper into that further on). I myself for a long time was accused of "having no opinions" (which I almost prided myself on🙈), and now it's often the opposite accusation.

The opposite extreme of our pendulum, throwing everything transient away, as some people do with "thought-noting" practice, also isn't healthy, except as a meditation exercise. Transient feelings and thoughts can be meaningful signals. For example, if you don't want to be touched, say no now and work out what it means later.

## One Intention in the Morning, One Review at Night

In the morning, begin the day intentionally, not just because the sun chose to come up once more, since we can't count on that. 🫣 Here's one way you might pl/orkfully start off:

"Today I will neither be Peter Pan nor Captain Hook. I will notice a hook or 2 which lead to unconscious behaviors."

Then at night, pay yourself to be your own therapist so as not to break the bank. Equipoisedly look into how the practice went for you today:

"Where did I notice the hook?" At dinner with the in-laws? Watching animé?

"Where did it still seem to control me despite noticing it?" (That's the most frustrating part, when you know better, and you still act the same.)

Then get practical so it doesn't become another OCD loop: "What small repair or protective act shall I bring in for a proper response to that?"

And don't forget to appreciate the good! Moments of warm, beneficial connection (whether internally from child to adult, socially, spiritually, etc) are all to be celebrated. Were you maybe firm like an elephant today, or courageous like a lion? Adopt the entire spirit animal zoo if need be! Turtles are good if you need impulse control.

Count recognition as progress, regardless whether it came up a bit late, as the goal is to bring the awareness closer and closer to the present. All or nothing from the get go would be cool, but we normally have to learn step by step. If/when you interrupted the pattern, pat yourself on the back, or even kiss yourself on the shoulder. You can renew your intention for tomorrow, getting better & better, every day, in every way, as my Dad used to say. And enjoy your well-earned rest tonight.

## Close the Session and Return to Life

Without an ending, the processing can just start looping. So when you're done, look around and say where you are. Feel your feet on the floor, or the chair under you, and remind yourself about something silly and obvious, like what year Pharaoh Tutankhamun was crowned (1332 BCE in case you forgot!)…

And what's next for you two? Maybe it's making food, or just going to bed. Whatever it is, tell your little one, "That's enough for today. We're going back to the life we're building." Nothing has to be resolved first, and whatever you're still feeling can come along.

# Discovering and Healing Old Patterns

## How Distrust Reproduces

Say you learned somewhere along the way that if you need someone, they end up leaving you. You probably never said it to yourself in words, but it steers you before you know why. But the need for socialization remains a pull, regardless of how unlikely it may be to work. And so we develop various coping strategies, ranging from distracting ourselves from that natural pull with other things (whether harmless hobbies or bingeing on TastyKakes), to people-pleasing, to insisting we don't need anybody (sour grapes). (The TastyKake corporation has asked me to remind you that sometimes a sweet treat is more a celebration than an escape.)

Some people pleasers go along for months as if nothing's wrong while the pressure builds and then they blow up out of nowhere (one of my old patterns). Then they feel so bad about it they go right back to going along to get along. With any of these patterns, I'd ask what it's protecting you from, because it doesn't really help to just label it a bad habit.

The most insidious part of these expectations is that we can get stuck in them even when they no longer make valid predictions about the people in our life. Like my second wife, Bee, who wanted with all her heart to trust me, but set up so many impossible tests that not even Einstein could pass with a perfect score. I tried to explain to her that she was an unwitting prophet.

Have you ever heard of a self-fulfilling prophecy? Perhaps nobody ever hears that you're struggling, since you didn't think they'd want to. Then nobody offers to help, and that can look like proof nobody cares. Keeping quiet might have been the smartest thing you could do as a youngin' with nobody safe to tell, but now, if there are people who are safely available, it can keep help away.

## Shame can make you feel unlovable

Shame comes when you're holding a dark secret. It might be a secret for someone else who abused you, because it made you feel dirty or worthless. Or it might be due to thoughts in your head that bother you. Or it might be from bad patterns of behavior you have. Whatever the cause, finding someone trusted that you can slowly open up to is a magic cure. You still have to take accountability and repair any damage you caused if you did.

Shame becomes toxic when it gets stuck, and you don't know what to do with it. Then you can become ashamed of being ashamed, and on and on. Get curious about what makes it so hard to give up. I went through toxic shame for 12 years. I'm sure it will help you to read my [full guide on shame](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/dont-be-ashamed-of-shame-a-nuanced?utm_source=publication-search). Here's one trick from that article:

### "And So That's the Way It Happened!"

My ex, X, taught me to append "And so that's the way it happened!" to a difficult memory. Say it the way a grandpa would finish a story on the porch. ("Once upon a time…" helps if the start is where you get stuck.) Somehow it moves the whole thing out of the courtroom, where the case was still being argued, and into your life story. Pick a memory you can handle for the first few tries.

## How to Forgive Without Forgetting

Healing does need to get around to some forgiveness eventually, or at least to not being run by the resentment anymore. It can't be put on a schedule. If someone pressures you into forgiving early, what you get is an enlightened-looking act, with the wound gone underground. And you don't have to forgive first to get safe, set boundaries, hold someone accountable, get justice or get better. Then again, active resentment narrows what you see, and your little one stays organized around the injury.

The understanding that helps me is seeing how a person's conditioning, history, pain, and capacity shaped the harmful move they made. Picture everything that had made them who they were by that moment, what happened to them and what they learned, the pain they carried and what they could and couldn't handle. From in there, it was the only move they had.

"Hurt people hurt people, healed people heal people" means that hurt people pass on pain they never worked through, although "externalizers" are the ones who directly hurt others, while "internalizers" try to protect others by holding that "[secret burning thread](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FyZI-VaVg2g)" inside themself, which ends up exploding onto others anyway at times when it gets to be too much to hold. You may have heard this story from somebody telling you to forgive, and it sounded like they must have no clue how deep the betrayal was. When you see it for yourself, though, you might find yourself forgiving them, and if you've held them uniquely to blame, the rage can soften into something more accurate.

They still have to answer for what they did, and it still hurt, even if you see how they got there. It's just that punishing them now can't reach back and give them what they were missing then, the sense, the support, or the skills they needed for a respectable response. What's left is fixing what can be fixed, and making it easier for something different to happen now.

So a boundary can still be appropriate to enforce with them. So can the law, if it comes to that, or telling them plainly it was wrong (or telling yourself, if they'd never hear it). Keeping away from them for good can be right too, and you can still forgive them from over there. Understanding somebody's limitations should make my expectations more realistic, not make those limitations less consequential. "I understand how you became this way" can sit right next to "I now understand why I shouldn't rely on you for this."

### Forgiving Yourself

Forgiving yourself works the same way. Own what you did (only what was really yours), and let yourself feel the remorse. Repair what you can, by apologizing or making amends, and by changing behavior going forward. Then you're allowed to stop holding yourself hostage, and the responsibility stays. If you're thinking "not me, not after what I did," forgiving other people tends to be harder too.

## Using hindsight to "re-do" memories of trauma in a healthier way

Now, once you've got this cast of perfected future characters (from Borrow Hindsight) in your mind, you can also go back to earlier traumatic memories and relive them in a better way. For example, I'll take one instance from my own childhood. I was only spanked one time as a child. I hung up the (expensive) phone call my dad was on with a very important official in another country, because I wanted some attention. I don't think he was exactly furious at me, but he decided this deserved a spanking, even if he didn't agree with spanking in general. Since he wasn't furious, the spanking didn't really hurt, but it hurt me emotionally quite a lot, because I was so extremely shocked that my parents would use physical violence on me. Like I lost my sense of safety with them at that moment. And I may have learned that I was undeserving of attention, that I behaved in ways that made me deserve physical violence, that I was bad, etc.

Many people experienced something like this where the consequences for their little mind were much more severe than anyone would imagine, and in almost every case, the adult themself does not currently understand the extent of the damage until the little one feels safe to tell them. I can't tell you how many people I've worked with have laughed at how brutally they were beaten as kids. Just laughed it off like it was nothing.

Whatever the situation was, we will contrast it with the corrected version. For my friend J., whose dad savagely beat him every time he smiled, I'd have the corrected version be, he smiles, and his dad smiles, happy to see him happy. Ok, that one might seem too easy, although for J, it might take years to imagine that correction.

### How should discipline have been done right?

While often it is obvious how a perfect parent could have responded, in my example, I'm not exactly sure what a perfect father would have done here. But usually what my dad preferred as discipline was a forced "time-in" via a "punishment hug", and I feel like that worked well for me. I did that with my son, Bear, as well. I would hold him as lovingly as possible, until he started to cry, because he did not seem interested in learning any lessons until that point. After he cried for a bit, we talked about what happened and what was wrong with it. When I felt satisfied he was understanding and aligning his values in a genuine way, the time-in was over, although he might want to continue the time-in sometimes a bit longer also, as it began to feel nice after the ego came down.

My sister disagrees that this was therapeutic for her. She thinks it caused her to feel like hugs in general are punishment. A regular time-out could work, but sometimes it has to be enforced somehow, like locking the kid in their room for a bit (and assuming they can't just have fun in their room). Recognizing the difficulty in finding a perfect parental response can also help us. Sometimes there was no perfect response that would have been harmless to all parties. But my sister should probably go back to those memories of punishment hugs and ensure that she learns that hugs are loving, even if that particular hug was also designed to teach a lesson about improving her behavior.

I think that what children really need when they are acting narcissistically is some way to break through to real emotional connection. With very stubborn children (most are at times), that may require induced crying. It didn't always require a punishment hug to do that, either. Just shouting "BEAR!" could make my son cry at times. It's crucial here that the caregiver producing the child's egoic breakdown must have the moral high ground along with compassion, so they aren't seen as just someone abusing their power. And they should be open themselves to being corrected by the child if the child has a valid counter-argument for who is in the right.

Importantly, there is currently no expert consensus on how children should be disciplined, aside from using the least amount of force possible, in the least traumatizing way, whatever that may happen to be. [There isn't even agreement yet if logical consequences work](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jftr.70049). And one thing [Visakan made me consider](https://x.com/visakanv/status/2117162069539913745?a=fKFf5W4rETy6wpTQfwI&s=20) is that the parent-child relationship is the only one that's supposed to change in major ways many times over the years. A parent might be great at a certain phase of child-rearing, and terrible at another (like treating you forever as if you're 5).

## Answering the Strict Disciplinarian

A good routine, like yoga in the morning and a proper bedtime, still feels like lockdown to your little one when it's done in a disciplinarian style. One exercise you can do to handle this is to go back and rewrite your responses to that strict disciplinarian. First, remember who it was that drove you so hard as a child. Then start a little rap sheet of their provoking lines. Comfort your little one before you answer any of it. Your answers can be firm or funny, as long as they aren't mean, since mean is the Critic's department.

The Critic's voice can often pile on, to the point where you can't even understand why or what you're really feeling anymore. You feel sad at first, then the Inner Critic calls your inner child pathetic for being sad, and now you're ashamed and want to hide. You could probably have handled the sadness with some simple nurturing instead from the beginning, but now you have to peel back this other nonsense to get to it.  

# When the Practice Gets Difficult

## When the Adult Voice Feels Fake

A lot of people get stuck right at square one. You call up the Nurturer and try to talk to your little one, and you get silence or a numb feeling, and all of a sudden your phone or the laundry feels urgent. Or some voice says this is silly, and it's hard to tell which part of you is saying it.

My friend Scott said:

"Half the time when I try to connect to baby me, I feel fake and it seems crazy."

I used to be all personal trainer and teach persistence on this as the remedy. Keeping at it does help, since a little one who learned to expect being dropped won't trust you right away. But I don't think that explains the adult feeling fully. More importantly, I think it could even be like unintentional gaslighting to the little one to ask to connect with them when you don't believe they're even real. Like when my mom pretended to listen to me when I was young, but even less excusable (from the inner child's view) because she was not asking for my time when she did that. So, it's worth meditating first on why it feels fake.

### For some people it only stops feeling fake in an altered state

Like on MDMA or deep in meditation. All of a sudden your little one is right there! Oh my gosh! And the love or grief that sounded like therapy talk is just obvious. Old memories might come up too. They can feel totally real, but that alone doesn't prove they happened. I'd still hang on to that connection you felt, even once it's gone, and try to bring a little of it into your normal life. You've felt that your little one is real now, so talking to them afterward isn't just pretending. If you're "lucky", it's not a question of trying to maintain that connection, but trying to figure out how to deal with the rawness of it after the high wears off. That's what integration therapy is for. I have more advice on using psychedelics with the therapy in the "Altered States Can Deepen Therapy or Escapism" section further on.

## Sometimes There Isn't a Clear Child Yet

Reparenting assumes there's a clear little one in there to love, and for a lot of people there is. But if you grew up somewhere you only belonged as long as you played somebody else, like a really controlling family or a strict church, the real one may never have had room to form. All this stuff about your little one could have left you wondering who I'm even talking about.

Maybe you started playing that part so early, and it took over so completely, that "What do I believe?" or "What do I want?" has no clear answer of your own. They sound like such easy questions, too. Back then, asking might have gotten you in trouble, or laughed at. Or there was just no way to ask. That can make "love yourself" feel pretty empty (love what, when the part you've been playing doesn't feel real?), and the authentic self never received the conditions needed to develop. We get deeper into finding and building that self in "Untangling the Belonging Bargains" further on.

## When the Practice Feels Real but Stays Shallow

Some people have the opposite problem, where it feels like they're connecting with their little boo, but then they find out somehow that it was still pretty superficial. Dreams can open up much more emotionally direct stuff, especially in the second half of the night's sleep, if you wake up in the middle to meditate, as I've discovered. That's a good point to ponder over what the dream let you feel that the session didn't, and look at it again in the morning (but write it down so you remember!). And if nightmares start wrecking your sleep, shorten the practice or do it earlier.

## When Healing Turns Into Checking

You can get hooked on healing too. The wound can be completely real while you're checking it for the hundredth time to see whether you've finally grieved it correctly.

Going back to reconnect with your little one is normal parenting/reparenting, but returning to lick the same wound over and over can become a search for certainty in an inherently uncertain existence. Spending all day asking, "Did I process it properly? Did I grieve it enough?" is clearly not what we're after here.

And the checking can take many forms, aside from inner mental checks. A body scan can turn into one, or a confession. Even going on a date, or checking your dating profile matches, can be a version of this if what you're really after is proof that you're good and lovable. It might calm things down for a while, and then the next little wobble matters even more.

If you're feeling guilty because you didn't put your seat belt on, just put it on. If you still want to investigate it later, fine. Perhaps now and then you feel bad about that time you refused to put it on, broke your collar bone in a crash, and then got blamed for not wearing it. That's natural, but doesn't mean there's something new to look at.

## How Would You Know It's Helping?

Don't be afraid to ask your therapist/yourself, "What is this practice supposed to help, and how would I know if it's helping?" This sovereign, gently curious stance should apply to everything in life.

Feeling calmer or having deeper insights or explanations for the trauma response are some possible signs of initial progress, but if that's the endgame, it's just a cope. Real healing is ultimately measured by reduced need for the therapy.

It's like real parenting: you might start out reading parenting books and thinking hard about what to say or do, but after a while you get into the groove and it becomes natural. Inner parenting can be like that too. You don't need to keep checking this guide or planning every conversation with your little one; the nurturing, protecting and guiding become more automatic. You might still turn to therapy when you need it, and life will keep offering chances to grow wiser and more compassionate.

And if the therapy is actually working, it should improve your daily life, and especially the health of the inner child-inner adult relationship. Going at it harder isn't as useful as going at it with more wisdom and compassion. Take the parts that work, and innovate on the rest.

If these exercises start feeling like a ritual you have to complete before you're allowed to live, stop there.

## When to Change the Strategy

Weeks in and nothing much is changing? I'd want to quit too, or else keep doing the exact same thing forever so I never have to find out what's underneath this. Before going with either of those paths, look for what exactly is in the way.

### How many tries would I give it?

It's not like we can just put a number on it. Some things shift in a day. Others take about as long as learning guitar. But do notice if progress is stagnating. If it's making things worse, or you just can't do it right now, I'd change course sooner.

### If you keep following the reparenting steps correctly without getting the expected results

Consider if you have a unique case these steps don't match yet. Maybe you'll find something that works better for you, or you can ask me what I'd do for this case, or ask a therapist you work with. Either way, I'd like to hear about it! Leave a comment here in the article.

### If you'd rather stop

Not wanting to do it anymore might be reason enough, but if that "nope" sounds like a part of you that's trying to protect you, I'd hear it out first to get a better understanding.

## When the Urge to Escape Arrives

The avoidant guard shows up when you get close to some painful memory or insight. Suddenly you'd rather have a drink, check your phone, or do anything aside from directly feeling it (even a yawn can come with an "I'm so done with this" feeling). Maybe this guard doesn't believe there's a grown-up around who could handle what's coming? That yawn might actually be the opposite of checking out, though. Re-evaluation Counseling counts yawning as productive emotional discharge, a feeling on its way out.

I've found many times that simply staying with the feelings in a meditative way is miraculously transformative. ## Physical Pain Makes It Harder

Physical pain can bring on the urge to escape too. Say an attack has you curled up on the bathroom floor, and whatever knocked the pain out last time (say, fent, or ketamine) starts looking real good, though you know it'll leave you worse if you keep resorting to that quick fix.

By now, you may have already been through a million doctors who shrugged their shoulders, or even alternative treatments that were too costly or hard to grade. Sometimes it really is so simple that the best doctors and scanners can't figure it out, like your house is moldy. It is worth going through all the basic checks first that you can do yourself, and trying the things that make most sense. For more on that, check my [detox guide](http://detox.u-dont-exist.com).

And when the symptoms come and go randomly, the best time to get diagnostics done could be right at the actual time of the crisis, rather than waiting until you feel well enough to make an appointment, and they may be able to treat it while it's happening. But what if the attacks come almost every day? You can't move into the ER. And normal test results don't prove the pain is imaginary, just as a treatment that helps doesn't explain what's causing it.

I'd ask for a chronic pain specialist and a written plan for the flare-ups you can see coming, including the new or severe signs that mean you go to the ER right away, but don't let amelioration of symptoms be the end of the investigation.

# Altered States and Intensive Practice

## Altered States Can Deepen Therapy or Escapism

When the physical or mental pain is intense and impenetrable, the urge usually sits on the fence between escapism and doing hardcore battle with the demons. Why not just blast right past that guard part, with [intensive meditation](https://goenka.u-dont-exist.com) or psychedelics? Sometimes that can help tremendously, but both can get you past the guards before there's a grown-up big enough to hold whatever comes through, so I'd hold off until there's enough Nurturer and Protector around. How much is enough depends partly on the medicine and what therapy is done with it. Borrowed is fine, as long as it's from someone steady who won't try to run your trip for you.

I'd learn to do inner-child therapy sober first, in your ordinary mindstate, since that's the one you'll be living in. Cars don't start at 60mph, that's not the way the universe seems to work. First gear leads to second leads to third, etc. Second gear, in this case, may refer to [gentle meditation](http://love.u-dont-exist.com) or self-hypnosis. When you're ready, my [self-hypnosis guide](http://hypnosis.u-dont-exist.com/) and [lucid-dreaming guide](http://lucid.u-dont-exist.com/) can take those deeper, into third gear.

Low doses of entheogens can also synergize compatibly with various gears. Like MDMA, [female iboga](https://iboga.u-dont-exist.com), [Loveyhuasca](https://soma.u-dont-exist.com), certain mushrooms, like Orissa India, and San Pedro cactus (esp. with sublingual mapacho + full spectrum CBD tea) are more protective and nurturing than many other medicines, especially at lower doses, so you don't need as much Nurturer and Protector built up for those, though you still need some. Some people benefit more from solo therapy, while others benefit more from having a trained therapist guide the experience.

## What to Do With a Peak

Now, the beautiful stuff. Say one night (on a psychedelic, in a dream, ten days into a meditation retreat, whatever) you felt more love than you knew was possible. Wonderful, and it matters! Keep it as a reference point, and chase it playfully, because it is already a part of you, and you'll be like a cat chasing its tail, which can be cute and fun, or dizzy and absurd!

Jack Kornfield has a whole book on what comes next, [After the Ecstasy, the Laundry](https://jackkornfield.com/after-the-ecstasy-the-laundry/). The title kind of says it all. That night can't be the bar for every morning after, or regular life starts to feel like failing. And gorgeous as it was, it doesn't mean you're done healing. The laundry will tell you more, and it's the same test as in Timing Is Individual.

One reason the peak is so momentary is because we often don't need to truly give anything up to get it. Say you're still pretty angry, and you want enlightenment, but it has to come with the money and worldly success in one package. Ketamine can get you to a peak anyway. Then, you might now have some kind of transcendental "understanding" glimpse that seems like it could fix everything, if only you could be more aware of it, which often leads into a cycle of taking more and more medicine over time. I'd prefer to ask if any of the discontent has been let go, or the anger? If not, that peak was less the love we're after here than "I'll be grateful once I've got it all," the gratitude with conditions from Ask God for a Loan.

And if your little one still can't take the love in? Hold off on going for bigger, crazier breakthroughs, however tempting it is, or at least know that they are a double-edged sword. Something's in the way, and getting to know it is your next job. Often it's a protective part that's seen love come with strings before. Hear it out first.

## When Escape Leaves Reality Behind

All kinds of psychoactive drugs can mess with your sense of what's real, and that's often the point of them, so you can break free of all your old patterns completely for a bit. But in general, try to select the healthiest ones and use slow and gentle protocols. I'd say one of the most abused psychedelics (aside from ketamine, if you call that a psychedelic) is 5-MeO-DMT. I have a [guide on that](http://bufo.u-dont-exist.com/). People very often believe you need a huge dose of it for a "full release." They go through all kinds of screaming and shaking, nearly killing themselves, with absolutely zero inner-child work being done. Breaking armor down alone is not real healing.

### If a past trip went badly

If you go manic, psychotic (by which I mean losing your sense of reality to the point of posing a risk to yourself or others), get super paranoid, or end up worse afterward than before, those are signs to reconsider trying the same thing again, and try to figure out why that happened. Don't try again until you understand what would make it safe. I'd look at the drug and the dosage, as well as mindset and setting etc. I have a [guide on dealing with bad trips and altered states](https://badtrips.u-dont-exist.com).

### Is your paranoia a warning sign?

Paranoia during a rough trip can be a warning sign for psychosis, but it doesn't automatically lead there, since it can be valid. It tends to go that way when it isn't balanced by practicality. "The CIA is mind-controlling me!" is (mostly) paranoia. "The CIA, marketers, and other forces are conspiring to mind-control me, the way they do to everyone else! But since I can see that, I can prevent it to some extent, just by being aware of how it operates" has a noticeable dollop of paranoia too, but it's practically useful, and less likely to lead to psychosis. "...which means I can prevent it by standing on my head and counting backwards from 33, since 33 is their magic Freemason number!" is leaning psychotic. Some drugs are known for causing paranoia, like THC, amphetamines, PCP, dirty LSD, so I'd avoid those.

### If you might be prone to mania or psychosis

Any of those reactions, from mania to paranoia, can also point to a vulnerability in you, so I'd be careful with other drugs that mess with what's real too. And any predisposition toward mania or psychosis is a warning sign too, whether it's family history, a bad environment or a bad mindset. These are just caution signs, and when you see them, you take more precautions of every kind. You have to figure out what they mean for you, and if you can't, get help from trusted advisors.

### Who counts as trusted

By trusted, I mean someone you know is trustworthy from a long relationship with them, not someone people on Reddit said was super magical and healing. That's called a crapshoot. It might turn out well, it might not. It might seem like it did, and then later you realize you were duped, as happened to me with [Sidney the iboga shaman](http://sidney.u-dont-exist.com/). A new therapist is not as trusted as an old one. As they say, "new friends are silver, but old friends are gold."

### If you keep coming back from trips confused or spaced out

Maybe nothing worse happens, but after several trips like that I'd be concerned. If you're having episodes when you're sober, or any of them are severe, see a psychiatrist.

### Before you quit a medication or alcohol

Don't suddenly stop a medication just because you suspect it's involved. Talk to the doctor who prescribed it. Benzos can be dangerous to quit cold turkey. Alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous if you're physically dependent.

### What you mix

Be careful what you mix, too, and not just prescribed medicines. Even some basic things like cinnamon can be dangerous (and helpful) with ayahuasca, and doctors & AI don't know about a lot of this. I also learned that mixing acacia confusa with mimosa hostilis is very destabilizing and challenging, but excellent if you want to know how it feels to be painfully autistic + schizo! I have a [list of things to be cautious about with ayahuasca](http://ayasafety.u-dont-exist.com/) in particular.

# Bringing Reparenting Into Real Life

Reparenting is usually talked about as something you do alone, or with a therapist. But your little one got hurt by people that weren't either of those, so some of the healing has to happen with real life other people too. If you're presently lucky to have some support, it might be mostly one person carrying a lot of it. Maybe you're picturing them right now. Having someone like that while you're struggling can be an enormous blessing. If it's more distributed, with various people you can count on for small parts, that can be more sustainable, even if it doesn't feel as deep.

Over time, reparenting can help you take in some of what they've been doing for you, and start doing it yourself. You'll find the balance is dynamic between inner & outer reliance, and going too far in either extreme isn't generally the healthiest.

## Rediscover Play and Your Own Preferences

### Play Is Part of the Work

Your little one wants to play, too, which is why I call the deeper therapy pl/ork. Many times we adults see things as work that a child would see as play, like yoga, for example. So if it feels like you're enforcing everything as a discipline, see if you can change that discipline into a game. Personally, I can really get a lot of enjoyment when I feel those lovely stretches without thinking it's another task. A lot of the best discoveries come when you're both just messing around, kind of like how kids tell you what's really going on from the back seat, when nobody's looking right at them. Play not only paves the way for therapy, but is a part of how successful therapy should be measured.

A lot of what adults tend to lose, your little one still has, like the silliness and the irreverence, the rawness, the creativity, all that, and maybe a connection to the Great Mystery from before your karma ran over my dogma 🤪. That's the spark of life they bring, and you get to draw on it and give back, so it's more communion than command. For one example of the spark, there's my post [Treasuring the Inner Child's Gift](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/treasuring-the-inner-childs-gift).

### From Survival to Experimental Play

If your childhood meant avoiding pain and keeping the people you needed close, your attention got used up reading danger (was that sigh about me?) and meeting their expectations, and when curiosity or mistakes got you punished, or it felt like they loved you less, you got better at playing the "chameleon".

So early on, the practice is finding out who's actually in there, through long reflection with no outside input, and journaling just for yourself, without picturing a reader. ### Let the Child Be Bad at Things

Reflection can show you what got sMothered/suppressed. For identity you also need some experiments out in real life, so go try something new just for you. Maybe a foraging group, or a painting class. Stargazing can be awe-inspiring, and you might even see a UFO! Notice what holds your attention when nobody is impressed.

The old parent in your head may demand immediate talent, though, and then the first time you're awkward at it, that's "evidence" that you're stupid or clumsy. Or you hear, "Come on, you're not really the type." I think your little one is listening to see if anybody's going to stick up for them. The grown-up you're building has to be the one who does: "It's okay if we're bad at this, nobody's on trial. We're just seeing if we like it. And it's ok to be great at it also!"

Give a new interest enough tries to get past the humiliation of being a beginner, when you keep telling people, "Sorry, I'm new." Maybe after that you still genuinely dislike it, and then you may quit. You may also discover a part of yourself that never had permission to exist.

## Differentiation: Keeping Your Own Ground

### Untangling the Belonging Bargains

Part of building who you are is untangling yourself from whatever pulls you away from your own knowing in return for belonging. A political tribe might expect consistent positions and predictable reactions, while a family or religion that's kind to you might have expectations that just feel like being good. Your family might want a particular kind of son or daughter, and your friends might have rules about what you can joke about or get upset about. But if going along means betraying yourself, that's the bargain. It can help to write down the rules your family ran on, including unspoken ones like "Boys don't cry." Then write down the role you got handed, like the stand-in parent, or the one who got blamed for everything (ST-CA has lists of both, in sections 15.2.3 and 15.2.4).

Then there's another bargain, where you have to earn your worth: when you don't feel your value inside as a human being, you can become a "human doer," working for it in some arena outside yourself. That might be beauty, athleticism, sexual desirability, intelligence or grades. For some people it's money or status, and for others it's a sophisticated spiritual identity or becoming indispensable to everybody else's problems. It can feel really good to be the one everybody calls. In the more shadowy presentation, when you really need to feel needed, you might be like the firefighters who become arsonists to keep themselves employed.

Even with good parents, you could have learned unhealthy self-valuation when people repeatedly praised what you did rather than your inherent worth. That wasn't your flaw.

#### Four Pairs That Commonly Hook the Child

Pema Chödrön has a quick map for finding the bargain. Your little one can panic when something hurts, and also when something good might disappear, like praise, romance, money, status, belonging or a spiritual high. You might still be enjoying one of those when your little one starts asking how long this is going to last.

When that happens, you can ask yourself these, once you've caught your breath:

- What do I think I'm about to get, or lose?
- Whose approval suddenly feels like I can't live without it? It isn't always who you'd guess.
- Is this a value I'm standing up for, or a high I'm trying to keep?
- Would this still be something I'd feel is worth doing if at the end of the day, I didn't get praise from anyone for it?

You don't have to stop enjoying pleasure, praise, gain or belonging, as long as they don't define you.

### Practicing Differentiation

Differentiation can happen right inside a family, religion, community or relationship. You'd still belong, but now you come first and belonging second. As a differentiated adult you can stay deeply embedded, free to question things or disagree. If a parent used to go quiet and hurt whenever you disagreed, that might be hard to believe. A family where everyone gets to be their own person is healthier than an enmeshed one, and a faith that's your own is healthier than religious compliance. It can be the same religion either way. And two differentiated people have a sturdier relationship than two who've been absorbed into each other.

As you tell the part you've been playing apart from the authentic self that didn't get much practice, notice whether it makes you more curious about both, and better able to relate to them. Or does it have you thinking, "That chameleon's fake, and I'm getting rid of it"? Keep in mind the chameleon was a skillset that somehow worked to help you fit in. Even if you're not planning on playing James Bond anytime soon, you still might need to smooth your edges out sometimes as part of socializing. You can't always disagree with everyone on everything and still expect to be enjoyable/enjoy company.

Day to day, differentiation looks something like this:

- Caring about somebody's feelings without automatically adopting their opinions or sacrificing your own ground. Their reasons can still change your mind, though.
- Noticing when you agreed in the moment and disagree later: "Wait, why was I nodding along?"
- Noticing when what you thought you knew suddenly feels like water dripping through your hand because of praise or pity, fear or attraction, or something that really resonates with you.
- Disappointing people and withstanding social pressure, and still being recognizably yourself wherever you are (maybe louder in some places). That one can take a while.
- Questioning what you were programmed to believe while holding the discomfort, and without having to stop loving anyone over it.

Key developed this identity and differentiation portion of the method, drawing on Murray Bowen's family-systems work and her own self-therapy.

### When Being Happy Feels Like Abandoning Someone

Some families run on a bargain like "If I'm happy while someone I love is suffering, I'm abandoning them."

That's exactly why I was crying for 14 years. Beginning at the age of 12, when I really started to understand that life is brutal for most people, I became convinced that being happy myself, while others weren't, was selfish. I had this poster in my room that really resonated with me, by the famous socialist, Eugene V. Debs. I told this to a therapist in college and she actually agreed it was a good reason to be depressed!

What I found, once I started cultivating my own happiness, was that I got a lot better at helping other people toward theirs. Do your part for people who are suffering, and skip the part where you have to be miserable too.

Some friends or family really might not like it when you get happier, though. Someone might pull away, or keep dragging you back into the old misery. I'd take that loss seriously, and look at what they do and what you owe them, before deciding it's envy.

I'd still try a small good thing, like a nice meal, and see what actually happens.

## Finding People and Building Trust

### People? Where Are the People?

I can't tell you where to look for people who fit you, but I'd bet on more than one place. Keep the good people you've already got. Then maybe try a class, or a spiritual community if you find one that fits you. And if any group, or any one person, starts acting like they should be your only support, that's when we ask the Protector to start paying attention.

### First Social Connections, Done Right

Then there are people you're just getting to know. Meeting them can turn into a kind of test--"First impressions are everything," as they say, but it could be even worse than that, if you need them to prove you're lovable.

Some people try to make themselves as lovable as possible, hiding their real feelings to do so. Others have no filter, on purpose, to see if they are still loved and appreciated. Like my one friend who used to go into churches and tell pastors they were wicked or unfit for their jobs, and then he wondered why he didn't feel the love those churches promise (which was more proof he was right about them to begin with). He's not wrong to speak his mind, but I wonder whether his goal is to reach mutual understanding, to vent, or to re-try dealing with some traumatic childhood pattern of losing himself to fit in with the gang.

Neither of those approaches are the balance we're seeking, and both are treating the other person like they're your reassurance machine. I'm giving you permission now to quit school and flunk the tests. Instead, get curious about who this person is, and who you are, and let both of you have your own reasons for being there in the beautiful present. Leaning on each other is still a big part of relationships, and it goes both ways.

If you find yourself getting strange reactions from people all the time, check what your expectations were about how they'd react.

### Opening Up Honestly to the Right People at the Right Pace

And reaching out doesn't mean telling strangers everything. Privacy isn't fakery. People can earn more of you by how they handle the smaller stuff, like whether that thing you told them about your weird fear of birds turns into a joke at the next party 🙈, and how they take it when you say no. But if a relationship is getting deep and you're still keeping up a fake normal, happy version of yourself to hold onto it, I'd either bring more of the truth in, carefully, or let it be less deep for now. (If telling could put you in real danger, like violence, or being outed where that isn't safe, protect yourself first.) And when someone finally accepts you, it can feel amazing. It still doesn't prove you're meant for each other, or that they've earned your trust yet.

#### Before You Tell the Hardest Stuff

Then there's the stuff that's hardest to tell anyone, and of course that's exactly what you need help with. Maybe it's an urge that scares you, or something you did that could get you in legal trouble. I'd assume that what you tell won't be completely confidential, especially if it sounds like something dangerous.

You know that DM you deleted? It can still be sitting on their phone as a screenshot 🙈, and whatever's on a company's server can get handed over if a court asks. Just because Mr. Boopaloopa was your name on WhatsApp doesn't guarantee you're anonymous, by the way.

And some of the people you'd tell, like therapists, teachers, and doctors, can be required to report certain things (a few laws cover everybody, too). Which things depends on where you live, and it's nowhere near every crime you might mention. The cops are hopefully the obvious ones to be careful with, even if they pretend to care.

So I'd ask this person, before handing over any tricky specifics: "What does the law require you to report?" That's a normal and expected question. Although personally I wouldn't even bother with that, because whatever they tell you today, the law can change tomorrow.

You can always rephrase things as a dream narrative to get the emotional content across without triggering alarm bells (although I actually got in trouble with TalkSpace therapy bot even for a dream!). Just don't hold back a danger that's happening now from people who can help, and get anyone in danger safe before worrying about the rest. Sometimes telling on yourself is the thing that keeps someone safe, whatever it costs you legally.

### Ask What Kind of Company They Want

Before giving anyone advice, your little one included, it can help to find out what kind of company they want: "Do you want me to mostly listen, help you untangle this, brainstorm, or...?" Not that you have to make a ritual of asking every time, if it's obvious what they want. But as they say, "assume" makes an "ass" out of "u" and "me."

### Hearthwork: Taking Turns

The basic premise of [Hearthwork](https://hearthwork.u-dont-exist.com), my peer-counseling framework, is: Equal time. Attention without interpretation, advice, or fixing. You speak. I listen. Then we switch. Presence, Warmth, Discipline, Confidentiality. It's kind of a third type of relationship, alongside ordinary friendship and professional therapy, and one your little one might really need, because modern life rarely gives you anything like it.

Some of what the three grown-up jobs do can come from outside you, too. When you take turns listening with a good peer, as equals, their warmth and the way they stay with you lend you the Nurturer for a while. Keeping what you say private, and stopping when the time's up, lends you the Protector. If you say no, they don't push. But they don't act as your Guide, or decide what your experience means. Maybe they're thinking, "Oh, I know what this is about," or "Here's what you should do." They keep it to themselves. A question from them at the right moment can help you hear your own Guide, and what you answer is up to you. Then you trade places, and it's your turn to keep "Here's what you should do" to yourself.

The peer doesn't become your permanent parent, though. They show you a capacity, help you get the hang of it, and give it back to you, which is borrowed adulthood in its healthiest form. Is that therapy? Well, not the mainstream kind, but yes, for sure. Professional therapy's still useful for what it does, and peers and community do something else, so a serious healing path can include both.

### Dating While You Heal

Maybe you'd rather that your support came from a lover, as well. Lots of people open a dating app in the middle of the night, hoping someone will make it all stop hurting. 🙈 If that's you right now, dating can wait while your friends and the steady people around you do their part. That doesn't mean you have to be completely healed before you date, but romance should follow when two people really love each other and want to build a life together. It won't rescue you from the pain or reward you for healing, and it sucks to mess up a beautiful relationship because of unhealed trauma, believe me, I know. I wrote a whole [romance guide](http://romance.u-dont-exist.com), mostly due to this.

## Speaking Up and Handling Conflict

It's often in handling conflict that we see the real inner child jump out. So if you've been wondering where they are in a session, here you go, let's pl/ork!

### If Someone Has Actually Hurt or Threatened You

Even once, whether or not it brings up old stuff, get safe first, which is not a small thing at all, I know.

### Speak Toward What You Are Building

It's not so hard to understand that words have power. They point your attention somewhere, so aim them at what you want more of, whether that's something you'd like to be able to do or a relationship. Say you tell yourself "I'm not dependent." Well, now you're thinking about being dependent, just like that Pink Elephant. Being totally independent can even seem like just a reaction to the original problem. We can often strike a nice nuanced poetic balance, like: "I'm provided for by the universe, like the birds & lilies Jesus reminded us of." With your little one, and in hypnosis especially, make it easy to picture: warmth, protection, steadiness, rest, whatever's really needed to melt into your happy place. You'll save a ton of money on vacations!

But let's also make sure we don't hide from shadow stuff with toxic positivity. Grief is natural, and letting tears flow can be immensely therapeutic, as long as they don't hijack your perspective with a cemented story of what will always be true. If your little one says "he hurt me," we honestly label that as harm, even if it was also a learning experience. Same for anger, a "No," who's responsible, all that. Then once the difficult experience is acknowledged by the Nurturer, the Protector & Guide can lead the way up to more safety and maturity.

### Being Your Own Advocate With Doctors

No need to give them a good impression if that means not being your own advocate. If you don't feel up to it, bring someone you trust to speak up for you (like, not that relative who never respected your wishes), and ask what a treatment's for.

### Write It. Don't Send It Yet.

If you've got something burning you up that you wanna say, write it out as a reply. There. That got it out. Ahh. Breathing easier. 😌 But don't send it. Take that breather you earned and then come back to it from a cooler place.

It doesn't mean you need to hide your upset, or apply for a permit to protest, but choose your battles and execute them wisely, so you don't end up regretting how you communicated later. As Lao Tsu might have said (if I could ever have read that whole boring Art of War book), a war with no casualties is the best possible war. When high IQ & EQ communication hits a writer's block, sometimes the best thing is to simply say, "I need some time to think about my response." And if the conflict really requires a response right now, then obviously just do your best rather than wait for the perfect timing.

Be honest with yourself about what your goal is for this communication. Do you want to break someone mighty down? To show that you or your views matter? Or do you want them to listen and hear you without getting defensive? Sometimes folks are shocked at the response they get (why does everyone always reject me?), without even considering whether that response was easily predictable based on the energy they gave out to spark it.

This is where I turn inward and talk to my writer, which is often my inner child when emotions pop up. It's like I'm holding his hand and showing him a better way, not like I just stole his pencil because he wrote something embarrassing. So what was he actually trying to say? A lot of the time it's just "that hurt."

Other times it's "I did this too." My friend Tyler saw a guy smoking in front of a baby and asked him if he thought that was wise. The guy asked if he thought it was wise to mind his own business, and Tyler said, "Well, I believe in Christ, so I thought you might care about life too." Afterward he wasn't sure he'd handled it right, or whether he should have thrown the first stone at all, since he'd smoked in front of a baby himself.

But Jesus also said to take the log out of your own eye first, and then "[you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's eye](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%207%3A3-5&version=ESV)." That's exactly what made Tyler the right person to say something. If you actually have the problem they have, and you want to call them out for it, call yourself out for it instead. What we came up with was something like, "Hey man, when I was younger I smoked in front of an infant, not realizing how selfish that was, and I notice you doing the same thing. I'd ask you to please have some compassion for him. I have a young child now, so this really touches me." Tyler's an evangelical Christian who preaches on YouTube, so the fact that he really appreciated this take on Jesus' message is pretty salient to me.

It's also how your voice sounds, though. You can say all the right words, but if the tone is accusatory, it doesn't matter.

Before sending anything, I'd read it once as if I were them, sitting there with my phone. Stuff I wrote while I was scared can sound like a threat to them.

### If You Really Did Hurt Them

Repair it. Easier said than done...

Are you sorry for what you did, or are you starting to take back the complaint too because they're still upset? Maybe your first explanation sucked. Try again. 😅 And if it fails again, and you've said what you mean, well, you can't guarantee they'll understand it. Guilt may keep shouting at you. That doesn't tell you the whole conflict was your fault, nor does it prove they're manipulating you.

### Other Folks Also Have Hooks

Once you're in your happy place, look at the other person too, and don't go only by the one fight where they crossed your boundary, or, the other way around, by the one "no" or boundary of theirs that made you mad – after all, they're allowed to say no to you. Does it keep ending the same way, where you bring up something that hurt and then still end up reassuring them they're not a bad person, and the talk never comes back to what happened to you? (The psychologist [Lindsay Gibson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlNpgFWOLPw) wrote the book on this: [Disentangling from Emotionally Immature People](https://www.newharbinger.com/9781648481512/disentangling-from-emotionally-immature-people/).) Or can they hear you disagree and look at their own part?

If it's the first, it can seem like you haven't explained yourself well enough yet. You probably have, and it makes more sense to count on what they keep showing you they can do than on the right words finally getting through to them. Maybe what they can do is text you something kind the next day and not bring any of it up.

#### Can They Change?

You may not know for certain whether the other person is or could be capable of a healthier response, and neither do they. If they promise to change, that's probably as useful as their prior promises were. Think about how long it has taken you to become more healthy. Could you have done that from one moment to the next based on a promise? Sometimes we can be a better support for someone when we maintain a realistic boundary, so that we can hold high expectations, and still feel prepared for their failure to meet them.

#### If a Relationship Keeps Making You Feel Less Safe

Meditate on why it makes you feel unsafe, and what keeps you in it. Might it be due to a lack of the healed inner child/inner adult relationship? (If so, you're reading the right guide!)

### Taking a Break From a Family, Group, or Relationship

Differentiation is slow, and it goes on your whole life. Sometimes you need to take a break from a family, group or relationship, even if they aren't doing anything to you. Simply being around them may pull you into that programmed space where listening to what you really want becomes harder. Some people come back later on different terms, and sometimes the break has to be long-term because the pattern of losing yourself with them is deeply entrenched. As you differentiate, you learn to tell which of those apply.

## When One Life Has Ended and the Next Hasn't Begun

Your little one can get really loud in the gap after one (figurative) life ends and before the next one has formed. A breakup will do it, or moving to another country, an illness, losing your faith or a role, or your youngest real child moving out. Maybe you've left a family or community, maybe you've stayed on new terms. A therapist or helper might step back because you're doing so much better, and suddenly your little one is wailing. For a while the old you is gone and the new you hasn't shown up yet. Tibetan Buddhism calls an in-between state like that a *bardo*.

Your little one might be sure everything's over. Usually one real thing ended, and the related habits might be held onto like on autopilot. Ask your little one what they're scared will happen in the bardo between past and future (worst-case scenarios are kind of their specialty).

Something basic can mark the ending, like boxing up the old keys. Nobody needs to know what door will open next. But when the not-knowing feels too heavy, a new identity can sure look tempting. Like a borrowed coat that doesn't fit but at least it's warm. But you can't go far with a borrowed coat anyway, so don't force the future on. Keep taking care of yourself, and grieve what ended with your little one beside you during the metamorphosis.

### Missing Someone

When you miss someone, let the ache be a cue for love. It doesn't have to become one more problem to solve, and pushing it away won't help. If love's there, "I miss you" becomes "I love you." Or there's the loving-kindness wish: "May you be loving, peaceful, and free." You can picture them. Or don't, and just feel the care going their way.

All of this happens in you, so it doesn't matter whether they get any of it, because the main benefit is for your little one. I have an [*N of 1 case study that proves this works, also*](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/i-ate-loveyhuasca-every-day-for-90). It's what rescued me from deep despair when the love of my life, my second wife, Bee, left me.

## The Love Has to Move

Community can come before the big opening or after it, too. Sometimes loving people give somebody enough safety and belonging that a deeper opening becomes possible. Other times it's the other way around. Somebody has a big opening first, a love or a meaning they couldn't have imagined before, and then they start to "manifest" different kinds of people and relationships in their life. Either way, the community is what helps make it practical, real, down to earth, embodied and so on.

This is part of why I connect inner-child pl/ork with [Hearthwork](https://hearthwork.u-dont-exist.com) and with [building community](https://community.u-dont-exist.com). Having a beautiful experience on the inside isn't really the point. The love has to move, and it doesn't always go the same way round (the door you came in through matters less than the loop). From a spiritual experience to your little one, then into your relationship with the grown-up you, then out to other people, and back again.

You want someone who won't tell you what yours really mean, downplay them, force them, or treat them as a symptom. I'd also want one who doesn't put ideas in your head, and who understands dissociation. What have they found too challenging to deal with so far, and where have they seen the best results? A good therapist won't charge you an arm and a leg just for this kind of interview, and they should already have this information on their website to avoid wasting too much time. A certificate on the wall can't tell you any of that.

That doesn't mean you can't get a lot of great insights sometimes when talking to AI, but just expect it to go sideways sometimes, and don't let it replace real humans who love you and want to think with you. Actually, I find one of the best uses for it is to improve my interactions with real people. For example, it can help me formulate more careful replies to people who are too sensitive to take my excessive bluntness or wry humor (although I wouldn't send them actual AI text).

### Schema Therapy

Of the scientifically studied clinical approaches I know, Schema Therapy, developed in the 1980s-1990s by Jeffrey E. Young, is the closest to this guide. Its therapists do what they call [limited reparenting](https://schematherapysociety.org/Limited-Reparenting): within professional limits, they give you some of what a good parent would, with warmth and firmness and some playfulness too. Over time you take that in, and it becomes your own "Healthy Adult," who looks after your "Vulnerable Child" and stands up to the punishing parent in your head. The healthy adult recognizes when a need differs from the strategy you use to meet it, and it helps you set limits. And it gets on with [adult life](https://schematherapy.com/id72.htm), like work and the things you've committed to. All that sounds like my Nurturer, Protector and Guide by other names, and since the therapist's version becomes yours bit by bit, it's borrowed adulthood too.

Being all scientific and stuff does have its drawbacks also. Schema Therapy's name pretty much tells you all you need to know. What the heck is a Schema anyway? It's normally described as a "pervasive pattern of thinking, feeling, and behaving" (learned in childhood). Although I find a slightly different definition of it [from Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/schema-therapy): "A schema is a long-lasting outlook or assumption that you have about yourself, others or the world." But yeah, put those together and that's basically what it's talking about. So the schema therapist takes answers from a questionnaire you fill out and maps your difficulties onto a set list of 18 schemas, which themselves map onto a set of modes, like the Vulnerable Child or the Punitive Parent. Based on those schemas and modes, the therapist assumes some things about your childhood that would explain them.

Where it really differs is spirituality. Schema therapy doesn't build it in, though the preface to its main textbook calls blending it with meditation or religious practice "an important development." That's not so surprising, since it grew out of cognitive therapy. Some people do blend them, though.

Tara Bennett-Goleman's book Emotional Alchemy, for one, fits a Buddhist loving-kindness wish to each schema. Her book barely mentions gratitude, and for me that's the door to the bigger love back in Your Loan is PRE-APPROVED! IFS goes overboard the other way, in my opinion (more on that in the next section).

Schema Therapy takes a more combative stance with the inner critic and guards than IFS or my approach, possibly a result of the lack of spirituality in it.

### Internal Family Systems (IFS)

Richard Schwartz, who developed IFS in the 1980s, called one of his books *[No Bad Parts](https://ifs-institute.com/store/419).* Honor, understand and love every part, he says, and the protective ones are no exception, so "meet the protector first" would fit right in over there, although I focus more on keeping the inner child safe first. The one exception is that IFS does understand some parts may be entity attachments ("unattached burdens") which need to be exorcised. My guide also talks about entity attachments, but I don't believe that's reason to start another war. Rather, I explain the root cause of such attachments as lack of protective love from the inner adult.

My reparenting method diverges from IFS at another important point. The IFS Institute's [outline of the model](https://ifs-institute.com/resources/articles/internal-family-systems-model-outline) says "everyone has a Self," (a divine True Self, from Advaita) and that the Self "can and should lead," once your parts stop "blending" with it. I emphasize the inner adult you borrow, build and practice occupying that has the roles of: Nurturer, Protector, and Guide.

Is a perfect Higher/True Self already waiting in there to be your guide or parent? I'd call that claim [spiritual bypassing](https://bypassing.u-dont-exist.com). I've experienced that Higher Self perspective myself, though, in very powerful meditations. For example, one time, while still married to my first wife Ann, who was becoming more violent and crazy as time went on, we got super high on weed and we were both in some higher realm as our higher selves. My higher self asked her higher self, "Are you going to keep getting more and more crazy?" Her higher self said, "Yes." It was like we could see the whole timeline of life from above. Although, my friends already told me the same thing, quite sober. 😅

So, there is a less subjective way of seeing things as perfectly ordained in reality, despite the ego's illusions, which matches what others say about it. As a Buddhist, I no longer see it as "Self", though it can still give the same love, peace, and clarity to whomever accesses it. Where I part ways with IFS is I don't think the Higher Self has any *specific* wisdom that can be followed mechanically. From that higher vantage point, problems can also look like they were never problems at all, and that can ironically lead instead to more foolish indifference.

You can see both sides of this see-saw in the guru types who supposedly embody their Higher Self. [Anthony de Mello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_de_Mello_(Jesuit_priest)) is an excellent example. He was a Jesuit priest who mixed stories from all kinds of traditions into his teaching on seeing through the ego's traps and coming back to the Perfect Present. He also had a background as a psychotherapist who understood trauma-informed healing methods. He seemed to drift between the two opposite paradigms in a way that contradicted itself. And that bizarre contradiction is itself cited as "crazy wisdom" by apologists.

I'm also not a fan of the strange and complex cast of characters in IFS. IFS splits up my "inner child" into an indefinite number of wounded young parts called "exiles." I find that pathologizing. I prefer to speak of a child who needs parenting and integration, rather than naming them by their wounded or excluded condition. And the parts just keep coming: managers, firefighters, exiles, polarized parts, legacy burdens, Self-energy, guides, new subparts... At some point it's a maze, and as my friend Pink, who has been healing her Dissociative Identity Disorder, explained, while it can be useful to have validation of the parts that one experiences, and ideas for how to work with them, it can also get overwhelming. People who are already overwhelmed can thus become more split up than before. I've seen many others echo this same evaluation, and it makes sense to me.
