Untangling the Belonging Bargains

Part of building who you are is untangling yourself from whatever pulls you away from your own knowing in return for belonging. A political tribe might expect consistent positions and predictable reactions, while a family or religion that's kind to you might have expectations that just feel like being good. Most groups expect some conformity. Your family might want a particular kind of son or daughter, and your friends might have rules about what you can joke about or get upset about. But if going along means betraying yourself, that's the bargain. It can help to write down the rules your family ran on, including unspoken ones like "Boys don't cry." Then write down the role you got handed, like the stand-in parent, or the one who got blamed for everything (ST-CA has lists of both, in sections 15.2.3 and 15.2.4).

Then there's another bargain, where you have to earn your worth: when you don't feel your value inside as a human being, you can become a "human doer," working for it in some arena outside yourself. That might be beauty, athleticism, sexual desirability, intelligence or grades. For some people it's money or status, and for others it's a sophisticated spiritual identity or becoming indispensable to everybody else's problems. It can feel really good to be the one everybody calls. In the more shadowy presentation, when you really need to feel needed, you might be like the firefighters who become arsonists to keep themselves employed.

Even with good parents, you could have learned unhealthy self-valuation when people repeatedly praised what you did rather than your inherent worth. That wasn't your flaw. In the process of self-differentiation, we learn to notice when we're making the trade, and we begin keeping our ground more when feasible.
