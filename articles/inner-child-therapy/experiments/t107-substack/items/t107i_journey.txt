My Journey

I used to preach Buddhist enlightenment too much, because that's what helped me. But an old childhood wound could still hit, like if someone accuses me of being lazy, and then suddenly it's like I'm that same little kid again, even tho the spiritual part of me felt free as a bird.

From what I've seen, most people (like me) who chase spiritual freedom, as valid as that is, are, at least at first, trying to get away from something painful in themselves, usually childhood trauma of some kind. That’s spiritual bypassing. Plenty of gurus do it too; I’ve written about the Dalai Lama as one example.

The way I think this works is simple. Imagine you’re broke and starving, and your baby is starving too. Understandable: there is no food. Then a food bank opens nearby and suddenly you’ve got lots of nutritious munchies. But you’re eating all of it yourself while baby you is still starving. Unforgivable.

Nobody leaves a baby starving on purpose. I just didn’t believe there was a baby. Inner-child stuff sounded silly to me until I could actually feel him. Then there was this little me actually hurting, and I could send some of the love I’d already found his way.

Spending your whole life processing your wounded inner child isn’t freedom either. Spirituality opened a much bigger horizon for me—love and happiness beyond the conditions of my life story. Maybe ordinary life already gives you enough love and meaning. Great. It didn’t for me. Conditional love felt radically insufficient, so I kept looking for something deeper.

I was well-loved as a child, but from age twelve to twenty-six I was depressed and cried every night from feeling the pain of the world. Loving-kindness (metta) meditation healed that depression almost instantly for me, so it became my regular practice automatically.

I also found more love in the rest of the Buddhist teachings—which, it turns out, also include inner-child self-love! and later in loveyhuasca.

But it didn’t reach everything. A lot of the child stuff around my attachment to women was still there. When I later tried self-directed inner-child therapy, it felt like I was making it up at first. That didn’t stop me from practicing, but I did stop “pushing” it to be a certain way, and I’d stop trying to feel it was realer than it was. I would go more into listening and waiting mode rather than imagining mode.

At one pivotal point, I used San Pedro cactus and CBD tea with sublingual mapacho, and that cracked it open.

Years later, I found a warm, intuitive inner child therapist, named Daja, randomly, while in Kenya. I figured I already knew this stuff, but I gave it a shot. She did actually help me see some things more clearly. For example, she had me write down how many people I could fully trust as a child (lots), then how many people I could fully trust as an adult (zero). That was difficult, and it’s actually the opposite of what you might expect from inner-child therapy, since usually that’s for people who had problems trusting as a child.

What it showed me is that I was ignoring a lot of things that were really important to look at, and that was one of them. I wouldn’t have even thought to ask the question she asked me if I were doing it on my own. It’s also an example of how therapy can sometimes make you feel worse than before, when you become more aware of problems. But personally I prefer to see things as they are, so I can figure out how to work with them, rather than just hide in delusions.

It also showed me that I really do need to count more on myself than I assumed. I need to be much better as an inner adult for my inner child, because my childhood was actually so nice that I kind of just assume I can trust everyone, and that gets me into trouble. So it got me curious to learn more about inner-child therapy, which is a process I’m still in the midst of as I test this guide with people.
