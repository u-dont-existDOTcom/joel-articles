The Chicken-and-Egg Problem

P1Céline’s song is called On ne change pas—”we don’t change.” I think that’s both fortunately, and unfortunately, true for most of us. Fortunately in the sense that the sweet, innocent, curious, vivacious little one is still inside us, even if hiding. Unfortunate in the sense that we often don’t truly develop the adult qualities that we might pretend to have, so we continue burdening that little one inside us with troubles they aren’t made for handling. This shows up in how we may feel abandoned, helpless, impulsive, frightened, or desperate to be chosen.

P2And I realized that it doesn’t make sense to ask the child to « heal  itself, » which is really a disguised request for the child to abandon its own nature and turn into an adult. I call that “forced adulthood,” where the child has to deal with situations the evolved inner adult should be handling--we see this in actual children all the time when their parents are not providing care. People even wear t-shirts saying, “I don’t wanna adult today.” The forced adulting often goes along with a misperception that there is something wrong with the inner child. Instead, we need to separately develop the qualities of a good inner parent for that rightly vulnerable child: the loving Nurturer, safe Protector, and wise Guide.

P3That’s one grown-up doing three jobs, not three more people moving in to help with the rent, and each job keeps the others in balance. Say a party gets to be too much for your little one. The Protector gets you both out of there, and on the way home the Nurturer tells them they did great, even though mostly they hid in the bathroom. And the Guide helps them think twice about the next weird party they might go to, avoiding another cycle of bathroom vacationing. 🙈

P4The Guide is the one people misunderstand the most, because a lot of the bad parenting or misdirection we originally received was likely lacking in Protector and Nurturer functions, while over-relying on harsh guidance. So left it out of the first version of this framework until my friend Key pointed it out as crucial.Without a healed Inner Guide, our lives can become small and like a never-ending therapy session when we focus only on caring for ourselves rather than giving ourselves a mission as well. That’s the Guide’s job. But on the other hand, some people over-rely on their discipline or their mission to avoid caring for themselves. And many folks try to find an ideal guide via some kind of guru-led political or religious cult.

P5If the phrase “inner parent” calls up the wrong archetype for you, pick another one. Your future self might work, or the captain of a ship, so long as they’d do those three jobs.

P6But here’s the chicken-and-egg problem. What if you’re so far inside the child-state that you can’t become the adult? In this case, you sit there trying to be loving while your mind is becoming controlled again by the inherited critic voice, or you start going numb, throwing a fit, or panicking.

P7Sometimes somebody else may have to do one part of the adult role with you or for you first. You get to feel what that is like and watch how they do it. Maybe you copy one small thing you’ve seen them do, or imagine them there when they’re not, and try one piece yourself while they’re still helping with the rest. That’s what I mean by borrowed adulthood. You may still need help with almost all of it for a while, but over time there are more and more of those little parts you can do yourself.

Five Stages of Reparenting

P1For now, I think of the whole path in five progressive stages. You might be starting at any point here (and it may change even from hour to hour as well):

1.Forced adulthood : You’re still stuck identifying as the lost inner child, forced frequently into handling situations that require more maturity, even if it looks like you’ve got it all together. At this stage, use your childlike superpowers to come into the “Wow of Now” (have you seen the movie Waking Life?), and make sure there’s enough safety around you to continue with reparenting:
2.Begin to borrow adulthood — remember watching Mr. Rogers or Sesame Street? Those adult role models are what we’re looking for at this stage. This can almost feel like playing house as a kid where you imagined how you’d take care of a baby, etc.
P23.Practice being the adult — do at least one part of caring for yourself while support is still there from the previous step.
P34.Adult mode itself — after sufficient practice with reparenting, you become a natural at sending love to little you, protecting them, and shepherding them.
P45.Adult and child in relationship — the adult leads. The child isn’t pushed away, but doesn’t have to run the whole life alone. This can actually feel really weird if you’ve been stuck in forced adulthood for a while. Our goal is to go from weird to silly and playful. Lose self-consciousness while gaining self-awareness, if that makes sense!

P5Whichever stage you’re at, saying the right thing when you sit down with your little one helps, but you also have to start actually doing some of it in the rest of your life. Depending on your maladaptive patterns, improvements could look opposite to each other. If you’re a workaholic normally, then your little one might start believing in grown-up you when they catch you lying down because you’re wiped out, instead of pushing through one more chore. Or the opposite, when you stop procrastinating so much and clean your room, then they start beliving in you.

P6Reparenting isn’t an exercise you finish, even if you make excellent progress. You might feel like you’ve mastered the adult/child inner relationship, and then it’s Friday night and your boss asks you to stay late again. You get up the nerve to tell them no, and go home feeling okay about it, and nobody had to cry on a meditation cushion for that to happen.

Love Can Start Before It’s Trusted ❤️

P1Sometimes the love is there for your little one and still feels unsafe to them, or safe for a minute and then not. Some researchers found a phrase for that: “compassion-threat.” People in their study with anxious or avoidant attachment, or a tendency to be self-critical, reacted to compassion imagery like a threat, especially at first.

P2Love and trust are reciprocal and intertangled, like the Chicken and Egg problem. Without trust, the love seems fake. And without love, the trust is shallow. When you show your love, it can sound like you’re asking them to trust that you love them. But your love doesn’t need their trust. It does need you to be honest with them, which isn’t the same as perfect. They’re still going over your track record (they’ve heard what you still call yourself when you mess up, sometimes out loud).

P3When your little one says “I don’t trust you.” you can answer, “I believe you. And I love you.” I learned this when I was 5. My mom told me it was time for bed (or something equally outrageous to a 5-year-old). I cried and screamed “I HATE YOU! I wish you were dead !” 😡 She answered, “But I love you!” 🥰

P4That completely threw me. Fights don’t usually go like that. Anger is a way to get more power when you feel a lack of justice or agency. You puff yourself up so you seem frightening or dangerous, or you demand to be heard. My anger was there to get me heard, and her answer showed me I didn’t need it for that. Whatever injustice I thought there was wasn’t coming from a lack of love, either. So the anger seemed stupid. I saw for myself that it was only hurting me, without helping me see clearly or get what I wanted. Still, what I screamed was true for a part of me right then, and what she said back was true too, so I could hate somebody who loved me and she’d still be my mom the whole time.

P5When it’s your little one yelling at you, though, try not to turn “But I love you” into a prompted line you say on cue, because then it can feel like a tactical ruse to brush the anger off. You’re little one will be watching to see if the love stays afterward and takes whatever’s true in their anger seriously.

P6But even when the love does stay, your little one might still come back with “Big fuckity whoopty doo. I don’t believe you.” 🙄 You answer, “Okay. I believe that you don’t believe me. I still love you. 🤗 You don’t have to love me back. Tell me what makes the love feel unreal to you.”

P7Your little one might say it’s you, without giving more details at first. Keep practicing your listening, and eventually you’ll get more of a concrete complaint, which you can consider without getting flustered from the meanness they packaged it in. Like my anger at bedtime, their swearing is there to get them heard, so once you’re ears guards are off, they don’t need it anymore.

P8And sometimes you might feel the love for them, yet there’s also some other voice at the same time, besides what you choose to say. When they say “I don’t believe you,” something in you might resent that, and maybe even fire back an accusation, and then they can learn your love is only there as long as they take it the right way. The resentment can be purely visceral: being doubted after you’ve tried feels unfair, but there’s nothing unfair in them going over your track record. On the other hand, don’t give in and start believing your love is more conditional than it really is, regardless of the past. Apply the Mr. Rogers reframing trick here as well.

P1If you weren’t loved well as a child, then the suggestion to “love your inner child” can sound like nonsense. Love them how? Maybe you really want the best for your little one. P2Maybe just imagining a caring grown-up makes you feel more threatened, or lonelier. Lonelier is a strange one, with somebody right there. A Heart to Child Loop

P1With a little warmth to work with, rest one hand at the center of your chest (the heart chakra) and feel for love, and the other on the solar plexus, just below, and feel for your little one. The solar plexus is the most vulnerable part of the torso. One punch there hurts a lot (as I sadly learned while getting beaten up every day at kindergarten recess by a gang organized by my best friend. Can you believe it?), so it makes sense to look for your little one there.

P2Picture the love traveling from your heart down to your little one. If some comes back up, let it, and send it on down again, so it goes around like a loop. That part’s a gift from them, and they don’t owe it to you, so if nothing comes back, just keep sending yours. Make a Simple Vow

P1If your promises still mean something to your little one, maybe because you’ve kept a few small ones, a quiet vow can help too. As many real-life parents know, your heart really explodes in such an unexpected and wonderful way when you hold your newborn baby for the first time (happened to me!). And if you’re not yet a parent, it can really help to imagine holding a baby in your arms to make the vow feel real. Imagine you just had a baby, how would you feel? P2“I know you’re in there. I love you. You don’t have to love me back or trust me yet. I can’t promise that nothing painful will happen. Or that I’ll always know what to do, or never react badly. When I fail, I’ll come back to make it right. You don’t have to believe these words. Watch what I do.”

P3Notice it only promises what you can keep, so your little one can actually watch you keep it.

P4Whenever your little one shows up looking for their parent, even if they don’t know that’s what they are doing, you go back to being the one they can rely on, and you’re kind to them while you do it. How Much Happiness Do You Know Is Possible?

P1When you wish someone well, what do you have in mind? Probably something like, “I want them to have enough to eat, a safe place to sleep, some people who like them, etc.” Wanting this for someone is a form of love (this is the kind babies already understand). It’s a good and important thing, this practical love. But the horizon of your wishes may be partly determined by how much happiness/peace/freedom you know to be possible.

P2Sometimes your little one already feels cared for in those normal ways, but they need help with deeper existential questions. Maybe your life isn’t meant to be just something to put up with, something to slog through, a hamster wheel, a rat race. Maybe you could live it with a sense of zest and discovery. What would you need for that?

P3Maybe you’ve had some big spiritual experiences, and for a while knew a kind of peace/love etc. that you hadn’t known before. Now when you wish someone well, your wishes may be bigger – perhaps much bigger – than they would have been before. You might [« love them in a language they will never learn. »] (<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8o4jvs0s55E&t=3m10s>) This is one reason why loving-kindness meditation can seem kind of silly but actually be very powerful. Saying, “May you be happy,” over and over won’t necessarily zap you into a state of profound love for all beings, but it’s one way people discover that there is such a love and learn to expand it.

P4If you’re curious about this bigger love and happiness, follow that curiosity. You don’t have to pretend to believe anything, but you might read a few people from different traditions (try a Sufi poet or a Zen teacher). If you know anyone who seems consistently happy in this bigger way, check out how they deal with people when things are going badly. And if they still pass that test, consider, “I don’t know if I believe their explanation. But what if the experience is possible for me?” That’s what I did, and it was the best move I’ve ever made !

A Healthy Mind Is Not a Perfect Mind

P1The Guide doesn’t need to know everything for sure (that would be an OCD Guide, and then you’d need a Guide for the OCD Guide). It chooses the next sane step that fits your values based on the info available, and that’s how it helps your little one grow toward a whole life. Waiting to be sure first is sometimes proper & prudent, but if it’s a pattern, it can keep life on hold, and then your little one’s world stays small. Sometimes something really is stuck on someone else’s end (you’ve asked, and followed up, and there’s nothing left for you to do yet), and waiting on that isn’t avoidance. The Guide can let it wait, keeping an eye out for when it frees up, and keep the rest of your life moving, so your little one keeps growing anyway.

P2Forced adulthood can get into your head too, and it might set the bar too high sometimes. At your grandpa’s funeral, some dumb jingle starts up in your head and you nearly laugh during the eulogy. That doesn’t mean you didn’t love him, or that something’s wrong with you. Minds come up with ugly and embarrassing stuff all the time, some of it the opposite of what you believe, and bodies do their own thing too. The Guide doesn’t need to police your every thought. It notices the jingle and knows you didn’t mean it, and that it isn’t who you are. Then it goes with what you’d stand behind, which here is probably squeezing your mom’s hand and listening, and lets the jingle go.

P3I think this is closer to Buddhist practice too. The point is not an impossible performance in which nothing unskillful ever appears. In one discourse (sutta), AN 6.63, the Buddha defines karma in terms of intention (so if you didn’t mean to cause harm, your mind is not poisoned). In DN 2, he explains that in the training to purify the mind, one first recognizes a fault, makes amends, and then restrains themself to prevent that kind of thing in future. So it’s not like, “you’re either pure, or you’re not,” which is where a lot of folks stay stuck. If you’re ashamed about some pattern you have, that might be a signal to give some attention to how you can improve on that pattern. If it’s shame just because of an intrusive thought, then it’s probably not adaptive, and can be simply let go of.

5. When One Life Has Ended and the Next Hasn't Begun

P1Your little one can get really loud in the gap after one (figurative) life ends and before the next one has formed. For a while the old you is gone and the new you hasn’t shown up yet. Tibetan Buddhism calls an in-between state like that a bardo.

P2Your little one might be sure everything’s over. Usually one real thing ended, and the related habits might be held onto like on autopilot. Ask your little one what they’re scared will happen in the bardo between past and future (worst-case scenarios are kind of their specialty).

P3Something basic can mark the ending, like boxing up the old keys. Nobody needs to know what door will open next. But when the not-knowing feels too heavy, a new identity can sure look tempting. P1When you miss someone, let the ache be a cue for love. It doesn’t have to become one more problem to solve, and pushing it away won’t help. P2All of this happens in you, so it doesn’t matter whether they get any of it, because the main benefit is for your little one. I have an N of 1 case study that proves this works, also. It’s what rescued me from deep despair when the love of my life, my second wife, Bee, left me.
