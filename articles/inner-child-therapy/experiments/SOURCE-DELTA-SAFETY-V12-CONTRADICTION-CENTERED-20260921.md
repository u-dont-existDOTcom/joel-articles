## Exact candidate

Central qualification:

> That assumes grown-up you is at least willing to protect vulnerability rather than exploit it.

New H2:

> ## When the Present-Day Adult Is Dangerous to the Child
>
> I was using “adult” a little too loosely above. Being able to look around the room, pause, and make choices tells me some grown-up capacity is there. It doesn't tell me whether vulnerability is safe with you. If frightening or exploiting it is something you currently want—or you want therapy to make you better at doing that—don't bring the child forward to find out. Their fear can't be the test, or part of the reward.
>
> Here is the kind of thing that makes the judgment harder. Somebody says, “I'm sovereign,” and then, “I have to obey this urge, doctrine, group, spirit, or master.” I don't need to settle the metaphysics to notice that those two claims don't sit easily together. Can the person pause? Question it? Refuse? Change their mind? Even then, that still doesn't answer a separate question: do they actually want to frighten or exploit vulnerability? A religious identity, an unusual belief, an intrusive thought or unwanted fantasy, anger, charged language, or something harmful they did before can all look frightening without answering that. If you're helping, you can keep caring without flattering cruelty, arguing theology, or teaching somebody to exploit people more effectively.
>
> If the person really does endorse harming vulnerability, the child is not where I would work. Stay with the grown-up problem: what are they actually trying to change? If the wish to harm is part of an identity, that identity belongs in the conversation—what it gives them, what it costs, who it serves, and whether it has actually produced the life or happiness they expected. A later promise of changed intent isn't a reason to bring the child in as a witness. Let the change show up in repeated non-harmful choices, including when exploitation would feel rewarding. Until then there is no child contact, directly or by another side door (dialogue, childhood photos, hypnosis, borrowed-parent imagery, spiritual figures). Some people may need sustained care from a real human being more than another internal exercise or a bot.
