## Inner child reparenting

I connect my reparenting therapy to self-hypnosis because I find they are a perfect match. The child may arrive as an age, image, posture, voice, memory, emotion, or plain vulnerability. Sometimes no child appears. Stay with a recognizable protector, or with the sensation, feeling, or preference that is actually present.

When the child does appear, resist the urge to begin a healing project immediately. Be with them first.

“Little one, I am here. You do not have to explain everything. You do not have to be healed today. You do not have to become impressive. I only want to know what would help you feel less alone.”

The need may be warmth, play, protection, grief, anger, food, rest, a boundary, a safer relationship, less fantasy, less overwork, or one adult promise kept today.

## Don’t rush the repair

When a younger state, protector, feeling, or bodily signal needs care, move patiently:

Prepare: create enough time, privacy, support, and adult capacity.

Inspect: notice what is present before deciding what it means.

Ask permission: “May I come closer, or would you prefer warmth, space, words, silence, or no contact?”

Respond to one need: do the smallest useful thing rather than attempting a total repair.

Wait: let the response settle. Some changes need quiet, sleep, or ordinary time.

Reconnect: invite contact only where it is welcome. No part has to merge, disappear, forgive, or become cheerful.

Rest: stop before care becomes another performance.

Reassess: return later and ask what actually helped.

The goal isn’t “good as new,” as though history should disappear. Aim for good as you: more supported and connected while still recognizably yourself. If parts language makes you feel more divided, use signals, feelings, younger states, or another metaphor that helps.

## Play and rest still need an adult present

Reparenting doesn’t have to become solemn. A younger state may respond to texture, drawing, movement, a plushie with a voice, or whatever feels interesting. Keep reality-testing intact. You’re recovering play, not becoming somebody’s child, pet, doll, or owned object.

Rest also doesn’t require helplessness. Reduce decisions for a defined period, put unfinished tasks somewhere visible, and let the internal adult keep watch. You remain able to move, stop, and return.

## Non-dominant hand dialogue

When speech or visualization feels fake, write. Let the non-dominant hand speak for the child or protector, awkward handwriting and all. Answer with the dominant hand as the adult.

Non-dominant hand: “I don’t trust you.”

Dominant hand: “I hear that you don’t trust me. I still love you. Tell me where I’ve let you down. I won’t demand trust; I’ll listen and keep one small promise today.”

Non-dominant hand: “Which promise?”

Dominant hand: “I will eat, clean the desk, and not text the person who makes us feel unsafe.”

# When “your voice” is everybody else’s voice

Sometimes the inner-child question lands nowhere because a private self had very little room to develop. In controlling families, rigid religions, enmeshed communities, or high-pressure achievement systems, performance can begin so early that a person doesn’t know what they feel, want, believe, or prefer until somebody else tells them first. Then “love yourself” produces a reasonable question: love whom? Reparenting and identity-building can proceed together. For some people the child-self is already easy to feel. For others, creating enough safety for preference, curiosity, and experimentation is part of how that self becomes easier to hear.

This may apply when:

You do not know what you want unless someone else says what they want first.

Your opinions shift dramatically depending on who is in the room.

You agree in the moment and later realize you did not agree.

You feel responsible for other people’s emotions to the point of self-erasure.

Self-love feels performative.

The question “What does my inner child feel?” produces guesses, not felt answers.

You can rebel, but you cannot yet choose.

A useful developmental explanation is that when childhood is organized around avoiding pain and preserving attachment, attention goes into reading danger, meeting expectations, and keeping the bond. That can leave less room for the ordinary experiments that answer, “What do I like? What do I want to try? Who am I when nobody is grading me?” This is a practical model, not a claim about one specific brain system.

Sometimes a temporary, intentional period of distance from the people or group supplying the answers helps quieter preferences surface. Use that distance only long enough to tell your own signal from the social echo; prolonged isolation would undermine the purpose.

The difference between a human doer and a human being helps here. When worth isn’t felt internally, it gets earned through beauty, performance, intellect, status, spirituality, or becoming indispensable to everybody else. Those can all be real gifts. They become belonging bargains when failure in the arena feels like losing yourself.

Useful questions:

“What do I like when no one is watching?” “What do I believe before I check whether others approve?” “What do I want that is neither rebellion nor compliance?” “Where am I buying belonging with self-betrayal?” “Which arena am I using to earn the right to belong?” “What would I try if nobody needed to be impressed?” “Which relationships let me remain myself?” “What am I noticing in my body, and what meaning am I giving it?”

Give the inner child a small experiment with no résumé value: painting, dancing, gardening, a language, a sport, volunteering, music, or anything else you’re allowed to be bad at. The inherited parent may demand immediate talent. The adult can answer, “We’re finding out whether we enjoy this, not proving our worth.” Give it enough repetitions to get past the humiliation of being a beginner.

For a child, belonging can feel like survival, so families, religions, romances, political tribes, spiritual groups, institutions, and online communities can reach astonishingly far into identity. Adulting means noticing where you buy belonging with self-betrayal, choosing your own ground, and learning that you can disappoint someone without disappearing. The subconscious contains a crowd of borrowed voices, which is why this belongs in a self-hypnosis guide.

“Where do I become less true in order to remain included?”

“What would belonging look like if I did not have to disappear?”

“Is this guidance, or is this a belonging program?” “Is this love, or is this fear of exclusion?” “Is this conscience, or is this conditioning?” “Is this devotion, or is this compliance?” “Is this rebellion, or is this freedom?”

Then wait for the body. It isn’t infallible, but it deserves a seat at the hearing.

## Inner child reparenting

I see a lot of connection between reparenting therapy and self hypnosis. They fit well together, though I’d learn to do inner-child therapy sober first, in your ordinary mindstate, since that’s the one you’ll be living in, and get enough Nurturer and Protector in the room before you go deep. When you go into hypnosis the child might come to you. You might see that child in any form. It can be an image, a posture, a voice, a memory, an emotion, etc. It can be any age. It can be any vulnerable part of you. It doesn’t even have to be a child. If there is no child there, just work with a guard or whatever you find.

## Don’t rush the repair

Just be with the child.

If you can, tell the child that you are there for them and that they don’t have to explain themselves, or be healed or impress you. You only want to know what would help them feel less alone.

What does the child need? It can be anything: warmth, to play, to be protected, to mourn, to be angry, food, rest, boundaries, better relationships, to stop fantasizing, to stop working their butt off, that you keep a promise to them today, etc. etc.

Find a younger you/guard/feeling/signal that comes up in your body, and

A. Prepare yourself to tend to them with some privacy, but still able to access support if needed. Check how much grown-up you is really in the room first. Can you pause before you act? Are you following through after making decisions? If you can’t do those things because you’re basically the frightened kid still, then let’s leave the deeper conversation until later. Do whatever helps you come back a bit before you try to talk to little you.

B. Look at what's there before judging or deciding what it is.

C. Ask them what you can do or if you can move closer to them. Even if you feel like you already know what they need, ask if they'd prefer warmth, space, verbal reassurance, silence, distance, whatever.

D. Give them what you can: pick a need and do the smallest thing that would help. Don't feel like you have to fix it all.

E. Give yourself some time to let what you did sink in- it may take some time, it may take sleep, etc.

F. Reconnect with them- don't expect them to merge, go away, forgive you, become happy, etc. Just reconnect with them if they're willing.

G. Stop and rest before the caring turns into another performance, and reconnect with them later to see what was helpful.

Remember the goal isn't to make yourself good as new but to make yourself good as you- more supported and connected to yourself. If you don't like the idea of part work or it makes you feel more divided, you can work with a signal or a feeling instead of a part, or with an imaginary younger you that you feel embodies the issue you're having.

## Play and rest still need an adult present

When you're reparenting, it's okay to play with things that interest your younger states, whether that means touching things of different textures, drawing, exercising, roleplaying with a plushie in a different voice, etc. As long as your reality testing is good and you remember that you aren't a child or pet or doll or whatever and that nobody owns you, it's okay to work on getting your play ability back. A lot of the best discoveries come when you're both just messing around. Play not only paves the way for therapy, but is a part of how successful therapy should be measured.

Resting doesn’t mean you have to become helpless. It’s okay to limit the number of decisions you are going to be making for a certain amount of time, it’s okay to put the things you might not get to on a list, and it’s okay to let your inner adult keep watch for a while. You can still move, stop and come back whenever you need to.

## Non-dominant hand dialogue

If talking out loud or visualizing conversations feels fake, try writing it out. Tell your child/ guard to write with your nondominant hand, and talk to them with your dominant. It might be a little hard to write with your nondominant hand but you can kind of do it. So for example, they might say "I don't trust you" with your nondominant hand, and you can say with your dominant hand "I know you don't trust me right now but I love you and I want to know how I've fucked up. I don't expect you to trust me but I want to hear you out and give you a promise I can keep today" and they can say "what promise?" and you can say "I'm going to make sure I eat today/ clear off my desk/ not text so-and-so". Only say the "I love you" part if you can feel it. If you don't feel the love yet, say you care.

# When “your voice” is everybody else’s voice

It can be difficult to connect with your inner child if your inner child didn’t have the opportunity to develop. This happens often when you grow up in a situation where you don’t have a private self, for example when a controlling family, a rigid religion or an enmeshed community takes over very early in your life. Or you are pushed to achieve very early in life. The result is that you don’t know what you feel, what you want, what you believe, what you like and dislike until you have heard someone else’s opinion about it. If you are in that situation, it can be difficult to follow the advice to ‘love yourself’. You don’t even know who ‘yourself’ is! So you can do reparenting and finding yourself in parallel. For others, it is very easy to work with the inner child because they can feel it right away.

You are in the first camp if:

You don’t know what you want, until you have heard what someone else wants.

Your opinion is very different when you are with different people

You agree with people in the moment but when you are by yourself you realize that you don’t agree with them

You take responsibility for the emotions of others, at the expense of yourself

When you try to love yourself, it feels more like a show

When you try to find out how your inner child feels, you feel that you are guessing instead of feeling

You can rebel, but you can’t choose yet

One way of explaining this is that your childhood didn’t leave you much room for finding out what you like, what you want to try out and who you are when nobody is grading you. You were too busy avoiding pain, reading what was dangerous and what you had to do to stay in a good bond, etc.

Temporary Separation. Sometimes it may be wise to remove yourself, for a time, from the people or group that you normally would get answers from. Some people come back later on different terms, and sometimes the break has to be long-term because the pattern of losing yourself with them is deeply entrenched. As you become more your own person, you learn to tell which of those apply. You want to let your quieter preferences bubble up to the surface so you can be sure you’re not just echoing your social group.

What is the difference between a human being and a human doing? If you don’t feel good enough as a human being, you will try to become good enough as a "human doing" by, for example, being beautiful, doing something well, being smart, having a certain status, being spiritual, etc. Those can all be real gifts, although they still become a kind of bargain for belonging when failing in these areas feels like losing what it means to have your own inherent value.

So in order to find yourself, you can ask yourself:

What do I like when nobody is looking?

What do I believe when I don’t check with other people?

What do I want that is neither in rebellion nor in compliance with others?

Where am I buying my sense of belonging at the expense of betraying myself?

What can I try on that I don’t need to be good at?

With whom can I be myself?

What do I feel in my body and what do I make out of it?

Give your inner child the opportunity to do something that has no value for its resume and that it doesn’t need to be good at. For example painting, dancing, gardening, learning a language, playing a sport, doing volunteer work, playing a musical instrument, etc. Because often your inherited parent script tells your inner child that it is supposed to be good at something as soon as it starts doing it, which is humiliating. The more consciously sculpted adult in you can answer, “We’re finding out whether we enjoy this, not proving our worth.” So try things out long enough until the humiliation is gone.
