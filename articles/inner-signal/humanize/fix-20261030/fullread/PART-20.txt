# Appendix J: A practical induction library

Preview the sequence, choose one purpose, and decide whether you want to begin. Each family can be light, eyes-open, or replaced by no-trance contact. Notice the actual response—including no change—and let it determine the next move. Move, imagine, change the sensory channel, remain still, or stop. Use the complete waking return after every induction.

If vivid images don’t come, translate the metaphor into words, rhythm, spatial sense, touch, movement, or simple knowing. The mind doesn’t owe anybody a movie.

## 1. Stretch and release

Feel the support beneath you and the position of one hand or foot. Press gently, stretch slightly, and release—or imagine it. Notice ease, tension, warmth, breath, or no change. Repeat with no more than three areas, letting “release” guide the sequence. Which area or inner voice wants attention? What support is needed? Restore ordinary movement and return.

## 2. Supported floating

Notice where your weight is carried. Let support carry 1% more effort, as if floating without losing contact, or keep ordinary weight and pressure. Notice the response. You may deliberately alternate attention toward ordinary awareness on the inhale and support on the exhale; name this fractionation beforehand. What becomes easier to hear? Keep the support, regain chosen weight and firm contact, then return.

## 3. Rhythmic breath or pulse

Accompany a natural rhythm: breath, a comfortable hum, a finger tap, outside sound, or silent counting. No rhythm is also an option. Repeat “here, and here again,” noticing where attention lands between repetitions. Stop the deliberate rhythm and notice the first quiet beat, without trying to become blank. Is a word, feeling, need, or action clearer? Let ordinary rhythm resume and return.

## 4. Unwinding object

Notice a concern with several strands. Imagine yarn unwinding, bubbles settling, or knots placed visibly beside you. Leave the object unchanged or label it in words when that fits better. Count only strands you actually set down; nothing is erased. Which needs warmth, protection, or action? Keep one useful strand, give the others a visible holding place, and return.

## 5. Sensory journey

Hear the actual room and feel the body before imagining a step toward a chosen destination. Use footsteps, textures, or landmarks to gather attention. Change scenery or skip directly to dialogue when useful. Notice a detail without assigning hidden meaning; the mystery is how the scene develops, not what will be done to you. Who or what needs to speak? Return by the same route and close the imagined space.

## 6. Gradual wave

Start with a small neutral area and what is already there. Imagine warmth, steadiness, or attention spreading to the next comfortable area, or keep it localized. Pause at each step and notice what changes without claiming medical repair. Does the body want contact, space, words, or ordinary care? Let the image recede, feel the whole body and real contact points, and return.

## 7. Care workshop

Prepare time, a place, tools, and boundaries for patient care. Let a signal, feeling, or younger state approach only as closely as welcome; choose language that doesn’t make you feel fragmented. Ask before responding, wait, reconnect only where welcome, rest, and reassess. An available inner adult or one borrowed function can help; you choose words, pace, and action. Aim for “good as you,” not erased history. Close the workspace, affirm wholeness and adult presence, and return.

## 8. Resource embodiment

Choose qualities you understand: warmth, boundary, steadiness, curiosity, or dignity. Imagine posture, voice, or attention with 1% more access to one quality. Observe rather than embody it when preferred. Notice a movement, word, image, or no response. Repeat to strengthen access, not replace identity. How would that quality meet today’s situation? Keep the quality and one useful action, release the role, affirm “I am myself,” and return.

## 9. No-trance inner contact

Observable pacing: Keep the eyes open, name the room and day, feel the feet, and read or write in ordinary awareness. Invitation: Ask one clear question and wait without narrowing attention further. Genuine choice: Answer, write “unsure,” rest, change the question, seek another person’s view, or stop. Autonomous signal: Record a word, sensation, impulse, objection, or silence without interpreting it as proof. Deepener: None. Full alertness is not an inferior version of the practice. Handoff: Let the internal adult respond in writing or through one practical choice. Integration and exit: Reality-test the signal and do one small action. Because no altered state was cultivated, simply close the exercise and continue the day.

# Appendix J: A practical induction library

Preview the sequence. Work out in advance which one you want to do and what you want to use it for (pick just one) and whether or not you want to do it at all! Any of these families of induction can be done as light inductions, with eyes open, or you can just do them as a no-trance inner contact.

Once you start an induction, you will want to notice what’s going on (even if nothing changes) and work out what to do next: move, imagine something else, change the sensory channel you’re using, do nothing, or stop the induction sequence and do something else… Whatever you do, make sure you bring yourself fully back to waking at the end of an induction.

NB: if you can’t ‘see’ the images you’re trying to visualize, that’s fine, change them into words, rhythm, spatial relations, something to touch or move or just plain knowing…

## 1. Stretch and release

Stretch and Release: Work out what will be supporting you, focus on the feeling of support. You can also focus on where one of your hands or feet is. Either actually, or in your imagination, stretch a little bit, either stretching that part of your body slightly or pressing gently down into the support, and then let go of the effort, just relax. See if there is any difference, even if there isn’t. Do this for no more than 3 places on your body. Let releasing guide you as you go through the sequence. Which bit of your body, or inner voice, needs this? What do you need to support it? Bring your body back to normal movement and return.

## 2. Supported floating

Supported Floating: Work out what is supporting you and where your weight is, imagine that the support is holding a tiny bit (1%) more of your effort. It’s like floating but you don’t have to lose contact, or you can just keep it at the same level, with just enough weight/pressure. See what happens, you can fractionate this if you want, perhaps noticing your normal awareness when you breathe in and the support when you breathe out. Tell yourself you are going to fractionate before you do it. What can you hear more easily now? Keep the support, come back to your normal weight and firm contact, and return.

## 3. Rhythmic breath or pulse

Rhythmic: Use your breathing or slightly hum in your throat to make a natural rhythm, or tap your finger or listen to sounds outside to make an artificial one. You can even do it by counting to yourself (silently) or with no rhythm at all. Tell yourself you are going to say “here, and here again” over and over, and notice what you pay attention to in between the phrases, then stop saying it. Notice what you become aware of in the first beat – you don’t need to make your mind blank, just notice what comes up. Does it make any words, feelings, needs or actions more clear? Bring back your normal rhythm and return.

## 4. Unwinding object

Unwinding Object: Good for when you have a few different strands to one thing you want to work on. Imagine an object unwinding – maybe some yarn, some bubbles, or some knots that you place beside you. Once you’ve placed them all, give yourself a minute to look at them all, you don’t have to change anything, but you can label them if you need to (just a word or two). How many did you put down? Which one do you need to work on, what does it need – warmth, protection, action? Keep one strand in your attention and put the others somewhere you can see them and return.

## 5. Sensory journey

Sensory Journey: Pay attention to the sounds in the room you’re in and how your body feels, then imagine yourself walking somewhere. Where do you want to go? Listen to the sounds of your footsteps and take in your surroundings as you walk, what do objects feel like if you touch them, what landmarks do you see? When you feel you have gathered enough of your attention, imagine the scenery changing until you are in a place where you can talk to whoever you need to (or you can just cut to the chase, if you prefer). Pay attention to something without worrying about what it means. Allow yourself to be curious about what’s going to happen next, but don’t make it a “what are they going to do to you?” kind of thing. When you are ready to finish, imagine yourself walking back the way you came and close the space.

## 6. Gradual wave

Gradual wave: start with a very small neutral area, feel into it a little and then imagine a wave of warmth, steadiness, or attention spreading out to a new area that is still ok. Sense into the new area (or continue using the old). See if there is anything different at each step. Does the body want any contact, space, words, other care? Let the mental image go, sense your body, sense your contact points, come back.

## 7. Care workshop

Care workshop: prepare the workshop (time, place, tools, boundaries, etc). Invite the signal/feeling/young part to come only as close as is welcome. Try to avoid any language that makes you feel you're not one person. Care for it as best you can, by asking what it wants and giving that if you can, or sensing into it and responding, or just being with it. Take breaks, get back in contact if that's ok, take more breaks, etc. Keep bringing in adult functions (or borrow someone else's function). Have the adult part of you run the show. Goal is to make it "good as you" rather than "good as new", not to erase history. Tell your little one, "That's enough for today. We're going back to the life we're building." Close the workspace, reassure yourself that you are one whole person, that you're an adult now, and come back.

## 8. Resource embodiment

Resource: pick a resource quality you understand (warmth, boundary, steadiness, curiosity, or dignity). Imagine yourself accessing 1% more of that quality (or, if you prefer, just observe it rather than embody it). What is different? How is your body, voice, etc? Do this a few times to strengthen it. Come back, keeping the quality and one positive action that you would do with it, but leaving the imagined you behind, saying "I am me".

## 9. No-trance inner contact

For the no trance inner contact, I would follow something along the lines of these steps:

For pacing (warm-up), notice reality as it is now: what you can see, what day of the week it is, that you can read or that you still have your feet, etc. Hopefully your name didn't suddenly change to Larry.

For the suggestion: in your mind, ask a clear question and then listen gently.

Now keep your sense of agency during the answer phase. You might notice an answer popped up, or you might decide you don't know. You can always take a break or rephrase the question. Sometimes you might ask advice from someone else.

Keep aware of the subtle inner signal: write whatever you get. It might be a word or sensation. An impulse or objection. Sometimes there's no signal, just silence, itself a kind of signal. Don't jump to interpreting it as validation of some theory.

Hand off the role: have your adult self answer that same question in whatever way is appropriate, preferably by writing or doing.

Integration/exit: reality test the signal you noticed. As part of testing, if it seems appropriate, you can do something simple based on it.

There's no elaborate exit protocol here since you didn't go into trance. Just close the exercise and carry on with your day.
