## Gendlin Focusing

“What is the felt sense of this whole anxiety?”

Don’t answer from the head immediately. Give the body time to form one vague sense of the whole situation, then ask:

“What would this felt sense like me to know?”

Try a word or phrase and check how well it fits the felt sense. Then examine what it suggests about the situation; feeling right doesn’t settle that. Words and bodily attention can help correct each other.

## Body supports

Sometimes the mind is too activated to meditate usefully. Then help through the body: foot, scalp, temple, neck, or shoulder massage; warmth; pressure; a weighted blanket; darkness; familiar calming music; or a massage gun used carefully. Avoid the front of the neck, injuries, numb areas, bruises, inflamed tissue, recent surgery sites, varicose veins, and anything that produces pain, dizziness, tingling, or a symptom that feels wrong.

Other people settle with warmth, darkness, music, massage, weighted blankets, breathwork, familiar objects, or personal relaxation technology. Use the support that actually helps rather than assembling a tiny wellness trade show around the anxiety.

# Appendix E: Is the daydream helping or replacing your life?

Daydreaming can be intuition, simulation, grief digestion, creative rehearsal, symbolic teaching, or the subconscious showing a possible future, fear, or desire. It can also become avoidance, fantasy addiction, dissociation, romantic obsession, or an escape hatch from adult life.

Does the daydream return me to life with more clarity, love, and action? Or does it pull me away from life into repetition, craving, and avoidance?

When it seems useful, ask:

“What is the guidance inside this?”

When it seems to be pulling you away, change the body before debating the story. Sit upright. Open the eyes a little. Feel the feet.

“Subconscious, thank you for showing me images. If this daydream is useful, please reveal the guidance inside it. If it is avoidance, please bring me gently into the present and help me deal with what I need to deal with in a gentle but structured way.”

Then get embarrassingly practical:

“What real-life action is this daydream replacing?”

The answer may be: send the message, stop sending the message, clean the room, eat, sleep, grieve, create, apologize, leave, tell the truth, make the plan, ask for help, return to the task, or stop chasing unavailable love.

# Appendix F: Self-trust, outside guidance, and oracle-like states

The synchronicity cue above turns noticing into orientation and reality-testing rather than an oracle.

Suggestibility varies wildly. Some people open more with an external hypnotist, some with themselves, some only in particular states, and some barely respond to commands while responding deeply to imagery, music, metaphor, or spiritual feeling. A professional can bring pacing, containment, and another set of eyes. Self-hypnosis gives you instant follow-up, real-time correction, and a private line to your own protectors.

Self-hypnosis may be best for daily intimacy with the subconscious. Skilled outside support may be best for trauma complexity, blind spots, dissociation, destabilization, and relational wounds that need another nervous system.

## Finding a hypnotist who can work with you

Ask what they are trained to treat, how they use hypnosis, and how they respond when a suggestion doesn’t fit. For trauma work, ask about actual trauma and dissociation training, not only a hypnosis certificate. Credentials matter without guaranteeing competent care.

Describe what has helped, what hasn’t, your preferred directness, acceptable touch and imagery, and how you’ll stop. Ask about unexpected reactions, uncertain memories, and the full return. Firm encouragement can fit; refusal still ends it.

Evaluate what actually happened, not the method’s name. Was its purpose explained? Did you agree? How did the practitioner respond to you? Discomfort alone doesn’t settle the question; treating every objection as proof you need more of the same deserves scrutiny. Ask the app or another informed person for explanation, without expecting a practitioner to be certified from a brief account. Self-hypnosis, combined work, or another practitioner remain your choices.

# Appendix E: Is the daydream helping or replacing your life?

Sometimes daydreaming is something that your subconscious mind is doing to help you. It is simulating an idea or event to help you digest grief or teach you something. The very act of daydreaming may be helping you to access your creativity or allowing your subconscious to show you a potential future. Sometimes your subconscious will show you things that you may be afraid of. Sometimes it will show you what you want.

Other times daydreaming may be taking you away from your life in a detrimental way. It may be that you are escaping reality and are addicted to fantasies rather than the real life. You may be dissociating or obsessing about romantic partners or escaping from being an adult.

How do you know which it is? Simply ask yourself if the daydreaming is taking you closer to life, with more clarity, love and ability to take action or if it is taking you away from life, in a repetitive and craving way.

If it is helping you then simply ask yourself what the guidance is and how you can use it.

If it is taking you away from life then change your body position by sitting upright, open your eyes, feel your feet and ask your subconscious to help you. Simply say to yourself, “Dear subconscious thank you for showing me images. If what you are showing me is helpful then please also show me the guidance in it. If what you are showing me is a way of avoiding dealing with life then please show me in a gentle but structured way how to deal with life as it is right now.”

Then ask yourself what it is that you need to do in real life that you are avoiding by daydreaming.

You may need to send a text message or stop sending text messages. You may need to clean up your room, eat, sleep, deal with grief, create, apologize, leave, tell the truth, make a plan, get help, get back to a task that you were supposed to be doing, stop chasing love that is not available to you etc.

# Appendix F: Self-trust, outside guidance, and oracle-like states

[Please see how to use synchronicity as a cue, not an oracle, above.]

One of the issues here is that people vary dramatically in how suggestible they are, both internally and externally, and different people vary in how suggestible they are in different states. Different types of suggestion (commands, imagery, music, metaphors, spiritual, etc) can be very different for different people. So there are definite advantages to both having someone work with you (pacing, containment, another set of eyes), and doing things yourself. Doing it yourself means being able to follow up immediately, to correct something in real time, to have a line of communication with guards, etc.

That said, self hypnosis may be the best way of talking to your subconscious on a day to day basis. If you’re dealing with particularly complicated trauma, blind spots, dissociation, destabilization, or relational wounds that need another nervous system, skilled outside support may be best.

## Finding a hypnotist who can work with you

Ask them:

- What they are trained in (what they can treat)

- How they use hypnosis

- What they will do if things don't fit (if they are treating trauma, did they have specific training on trauma and dissociation? Having hypnosis certifications matters, but doesn't mean they are trained in trauma care)

Tell them:

- What has/hasn't worked for you in the past

- How direct you want them to be

- What kind of touch (if any) you are ok with

- What kind of imagery (if any) you are ok with

- What you want them to do if you want them to stop

Ask them:

- What they will do if things go unexpected during or after the session

- What they do if you aren't sure if something is a memory or not

- How they will do the full return (coming out of hypnosis) at the end of the session

You should be able to be firmly encouraged, but not forced to continue if you don't want to.

Then afterwards, check in about what was actually done, regardless of what you might call the method. Ask the person why they did what they did, and whether they told you in advance. Did you agree to it? How did the person respond to you? Keep in mind that just because something made you uncomfortable, it doesn't mean it was bad, but if they saw your discomfort as meaning that you needed more of it, that might be something to look into. You might even be able to use this app or ask around to see if someone can tell you what the things the person did meant. They probably won't be able to tell you if the person was certified in them or not, though. Then you can decide if you want to try learning to do self-hypnosis, using both, or find someone else.
