## You keep using hypnosis but not changing life

“What would prove to the inner child that the adult is real?”

Then do the thing.

# What not to do

Do not treat inner voices, images, autonomous movements, or body signals as commands or proof. Listen, then reality-test.

Do not chase grand downloads or turn demons, jinn, and “Unattached Burdens” into a hobby. Stabilize first, and seek help when reality-testing or function weakens.

Do not use self-hypnosis while driving, bathing, swimming, operating equipment, supervising children, or doing anything that requires active attention.

Do not force memory recovery, use trance to prove what happened, or suppress medical symptoms.

Do not override hunger, fatigue, pain, grief, anger, or fear before understanding the signal.

Do not prolong altered states indefinitely or treat intuition as fact without testing it.

Keep the inner roles in proportion: the child does not lead adult life; the Protector cannot exclude all love; the Nurturer cannot erase consequences; the Leader cannot bypass the child; analysis and tenderness both remain available.

Do not use NLP to override protectors, manufacture certainty, manipulate anyone, or perform healing when the body says no.

Doubt, distraction, resistance, silence, and “no” do not prove that an induction is working.

“The unconscious will keep me safe” does not replace clear limits, a safe setting, the ability to stop, and a tested exit.

Ordinary sensations do not prove hidden trauma, illness, toxicity, possession, attachment, transformation, or subconscious truth.

Imagined repair does not establish physical recovery, neurological repair, cellular change, or a cure.

Do not install hidden instructions, undisclosed third-party activation, compulsory behavior, identity switching, or amnesia. A consciously chosen reusable cue should have a clear purpose, remain reviewable, and be retired if it becomes unwanted; saying “cancel” does not guarantee instant erasure.

End every waking session with ordinary identity, movement, orientation, judgment, and a direct statement that the session has ended.

# When this needs more support than a guide can provide

Use self-hypnosis as a relationship with yourself. When the situation needs actual care, the guide shouldn’t become a substitute for it.

Get urgent help now for immediate danger, severe confusion, inability to keep yourself safe, or concerning physical symptoms such as chest pain or fainting. New or worsening suicidal thoughts, severe insomnia, mania-like or psychosis-like symptoms, or dissociation that disrupts functioning need prompt professional assessment, not a four-to-twelve-week practice schedule.

For ordinary learning, start with shorter sessions, warmth, a manageable subject, and a full return. Over a week or two, track one target—anxiety, daydreaming, relationship repair, an avoided task, sleep, emotional regulation, or intuitive accuracy. Worsening function or growing certainty about unverifiable memories calls for reassessment, not more intensity.

Seek suitable ongoing help for recurring panic, compulsive behavior, substance-related destabilization, or major relationship instability.

## Methods to explore with a trained person present

Use a trauma-informed clinician trained in the method, present with you, for deliberate trauma-focused regression, searching for unknown traumatic origins through an affect or somatic bridge, or work with substantial flashbacks or dissociative reactions. A bridge follows associations from a feeling or sensation; regression revisits recalled or imagined younger experience. Either can have different uses, including resourcing. Ask what is intended and how uncertainty and unexpected reactions will be handled. Hypnosis cannot prove a historical event, even with a clinician present.

Trauma history, tears, or wanted difficult feelings don’t automatically exclude self-practice. Choose the working range with attention to the actual response and available support. Some people need a narrow range; others much broader. Confidence alone doesn’t supply missing support.

Major trauma guidelines emphasize treatments such as trauma-focused CBT variants, CPT, PE, and EMDR depending on the person and guideline. Self-hypnosis and inner-child practice may support that process, but high-risk trauma work needs a larger container.

If the practice makes ordinary life more loving, honest, functional, relational, grounded, and precise, continue gently. If it makes ordinary life less stable, stop deepening and return to sleep, food, grounding, boundaries, and outside support.

# Closing

The subconscious isn’t the enemy. Neither are the body, the sharp analytical mind, or the protector that has been braced for years. Most of them have been trying to help without ever being properly introduced.

I’m less interested in how deeply you can get hypnotized than whether you become an adult you can trust.

Listen until the kid is willing to come closer. Keep enough small promises that the guards can occasionally sit down. Love yourself enough that thought stops bracing against tenderness, and think clearly enough that love doesn’t drift into fantasy. Then take the practice back into ordinary life, where the adult keeps the promise.

# What not to do

DO NOT: View your inner experiences as commands or proof. They’re internal happenings that need to be reality tested at all times.

DO NOT: Engage in seeking “downloads” of information or make a hobby of demons/jinn/Unattached Burdens or the like, until you have stabilized your life. Get help to do so, if you are not reality testing well, or not functioning well in your life.

DO NOT: Attempt to “force” memories, to use trance to “prove” something happened to you, or to skip medical attention for symptoms just because you can “force” them down using hypnosis.

DO NOT: Force yourself to ignore your body’s needs, such as eating when hungry, resting when tired, responding to pain, etc., or your emotions, such as letting yourself grieve when someone dies, feeling anger when it is appropriate, acknowledging your fears, etc., until you know more about what the signals are telling you.

DO NOT: “Extend” the duration of altered states indefinitely.

DO NOT: Allow various internal roles to get out of proportion (e.g., allow the Inner Child to run your adult life, the Protector to shut out all love, the Nurturer to disregard consequences, the Guide to ignore the needs of the Child). You are capable of analysis, as well as tenderness.

DO NOT: Utilize NLP techniques to force down guard parts, to give yourself certainty you do not have, to manipulate others, or to heal yourself when your body is telling you not to.

Don’t consider negative indicators (doubt, distraction, resistance, lack of response, saying “No”) as good signs that the induction is working.

Don’t rely upon the safety of your unconscious mind as the only safety factor in any session. Be sure you have set up clear limits, are in a safe place, and have a way to terminate the session and get out which has been tested and proven to work.

Don’t believe that normal physical sensations are indications of hidden trauma, illness, toxins in your body, possession, “attachments,” “transformations,” or information from your “subconscious mind.”

Don’t believe that anything is “healed” in your body (neurologically, cellularly, etc.) simply because it is “healed” in your imagination.

Don’t put hidden commands in yourself, nor allow anyone to do so. Even if you are consciously placing something in yourself which you wish to utilize later, be sure that you know exactly what you are doing and why you are doing it, and be sure that you can review it and CAN “take it out” if you decide that you no longer wish to use it. It may not be as easy as simply saying, “Take out.” Nor should you allow anyone to activate these commands in you without your knowledge, or use them to make you do something you don’t want to do, to make you into someone you don’t want to be, to give you amnesia, etc.

# When this needs more support than a guide can provide

You should get help now if you are in imminent danger, if you are very confused, if you cannot ensure your own safety, and/or if you are experiencing concerning physical symptoms (e.g., chest pain, fainting, etc). You probably don't need any more support than this if you keep the practice shorter, make it warm, pick a small and tractable subject, make sure you come back out, and then see how it affects a specific target (anxiety, daydreaming, helping a relationship, facing a task you've been avoiding, helping you sleep, being less reactive emotionally, improving your intuitive accuracy, etc) for a week or two. If you notice that your functioning is getting worse, or that you're getting more certain about unconfirmable memories, then reassess instead of pushing harder.

## Methods to explore with a trained person present

If you want to deliberately do trauma-focused regression (going backwards in your memory or imagination to times when you were younger), or affect and somatic bridging (following associations from an emotional or somatic experience to find the origin of that experience, particularly if it was a traumatic experience you don't know about), or if you find that you're having substantial flashbacks or dissociative reactions, then you should do this with a trauma-informed clinician present. Make sure they're trained in the specific method you want to do. Ask any potential clinician what they would do and how they deal with uncertainty and unexpected reactions. Note that even if you work with a person, hypnosis is not a way of knowing what did or did not happen to you.

Note that having experienced trauma, crying, and even wanting to feel difficult feelings are not necessarily signs that you need more support than working on your own. You get to pick how wide or narrow a range you want to work in, but be aware that different people need different ranges. Pay attention to how you respond and what support you have. Feeling like you are handling it isn't a substitute for having support.

The main clinical practice guidelines for the treatment of major trauma recommend one or more of trauma-specific variants of cognitive behavioral therapy (often referred to by specific names, such as cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure, etc.) or eye movement desensitization and reprocessing, depending on the person and the guideline. These approaches in turn can be further supported by techniques such as self-hypnosis and inner child work. But high-risk trauma work needs a bigger container than that.

## Your functioning in ordinary life is the feedback

Always keep in mind that your functioning in ordinary life is the feedback. If you find yourself more loving, more honest, more functional, more relational, more grounded, more precise, etc. than when you started this process, keep on keeping on gently. If you notice that your ordinary life is becoming less stable, don't try to go deeper. Go for more sleep, more eating, more grounding, more healthy boundaries with other people, more getting support from other people, etc.
