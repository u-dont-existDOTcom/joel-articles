[B10] A useful developmental explanation is that when childhood is organized around avoiding pain and preserving attachment, attention goes into reading danger, meeting expectations, and keeping the bond. That can leave less room for the ordinary experiments that answer, “What do I like? What do I want to try? Who am I when nobody is grading me?” This is a practical model, not a claim about one specific brain system.

[B11] Sometimes a temporary, intentional period of distance from the people or group supplying the answers helps quieter preferences surface. Use that distance only long enough to tell your own signal from the social echo; prolonged isolation would undermine the purpose.

REWRITE:
[CHANGED, replaces B10]
You are like this because in your childhood you didn’t spend enough attention in finding out what you like and what you want to try out and who you are. You were too occupied in not being in pain, reading out what was dangerous and what you had to do to stay in a good bond, etc. This is not about a specific place in your brain, but a way of looking at it.

[CHANGED, replaces B11]
Temporary Separation. Sometimes it may be wise to remove yourself, for a time, from the people or group that you normally would get answers from. Not for too long, however, or you’ll defeat the purpose of the exercise. You want to let your quieter preferences bubble up to the surface so you can be sure you’re not just echoing your social group.
