PUBLISHED section:
[B1] Use self-hypnosis as a relationship with yourself. When the situation needs actual care, the guide shouldn’t become a substitute for it.

[B2] Get urgent help now for immediate danger, severe confusion, inability to keep yourself safe, or concerning physical symptoms such as chest pain or fainting. New or worsening suicidal thoughts, severe insomnia, mania-like or psychosis-like symptoms, or dissociation that disrupts functioning need prompt professional assessment, not a four-to-twelve-week practice schedule.

[B3] For ordinary learning, start with shorter sessions, warmth, a manageable subject, and a full return. Over a week or two, track one target—anxiety, daydreaming, relationship repair, an avoided task, sleep, emotional regulation, or intuitive accuracy. Worsening function or growing certainty about unverifiable memories calls for reassessment, not more intensity.

[B4] Seek suitable ongoing help for recurring panic, compulsive behavior, substance-related destabilization, or major relationship instability.

[B6] Use a trauma-informed clinician trained in the method, present with you, for deliberate trauma-focused regression, searching for unknown traumatic origins through an affect or somatic bridge, or work with substantial flashbacks or dissociative reactions. A bridge follows associations from a feeling or sensation; regression revisits recalled or imagined younger experience. Either can have different uses, including resourcing. Ask what is intended and how uncertainty and unexpected reactions will be handled. Hypnosis cannot prove a historical event, even with a clinician present.

[B7] Trauma history, tears, or wanted difficult feelings don’t automatically exclude self-practice. Choose the working range with attention to the actual response and available support. Some people need a narrow range; others much broader. Confidence alone doesn’t supply missing support.

[B8] Major trauma guidelines emphasize treatments such as trauma-focused CBT variants, CPT, PE, and EMDR depending on the person and guideline. Self-hypnosis and inner-child practice may support that process, but high-risk trauma work needs a larger container.

[B9] If the practice makes ordinary life more loving, honest, functional, relational, grounded, and precise, continue gently. If it makes ordinary life less stable, stop deepening and return to sleep, food, grounding, boundaries, and outside support.

If you need help now: You may need help now if you feel in imminent danger, very confused, or unable to take care of yourself. You may need help now if you have physical symptoms (chest pain, fainting, etc.) You should seek professional help promptly if you have new or worsening suicidal thoughts, severe insomnia, new or worsening symptoms of mania or psychosis, or if dissociative symptoms are affecting your functioning. These are not problems that this guide will resolve in 4-12 weeks.

You probably don't need any more support than this: If you keep the practice shorter, make it warm, pick a small and tractable subject, make sure you come back out, and then see how it affects a specific target (anxiety, daydreaming, helping a relationship, facing a task you've been avoiding, helping you sleep, being less reactive emotionally, improving your intuitive accuracy, etc) for a week or two, and don't notice that your functioning is getting worse, or that you're getting more certain about unconfirmable memories, then you're probably doing fine.

You probably need more support if: You are experiencing recurring panic attacks, compulsions, substance destabilization, major relationship instability, etc.

[CHANGED, replaces B6-B9]
If you want to deliberately do trauma-focused regression (going backwards in your memory or imagination to times when you were younger), or affect and somatic bridging (following associations from an emotional or somatic experience to find the origin of that experience, particularly if it was a traumatic experience you don't know about), or if you find that you're having substantial flashbacks or dissociative reactions, then you should do this with a trauma-informed trained person present. It's best if they're trained in the specific method you want to do. Ask any potential person what they would do and how they deal with uncertainty. Note that even if you work with a person, hypnosis is not a way of knowing what did or did not happen to you. (Both regression and affect/somatic bridging can also be used to find resources and do other things, of course.)

Note that having experienced trauma, crying, and even wanting to feel difficult feelings, are not necessarily signs that you need more support than working on your own. You get to pick how wide and narrow a range you want to work in, but be aware that different people need different ranges. Pay attention to how you respond and what support you have. Feeling like you are handling it isn't a substitute for having support.

Note that many guidelines for treating major trauma recommend one or more of trauma-focused variants of cognitive behavioral therapy (sometimes called cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure, eye movement desensitization and reprocessing, etc). Depending on the guideline and your situation, it may recommend one or the other or both. These can include self-hypnosis and inner child work. If you're working with high-risk trauma, you need a bigger container.

Always keep in mind that your functioning in ordinary life is the feedback. If you find yourself more loving, more honest, more functional, more relational, more grounded, more precise, etc. than when you started this process, keep on keeping on. If you notice that your ordinary life is becoming less stable, don't try to go deeper. Go for more sleep, more eating, more grounding, more healthy boundaries with other people, more getting support from other people, etc.
