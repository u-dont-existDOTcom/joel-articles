PUBLISHED section:
[B1] The induction and inner meeting bring you to a place where you can listen. From here, learn to answer what happens instead of reciting a speech that has already decided what every part will say. Read these examples before practicing, then put the screen aside.

[B2] Notice one thing accurately. Offer a small, believable invitation, an act of care, or more time. Acknowledge only what actually happens, then wait. A sensation can lead to an image, a gesture, a feeling, or words; you don’t need to fill every category.

[B4] Suppose you notice, “My chest tightened when I imagined asking for help.” Start with that, rather than deciding you’ve found an abandoned child or a protective mechanism.

[B5] And as I notice that tightening, I can give myself a little time before deciding what it means … and notice whether there’s something I’d like to say, change, or simply stay with.

[B6] Then listen. That is enough language for one invitation. A plain “What happens next?” may be better than an elegant sentence. When a feeling is developing, pause the words if that gives it room; keep using them when they help you understand.

[B7] You can be firmer when that is the help you chose: “Let the explanation wait a moment. What do I feel as I say that?” Directness can focus attention without deciding the answer. A difficult feeling needn’t become pleasant before you can stay with it. When you remain present, can change direction, and want to continue, sadness or tension alone doesn’t require an exit.

[B8] Keep apart what you noticed, what it seemed to mean, and what you think about that meaning now. These can overlap; you don’t need to prove which came first. When useful, ask: “What did I notice? What did it seem to mean? What supports or challenges that interpretation?”

[B9] “My stomach tightened” reports a sensation. “That person is evil” is a conclusion—even if it arrived as a wordless certainty. Ask what they actually did, what cues you noticed, and what else could explain the alarm. A sentence you find now can help examine the meaning without being a sentence you thought then. Nonverbal doesn’t mean deeper or truer; earlier doesn’t mean more reliable. Feelings deserve attention, and their meanings remain open to analysis.

[B11] “My shoulders tightened” and “that wording feels wrong” are different observations. Tightening alone doesn’t explain itself. Ask what happened and whether the invitation fits. An explicit no needs no further justification; change the words, the pace, or the approach. Don’t repeat an unwelcome suggestion more persuasively.

[B12] I notice the tightness. I can leave it as it is for a moment. Does this invitation fit, or would I prefer something different?

[B13] Sometimes the sentence was simply wrong. Sometimes an unfamiliar possibility brings a feeling worth meeting. You don’t have to decide which before making space to find out. Skepticism is not automatically a protector, and silence is not proof that a hidden process is working.

[B15] When another person is involved, notice what they actually do before making the whole problem an inner-child reaction. Can they hear disagreement, take responsibility, and make room for your experience? Keep the judgment specific and revisable; disagreement or a boundary alone doesn’t establish emotional immaturity. You don’t need repeated exposure to danger before protecting yourself.

[B16] Notice your side too. Guilt doesn’t prove wrongdoing or manipulation. Did you cause harm that needs repair, abandon your position to make their disappointment go away, or both? Choose a realistic purpose for the next interaction. Sometimes it is mutual understanding; sometimes it is conveying information, saying no, or ending the exchange. You don’t have to make someone understand before you can act.

[CHANGED, replaces B1-B2]
Once you've read the induction and done the inner meeting, this will make more sense. Read through these, and then try doing it without looking at the screen.

Respond to each thing that comes up with an accurate notice and a small reasonable invitation (or act or taking more time etc). The 'thing' might be a sensation, image, gesture, feeling, words, or anything. The notice and invitation don't need to take all those forms, just notice and invite what happens.

[CHANGED, replaces B4-B9]
An example notice might be, "I notice my chest tightening when I imagine asking for help". Don't add interpretation like "this must be the abandoned child part of me, or a protective mechanism" - just notice what you notice. An example invitation might be, "And as I notice that, I can take some time to decide whether that's accurate or not, and notice if I want to say anything, or change it, or just stay like that".

Then just listen. You don't need fancy language - often it will be better to just ask what happens next, or whatever. If you notice a feeling developing, it might be best not to say anything while it's developing (if you can help it) but somedays you need to talk to understand, so just do that.

You can be pretty straightforward when you say you're not going to do something that isn't helping - "That's not helping, I'm not going to do that anymore." can be a perfectly valid thing to say to yourself, and help you focus. It's ok to just be with an unpleasant feeling, you don't have to wait till it's a nice one before you know you're ok. As long as you're ok, and willing to change if you want to, and happy to carry on, a sad feeling or tension or whatever doesn't mean it's not ok.

It's worth trying to separate the notice, the meaning, and your thoughts on the meaning, but in practice it will often be pretty fuzzy. When you can, it can be helpful to separate it out like that though - "I noticed... this seemed to mean... here's why that might be true / false" or something like that.

Is this a notice or meaning? "My stomach went tight" is a notice, "He's a complete bastard" is a meaning, even if you didn't think it in words. You can then go on to find out what he did, notice what it was that gave you the idea, and consider if there might be other explanations. It's also ok to use phrases that you didn't think in your head - if something comes up that you're analysing, it's still good to analyse, even if it's not in your head already. Nonverbal thoughts and early thoughts aren't necessarily any better or worse than other thoughts. Feelings are good to pay attention to, but it's still good to consider what they mean.

[CHANGED, replaces B11-B13]
If you notice something like your shoulders tightening in response to an invitation, that's different from saying "that's not quite right". The first one doesn't tell you why - it's worth considering whether it fits, but if you get a clear 'no' then you don't need any more reason to change what you're doing. If something doesn't fit, change it - don't keep trying the same thing.

"I notice the tightness, and I can just leave it like that. I wonder if that fits, or if I'd rather do something different?"

Sometimes it just isn't right - sometimes it's something you hadn't thought of. Neither is a protector, and just being silent doesn't mean there's a process.

[CHANGED, replaces B15-B16]
If there is another person involved in the situation, it may be a good idea to look at what they are doing as well. Are they able to communicate in a mature fashion? Try and be specific in your description, and be willing to alter it if you find out that it was incorrect. Just because someone is disagreeing with you or trying to set boundaries around you does not mean they are in their emotionally immature parts. You don’t need to subject yourself to something that may be dangerous to determine if this is the case.

On the other hand, you may find that you have acted inappropriately and you feel guilty. Having feelings of guilt does not mean that you have done something wrong or that you have been manipulated. Have you done something that was wrong or that caused harm? If so, what can you do to fix it? On the other hand, did you give in to someone else just to keep them from being disappointed in you? What do you want to do or achieve the next time you interact with this person? Do you need them to understand what you are feeling/thinking? Do you just need to communicate information to them? Do you need to set boundaries with them? Do you need to end the interaction prematurely?
