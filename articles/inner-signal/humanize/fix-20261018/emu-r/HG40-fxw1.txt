Appendix K: Sources and further reading

The personal accounts and the way I arrange this practice are mine. The re-vised teaching brings them together with the body orientated and hypnotic approaches described below. That combination is an adaptation; I do not claim that it has been validated as a whole treatment. These notes are for readers who want to follow an idea further; you don’t need them open while practicing.

Learning, entering, and returning

Maggie Phillips and Claire Frederick in Healing the Divided Self (1995), pp 24-27, 45-50, 83-90, deal with some aspects of interactive hypnosis such as helping the subject learn how to use other senses in the learning process, developing self-hypnosis and a definite return at the end. It is a professional clinical book, not a self-help one, so be careful about trying to introduce the case procedures described to yourself. The same goes for its historical claims: don't bring them wholesale into solo practice. Adam Eason, with Benjamin Parris, has an ISH article on self-hypnosis, dealing with self-directed learning and the question of another hypnotist and recordings.

Indirect language and re-entry cues

Phillips and Frederick's book, pages 53 - 57 and 96 - 100, explains pacing, utilization, metaphor and associational cues. Nimja's tutorial on hypnotic language patterns and the tutorial on triggers describe language and cueing techniques. They're sources for technique, not evidence that every NLP claim is established or that every example suits trauma work. Nimja did not write or endorse this guide. The original wording here, and the re-entry sequence I chose on purpose, are my adaptations.

Body awareness and changing the amount of contact

Note: Parts of this post draw on In an Unspoken Voice by Peter Levine, 2010, chapters 5 and 7, but also from chapters 3 and 4 of Freedom from Pain by Levine and Maggie Phillips, 2012, and chapter 4 of Healing Trauma by Levine. They discuss key ideas of bodily tracking, resources, titration, pendulation and the range of foci of attention, from sensation, imagery, behavior, emotion to meaning. Also, although movements and images are offered for trying out here, they are not offered with the presumption that this is what is “fixing” anything physiologically.

Inner-child care and adult capacities

There is a lot more information on borrowed adulthood, love without having to trust, practical and relational repair, spiritual struggle, working on differentiation, mutual peer support, etc. in the companion piece: Inner Child Self-Love Reparenting Guide. The matching practices here follow it, but keep this guide’s self-hypnosis entry, responsive language and complete return.

The information here is based on Healing the Divided Self pages 148-153 and 171-174 for renurturing work, somatic work, developing nurturing capacities etc. For information on attunement and how each child is different and needs to be responded to differently, see Trauma Through a Child’s Eyes by Levine and Maggie Kline, particularly chapters 4, 5, 10 which talk about actual kids. Applying those ideas to an adult’s younger feeling states is an adaptation. My arrangement of three adult functions and the borrowed-adulthood sequence are not those authors’ tested protocol.

Inner speech and putting experience into words

It’s worth noting at this point that Nedergaard and Lupyan’s study, Not Everybody Has an Inner Voice (2024), was not investigating different adaptations of self hypnosis, but rather it was investigating people who reported having different amounts of inner speech. Thus, the questions in Quick start are not intended to be any sort of validated screening or test for ability to do self hypnosis, but merely a practical self-check.

Memory, support, and choosing a practitioner

Chapter 7 of Peter Levine's "Trauma and Memory" (2015) is useful on the difference between a compelling experience and historical verification. The US Veterans Administration website has an overview of hypnosis that includes some cautions about strong reactions and the potential of false memory creation. Here is a website from the American Society of Clinical Hypnosis (ASCH) that explains what hypnosis certification means. It separates professional preparation from a guarantee that someone is competent. The links already in the evidence and specialist sections point to their particular claims; they don’t validate every use of hypnosis.
