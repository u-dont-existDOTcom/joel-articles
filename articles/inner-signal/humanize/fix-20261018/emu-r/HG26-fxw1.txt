When this needs more support than a guide can provide

Self-hypnosis is a relationship you have with yourself, and when the situation needs actual care, this guide shouldn’t stand in for it.

Get urgent help now if you’re in immediate danger, severely confused, unable to keep yourself safe, or experiencing physical symptoms like chest pain or fainting. If you’re experiencing new or worsening suicidal thoughts, severe insomnia, or symptoms of mania or psychosis, or if your dissociative symptoms are impacting your functioning, please get them assessed by a professional promptly, rather than trying to fix them with this 4- to 12-week practice.

On the other hand, you may need very little more support. Start small—do shorter practices, make them warm, choose a small and tractable subject to work with, make sure you’re coming all the way back out of hypnosis, and track the effect on one target area (anxiety, daydreaming, repairing a relationship, an avoided task, sleep, emotional regulation, intuitive accuracy, etc.) for a week or two. If you find that your functioning worsens or you’re gaining certainty about memories that can’t be verified, then it’s time to reassess, not to push harder.

Get the right kind of ongoing help if you’re dealing with recurring panic attacks, compulsive behaviors, substance-related destabilization, or major relationship instability.

Methods to explore with a trained person present

If you want to do deliberate trauma-focused regression, to follow an affect or somatic bridge to unknown traumatic origins, or to work with substantial flashbacks or dissociative reactions, work with a trauma-informed trained clinician who is trained in the method you’re using and who is present while you do it. (Affect and somatic bridges mean following associations from an emotional or bodily feeling; regression means remembering or imagining what it would have been like when you were younger. Both can have other uses too, like finding resources.) Ask what they intend to do and how they’ll handle uncertainty and unexpected reactions. Even if you work with a clinician, please note that hypnosis can’t prove what happened in your life.

Having a history of trauma, crying, or even wanting to feel difficult feelings, doesn’t necessarily mean you need more support than working on your own. It is up to you to find how wide or narrow a range to work in, and that will be different for different people. Watch how you actually respond and what support you have. Feeling confident doesn’t make up for support you don’t have.

Major trauma guidelines emphasize treatments like trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy variants, cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure, or eye movement desensitization and reprocessing, depending on the person and the guideline. Self-hypnosis and inner child work can be part of that process. If you’re working with high-risk trauma, you need a bigger container for that work.

The ultimate feedback on whether your process is working well is how you’re functioning in ordinary life. If you’re being more loving, honest, functional, relational, grounded, and precise than you were before you started, then keep going, gently. If ordinary life is getting less stable, then stop trying to go deeper and focus instead on getting enough sleep, eating, grounding yourself, maintaining healthy boundaries with others, and getting support from others.
