When “your voice” is everybody else’s voice

Working with your inner child can be harder when your private self had very little room to develop. This can happen when people come from a background where their family, religion or community took over their life very early on, or they were pressured to achieve in a certain way at a young age. This means that you don’t know what you feel, what you want, what you believe, what you like, or what you don’t like until you hear what someone else says first. This makes it hard to follow the advice to “love yourself” when you don’t know who “yourself” is. So in these cases, reparenting can go hand in hand with finding yourself. For some, it’s easy to work with their child-self because they can feel their child-self really easily. For others, creating enough safety for preference, curiosity, and experimentation is part of how their child-self becomes easier to hear.

You might be in this camp if:

You don’t know what you want until you hear what someone else wants

You find that your opinions change dramatically depending on who is around

You agree with someone in the moment and later realize you didn’t agree

You tend to take responsibility for other people’s emotions to the point of erasing yourself

Trying to love yourself feels like a performance

Trying to find out how your inner child feels, results in guesses instead of feeling

You find that you can rebel, but you can’t choose

A useful way to explain this is that you may have had less room to explore “what do I like?”, “what do I want to try out?” or “who am I when nobody is grading me?”, because as a child your attention went into avoiding pain, reading danger, meeting expectations and keeping the bond. (That’s a practical model, not a claim about one specific brain system.)

Sometimes it helps to distance yourself for a certain period from the people or group that you are getting your answers from, so quieter preferences can surface. However, don’t do this for long (staying isolated would defeat the purpose) – just long enough to differentiate what is yours from what is social.

The difference between a “human doer” and a “human being” helps here. If you don’t feel like you are good enough just because you exist, you will try to create a sense of worth by being beautiful, by doing something well, by being smart, by gaining status, by being spiritual, or by being needed. These can all be real gifts. They turn into a bargain with yourself when failing at them feels like losing yourself – that as long as you are good at something (or good at being good for other people), you will be good enough to belong.

To help you find yourself, ask yourself these questions:

What do I like when no one is watching?

What do I believe before I check what other people think?

What do I want that is not in rebellion to others, and is not in compliance with others?

Where am I buying my sense of belonging by betraying myself?

What “arena” am I using to try to buy my sense of belonging?

What can I try on for size that I don’t need to impress anyone with?

What relationships allow me to be myself?

What do I notice in my body and what meaning am I attaching to it?

Give your inner child a small experiment with no value towards your resume – painting, dancing, gardening, learning a language, a sport, volunteering, playing music, or anything else you’re allowed to be bad at. The parent voice you inherited may be telling you that you are supposed to be good at something as soon as you start doing it. The adult can answer, “We’re finding out whether we enjoy this, not proving our worth.” And give it enough tries to get past the humiliation of being a beginner.

For a child, belonging can feel like survival. So families, religions, romantic relationships, political tribes, spiritual groups, institutions, and even online communities can reach astonishingly far into who you are. Part of “adulting” is finding out where you are trading your sense of self in exchange for belonging, and then consciously choosing where you want to belong – on your terms, and learning that you can disappoint someone without disappearing. A large part of your subconscious mind is made up of the voices of other people, which is why this belongs in a self-hypnosis guide.

Where am I betraying myself in exchange for belonging?

What would it feel like to belong without having to betray myself?

Is this my own guidance or a belonging program I adopted?

Is this love, or is it a fear of being excluded?

Is this my conscience or is it conditioning?

Is this devotion, or is it compliance?

Is this rebellion, or is it freedom?

Then see what your body tells you. It isn’t always right, but it deserves a seat at the hearing.
