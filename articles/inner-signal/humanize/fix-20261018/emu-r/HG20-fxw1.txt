Let the next response guide you

Having read the induction and participated in the inner meeting, you are ready to learn to respond rather than read a speech that has already decided what every part will say. Read the following examples before you practice without a screen.

Respond by giving an accurate noticing of one thing and a small, believable invitation (or an act of care, or more time). A sensation can lead to an image, a gesture, a feeling, or words. It doesn’t have to fill every one of those. Then acknowledge only what actually happens, and wait.

Finding your own words

Say you notice, "My chest tightened when I imagined asking for help." Start with that,

rather than deciding, "This must be an abandoned child / a protective mechanism." Then you might say: "And as I notice that tightening, I can give myself a little time before deciding what it means … and notice whether there’s something I’d like to say, change, or simply stay with."

Then listen. That’s enough language for one invitation. A plain "What happens next?" may be better than an elegant sentence. While a feeling is developing, it may be best to pause the words if that gives it room, and somedays the words help you understand, so keep them.

When that’s the help you chose, you can be quite firm with yourself: "Let the explanation wait a moment. What do I feel as I say that?" Being direct can focus your attention without deciding the answer. You can simply stay with a difficult feeling, rather than waiting until it has become pleasant. If you’re still present, can change direction, and want to continue, sadness or tension on its own doesn’t mean you have to stop. It can be ok to stay.

Keep apart "What I notice", "What it seems to mean to me", and "What I think about what it seems to mean." The boundaries between the three are fuzzy, and you don’t need to prove which came first. When it helps, ask: "What did I notice? What did it seem to mean? What supports or challenges that meaning?"

Is it a notice or a meaning? "It made my stomach tight" is a notice. "That person is evil" is a meaning. (even if it came as a wordless certainty). Ask what they actually did, what cues you noticed, and what else could explain the alarm. It's ok to use phrases you come up with later to analyze the meaning, not only phrases that appeared in your head at the time. Nonverbal or early thoughts are not necessarily more true or valuable. Feelings deserve attention, and what they mean is still open to analysis.

Let the response change the invitation

There is a difference between "my shoulders tightened" and "that wording feels wrong". Tightening on its own doesn't explain itself: ask what happened, and whether the invitation fits. A clear no doesn't need any more reasons; change the words, the pace, or the approach, and don't repeat the unwelcome suggestion more persuasively.

I notice the tightness. I can leave it as it is for a moment. Does this invitation fit, or would I prefer something different?

Sometimes the sentence was simply wrong. Sometimes it's something I hadn't considered, and the feeling is worth meeting. I don't have to decide which before I make space to find out. Skepticism isn't automatically a protector, and silence isn't proof that some hidden process is working.

Look at the situation too

When another person is involved, notice what they actually do before you make the whole problem an inner-child reaction. Can they hear disagreement, take responsibility, and make room for your experience? Be as specific as you can, and be ready to change your mind. Just “disagreeing” with you or setting boundaries doesn’t by itself show emotional immaturity. And you don’t need repeated exposure to a dangerous situation before you’re allowed to protect yourself.

Notice your side too. Guilt does not necessarily mean that you have done something wrong or were manipulated. Did you cause harm that needs repair, or did you drop your position to make their disappointment go away, or both? What’s a realistic aim for the next interaction? Sometimes it is mutual understanding but sometimes you need to just convey information, set boundaries, or cut the interaction short. You don’t have to make someone understand before you can act.

Change the amount of contact

When you need less, widen your attention to something easier: the room, the chair, a sound, or somewhere in your body that’s easier to meet. Let that support become noticeable alongside what is difficult for you. Going back and forth between what is difficult and what is easier is called pendulation. Doing a little bit at a time is called titration. You get to set the pace based on how you are doing. You don’t have to do a certain number.

Ok, I can let this much be here, and notice my support beside it.

Only come back to the difficult stuff when you choose to. If you find that you are losing track of the present, things are becoming more and more unreal for you, or you are unable to switch or stop what you are doing, then stop exploring within and use the full return. If you find that you are having trouble orienting for a prolonged period, then please get human help, not a stronger induction.

If you feel the urge to move, turn, reach, push, or change your body position, then feel free to either do something small that is not painful or uncomfortable to your body or imagine that you are doing it, and notice what follows. You do not need to act out the urge or feel that you must move, shake, or release. If you find that moving causes pain or symptoms that you are concerned about, then stop moving.

Offer the care that is actually wanted

When a younger feeling shows up, I offer it company without making it literally younger or asking it to explain its history. If I can recognize who the protector is, I listen to its concern, without demanding that it step aside. All are welcome in the meeting, and I don’t need them to go away, or even trust me right now.

I’m here for you if you want me to be. If you need some space, I can provide that also.

If they don’t want me to come closer, I just do what they ask, and see how that feels. If I notice that a Nurturer could be useful in changing the tone, or a Protector could help me set a boundary, or a Leader could help me see how to live my life better, they don’t each need a speech, I just borrow the function that I don’t have at that moment. (I only use the Witness if I need help getting started, otherwise I don’t re-do a step I already can do.)

I try different ways of supporting them – a welcome hand placement, an imagined presence, words they’d like to hear, changing my body position, moving further away etc., one change at a time, to see what it does. A resource that sounds comforting only earns its place if it actually helps right now.

Getting care now can bring up grief for what was missing back then. That doesn’t mean the love is failing. Allow yourself to respond as long as you want and as long as you can ‘stay there’. More tears don’t prove more healing. If the voice, closeness, touch, image, etc. seems intrusive then change it. Don’t think that you can’t ‘take’ the love.

Let a metaphor develop before you decide what it means. You could say, “If this problem had its own image or story, what would show up?” and, “what wants to happen next?” Try out ‘doing something’ that you feel would give you the resource that you feel you are missing, or set a boundary, move away, or take an action, only as far as it seems useful. Keep in mind that this information is symbolic and might be a good hypothesis for what you need but it’s not a proven fact about your history.

Or spend the session developing something you want: love, play, courage or curiosity. Let a pleasant experience have time to grow. Rehearse something you’ve decided to do, and notice what it would take to actually do it. Hypnosis can invite something new, not only listen to what’s already there.

Finish without forcing a revelation

Once you feel you've had enough, you can stop asking yourself new questions. However, it is a good idea to explore whether there's anything you can do to help you to leave things where they are for the time being. This might include thanking the parts or capacities you've been working with, in words you can actually mean. A meal, a boundary, rest, a repaired relationship or a promise kept may matter more than another insight. Or, it may be that there's nothing you need to do.

Throughout this exercise, your analytical mind is welcome to comment on what's going on, to not go along with something, or to stop the process. However, it's best to postpone any long checking of the facts, and any commitments that matter, until after you've returned. An image can be meaningful without being a historical memory, and just because you've seen an image or experienced a shift or release in your body doesn't mean that your interpretation of what's going on is correct.

Even if you ended the process early, use the Come all the way back procedure to return. Afterwards, write a few lines: what was there before, what you tried, what happened, and what's still uncertain. Include any memorable phrase that came up, any signals from your body, any needs expressed by a child part, any concerns expressed by a protector part, any boundaries you set, any repairs you made, or anything you learned that it would be a mistake to try to force, but only what actually appeared. You don't owe the notebook a complete cast of characters.
