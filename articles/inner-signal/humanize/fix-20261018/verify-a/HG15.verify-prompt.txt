PUBLISHED section:
[B0] When “your voice” is everybody else’s voice

[B1] Sometimes the inner-child question lands nowhere because a private self had very little room to develop. In controlling families, rigid religions, enmeshed communities, or high-pressure achievement systems, performance can begin so early that a person doesn’t know what they feel, want, believe, or prefer until somebody else tells them first. Then “love yourself” produces a reasonable question: love whom? Reparenting and identity-building can proceed together. For some people the child-self is already easy to feel. For others, creating enough safety for preference, curiosity, and experimentation is part of how that self becomes easier to hear.

[B2] This may apply when:

[B3] You do not know what you want unless someone else says what they want first.

[B4] Your opinions shift dramatically depending on who is in the room.

[B5] You agree in the moment and later realize you did not agree.

[B6] You feel responsible for other people’s emotions to the point of self-erasure.

[B7] Self-love feels performative.

[B8] The question “What does my inner child feel?” produces guesses, not felt answers.

[B9] You can rebel, but you cannot yet choose.

[B10] A useful developmental explanation is that when childhood is organized around avoiding pain and preserving attachment, attention goes into reading danger, meeting expectations, and keeping the bond. That can leave less room for the ordinary experiments that answer, “What do I like? What do I want to try? Who am I when nobody is grading me?” This is a practical model, not a claim about one specific brain system.

[B11] Sometimes a temporary, intentional period of distance from the people or group supplying the answers helps quieter preferences surface. Use that distance only long enough to tell your own signal from the social echo; prolonged isolation would undermine the purpose.

[B12] The difference between a human doer and a human being helps here. When worth isn’t felt internally, it gets earned through beauty, performance, intellect, status, spirituality, or becoming indispensable to everybody else. Those can all be real gifts. They become belonging bargains when failure in the arena feels like losing yourself.

[B13] Useful questions:

[B14] “What do I like when no one is watching?” “What do I believe before I check whether others approve?” “What do I want that is neither rebellion nor compliance?” “Where am I buying belonging with self-betrayal?” “Which arena am I using to earn the right to belong?” “What would I try if nobody needed to be impressed?” “Which relationships let me remain myself?” “What am I noticing in my body, and what meaning am I giving it?”

[B15] Give the inner child a small experiment with no résumé value: painting, dancing, gardening, a language, a sport, volunteering, music, or anything else you’re allowed to be bad at. The inherited parent may demand immediate talent. The adult can answer, “We’re finding out whether we enjoy this, not proving our worth.” Give it enough repetitions to get past the humiliation of being a beginner.

[B16] For a child, belonging can feel like survival, so families, religions, romances, political tribes, spiritual groups, institutions, and online communities can reach astonishingly far into identity. Adulting means noticing where you buy belonging with self-betrayal, choosing your own ground, and learning that you can disappoint someone without disappearing. The subconscious contains a crowd of borrowed voices, which is why this belongs in a self-hypnosis guide.

[B17] “Where do I become less true in order to remain included?”

[B18] “What would belonging look like if I did not have to disappear?”

[B19] “Is this guidance, or is this a belonging program?” “Is this love, or is this fear of exclusion?” “Is this conscience, or is this conditioning?” “Is this devotion, or is this compliance?” “Is this rebellion, or is this freedom?”

[B20] Then wait for the body. It isn’t infallible, but it deserves a seat at the hearing.

[B0, as published] When “your voice” is everybody else’s voice

[CHANGED, replaces B1-B20]
Working with your inner child can be harder when your private self had very little room to develop. This happens a lot when people come from a background where their family, religion or community took over their life very early on, or they were pressured to achieve in a certain way at a young age. This means that you don’t know who you are, what you feel, what you want, what you believe, what you like, or what you don’t like until you hear what someone else says first. This makes it hard to follow the advice to “love yourself” when you don’t know who “yourself” is. So in these cases, reparenting can go hand in hand with finding yourself. For some, it’s easy to work with their child-self because they can feel their child-self really easily. For others, it requires creating safety and playing around more to feel their child-self.

You might be in this camp if:

You don’t know what you want until you hear what someone else wants

You find that your opinions change dramatically depending on who is around

You agree with someone in the moment and later realize you didn’t agree

You tend to take responsibility for other people’s emotions to the point of erasing yourself

Trying to love yourself feels like a performance

Trying to find out how your inner child feels, results in guesses instead of feeling

You find that you can rebel, but you can’t choose

A useful way to explain this is that you didn’t have the room to explore “what do I like?”, “what do I want to try out?” or “who am I when nobody is grading me?”, because as a child your attention went into avoiding pain, reading danger, meeting expectations and keeping the bond. (That’s a practical model, not a claim about one specific brain system.)

Sometimes it is necessary to distance yourself for a certain period from the people or group that you are getting your answers from so that you can distinguish between what you want and what you are told to want. However, don’t do this for long (staying isolated would defeat the purpose) – just long enough to differentiate what is yours from what is social.

This means moving from a “human doer” to a “human being”. If you don’t feel like you are good enough just because you exist, you will try to create a sense of worth by being beautiful, by doing something well, by being smart, by gaining status, by being spiritual, or by being needed. These can all be real gifts. They turn into a bargain with yourself when failing at them feels like losing yourself – that as long as you are good at something (or good at being good for other people), you will be good enough to belong. And if you fall short in that arena, you feel that you are not good enough.

To help you find yourself, ask yourself these questions:

What do I like when no one is watching?

What do I believe before I check what other people think?

What do I want that is not in rebellion to others, and is not in compliance with others?

Where am I buying my sense of belonging by betraying myself?

What “arena” am I using to try to buy my sense of belonging?

What can I try on for size that I don’t need to impress anyone with?

What relationships allow me to be myself?

What do I notice in my body and what meaning am I attaching to it?

Remember that your inner child needs to play! Try out some things that have no value towards your resume – painting, dancing, gardening, learning a language, a sport, volunteering, playing music, or anything else you’re allowed to be bad at. The parent voice you inherited may be telling you that you are supposed to be good at something as soon as you start doing it. Your job as your own parent is to remind yourself that you are not doing these things to prove your worth – you are doing them to see if you like them or not. And give it enough tries to get past the humiliation of being a beginner.

For a child, belonging can feel like survival. So families, religions, romantic relationships, political tribes, spiritual groups, institutions, and even online communities can reach astonishingly far into who you are. Part of “adulting” is finding out where you are trading your sense of self in exchange for belonging, and then consciously choosing where you want to belong – on your terms, and learning that you can disappoint someone without disappearing. A large part of your subconscious mind is made up of the voices of other people, which is why this belongs in a self-hypnosis guide.

Where am I betraying myself in exchange for belonging?

What would it feel like to belong without having to betray myself?

Is this my own guidance or a belonging program I adopted?

Is this love, or is it a fear of being excluded?

Is this my conscience or is it conditioning?

Is this devotion, or is it compliance?

Is this rebellion, or is it freedom?

Then see what your body tells you. It isn’t always right, but it deserves a seat at the hearing.
