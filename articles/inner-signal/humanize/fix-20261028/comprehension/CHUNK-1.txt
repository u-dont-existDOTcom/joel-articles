# Introduction

Hello Lovey Bunny!

You were born with a mind that can dream, protect, analyze, imitate, love, remember, hide, reveal, and heal. Nobody taught its different layers how to speak to each other.

Instead, most of us learned to push through, suppress emotion, perform confidence, obey or rebel against authority, and call that adulthood. Meanwhile, the deeper mind kept running its own programs: the child wanting safety, the guard expecting danger, the body remembering what the intellect had explained away, the intuition speaking before language, and the fantasy trying to become guidance.

A lot of hypnosis teaching treats the subconscious like a machine waiting for commands. That never did much for me. I get more from sitting down and listening until the scared kid, the guard, the body, the intuition, and the part trying to run Tuesday can all be heard without one of them seizing the microphone.

In this guide, self-hypnosis gathers attention until the conscious mind, subconscious, body, inner child, protectors, adult self, and Higher Self can participate in one honest inner meeting.

The method is simple: listen, warm, protect, clarify, reality-test, and act.

A dramatic trance is optional. The goal is a more trustworthy relationship with the whole mind, proven in ordinary life.

Communion is the closest word I have for that. The conscious mind stops shouting from the roof, and the subconscious no longer has to communicate only through symptoms, dreams, cravings, daydreams, and peculiar little body signals.

I treat the subconscious as a living ecology of parts, memories, impulses, images, protections, and wisdom. I bring in inner-child reparenting because a wounded young part won’t be repaired by a clever suggestion. I begin with Higher Self or the universal heart when the system needs coherence, and separate into parts only when that helps. I borrow from prompt engineering because the shape of a question changes the kind of answer we can hear. And I include influence literacy because hypnosis is hardly the only thing hypnotizing us.

# Quick start: do this first

Gather attention, notice what is here, offer one fitting response, wait, then come all the way back. You can meet a difficulty or develop love, curiosity, play, or confidence. Nothing dramatic has to happen.

Before the first session, read the induction and full return below while awake. Set aside time somewhere physically secure. Choose one purpose and what you are leaving alone today. Then put the screen aside; the practice is something you learn to conduct, not a checklist to keep reading.

As you listen, a sensation, image, phrase, feeling, objection, or silence may become noticeable. Let the next invitation follow that response. Use warmth, a boundary, direction, or more time as needed. Finish with the complete return, then note what happened and any real-world action it calls for.

When you become panicky, disoriented, increasingly unreal, or less able to function, stop deepening and return to the room and appropriate support. A difficult feeling you freely choose to stay with while present is a different situation.

## Do you usually think in words?

Before choosing a script, notice how words actually work in your mind. Do silent words or phrases arise often, occasionally, rarely, or hardly at all? They needn’t sound like an audible voice. Does this vary when you plan, remember, reflect, or feel upset? Can you deliberately form a silent sentence, even if words seldom arrive on their own?

That is different from feeling something before words come in a particular moment. Neither question requires deciding that feelings always come first. “Not sure” is a useful answer; this is a way to choose workable instructions, not a diagnosis.

Read “say,” “ask,” and “listen” in this guide as invitations, not a requirement for an inner narrator. Speak aloud, write, use a recording, or follow the invitation without rehearsing a sentence. The child and protector needn’t speak in sentences either. Don’t assume an image must appear instead. Try whatever is accessible, and change the route when it stops helping.

# What I mean by hypnosis

APA Division 30 commonly defines hypnosis as focused attention, reduced peripheral awareness, and increased responsiveness to suggestion. That’s basically true, but there’s a lot more than meets the eye. In fact, hypnosis is such a broad and common type of altered state that it’s hard to confine it to one use. But I’m using hypnosis here as a therapy.

And hypnosis as a therapy is as old as time itself. While the modern technique of it was discovered by Franz Anton Mesmer in the 1770s, shamans have been using dancing, music, and drugs to induce hypnosis for therapeutic purposes forever. We enter into trance states every night which resemble hypnosis as we go to sleep and as we wake up as well. Those states can also be used for deep therapy.

# Protecting Yourself from Hypnotists

Hypnotism can happen in a therapist’s office. It also happens through music, marketing, religious ritual, sexual chemistry, charismatic authority, social media, grief, fear, political messaging, belonging, fatigue, love, trauma, and beauty.

Influence doesn’t wait for the conscious mind to sign a form. Marketers and interface designers know this perfectly well. The FTC’s report on dark patterns describes designs that trick or manipulate consumers, while the OECD describes commercial patterns that steer, deceive, coerce, or manipulate people toward choices that may work against their interests.

Conscious agreement is one thing. Unconscious receptivity is another. Emotional compliance is another. Legal and ethical consent is another. A mind can be influenced without the adult self having clearly chosen the influence.

A line associated with the Silva Mind Control tradition says it plainly:

“If you don’t learn to control your mind, someone else will!”

Advertising, fear, craving, screens, tribe, resentment, authority, loneliness, and repetition are already training the mind. Self-hypnosis lets you practice from inside it: befriending the deeper layers, listening closely, and making adult choice more available.

# Affirmations Done Right

My dad always said: “Always, and in everything, expect the very best. But be prepared to take what you get. The best might come afterwards.” That’s a better affirmation than what most people think of as affirmation, because it’s not telling you something which you know to be false in the present. If you say “I am beautiful” but you know that’s not true, then you’re challenging your beliefs, and if you’re challenging your beliefs you should have some argument or reason why they are wrong, otherwise it’s bullying.

Another quote I use all the time from my dad… when someone asks me “How’s it going?” I say “Better & better!” This really confuses a lot of people. Some think it’s amazing that I’m always doing better. Some ask if I was sick or something. But it’s doing multiple jobs. On the one hand, it’s deflecting from the fake question because most people don’t actually want to hear how you are doing in detail anyway. On the other hand, more importantly, it’s reprogramming your mind to remove you from tunnel vision. You might be sad now, but if you say, “I am sad” then you’re subtly identifying with the sadness and it keeps you stuck. It’s best to say, “I’m feeling sad now, but I’m doing better and better, every day, in every way!” That’s more like a mission statement than a historical analysis. Although you can also re-evaluate your history as lessons to make that true.

With the intentional correction, you don’t have to be unconsciously led astray but your random wrong thoughts.

# Hypnosis Evidence

Hypnosis has real evidence, although it doesn’t have magical evidence. A 2024 overview of 49 meta-analyses, covering 261 distinct primary studies, found the strongest support in pain, medical procedures, and work with children and adolescents. The quality still varied by outcome and study design.

Self-hypnosis has its own evidence. A systematic review of randomized controlled trials reported benefits in studies involving pain, childbirth, pediatric applications, stress, and anxiety. It appeared more useful when people learned it as a real self-directed skill and practiced it several times, rather than merely pressing play on an audio file.

That distinction matters here. I want you to learn an inner skill, not become a consumer of increasingly elaborate trance recordings.

Clinical caution still matters. Mayo Clinic warns that hypnosis used around stressful earlier-life events can trigger strong emotional reactions, and that other treatments may fit historical trauma work better.

Use self-hypnosis for regulation, dialogue, reparenting, clarity, habit change, present safety, emotional integration, and ordinary-life action.

Do not use it to force trauma memories, prove what happened, suppress medical symptoms, destabilize yourself, or replace appropriate clinical care.

# Forget the left-brain/right-brain cartoon

“Syncing the hemispheres” can be a useful description of how the state feels, but I wouldn’t turn it into literal neuroscience. A large resting-state fMRI study of 1,011 people didn’t support the pop-science picture of whole people being globally “left-brained” or “right-brained,” even though particular functions and networks can be lateralized.

You’re inviting the verbal, analytical, imaginal, emotional, somatic, intuitive, relational, and spiritual modes to cooperate. Nobody needs to win a hemisphere election.

The loving mind stays warm while the analytical mind stays bright. The intuitive mind offers signals without becoming dogma. The rational mind tests signals without killing them. The body gives yes, no, and not-yet. The imagination opens the field. The adult self chooses the next real action. Peace and precision become compatible.

Love can drift into fantasy without analysis. Analysis can become sterile without love. Intuition can become delusion when nothing tests it; testing can become dead when nothing intuitive is allowed to speak. Let the modes correct each other.

A powerful state matters only if something survives it: kinder behavior, more humility, better judgment, real service, or a more workable ordinary life.

An outside hypnotist can’t easily conduct that rapid back-and-forth because they aren’t inside the mind receiving the suggestion. A skilled guide can still notice blind spots, help regulate the process, and hold a safer container. Self-hypnosis simply has one advantage nobody outside can duplicate: immediate dialogue with the system itself.
