Mitochondrial fuels (glutamine, butyrate, TCA intermediates): your gut barrier needs a lot of energy, right there and right away, to keep its tight junctions intact. If your enterocytes (small intestine) or colonocytes (large intestine) are starved of the fuels their mitochondria prefer, the barrier physically falls apart, and lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and endotoxins leak through into your bloodstream.
L-Glutamine (watch Dr. Rhonda vid): the main fuel for enterocytes, and for activated immune cells too. It skips the complicated digestive steps and turns directly into alpha-ketoglutarate (α-KG) inside the mitochondria, where it powers the citric acid cycle (the TCA cycle). So the cells get that energy right away, and it drives the repair of the gut lining and prevents LPS from getting through. Take 5g a day as a baseline to counter the gut lining breakdown caused by high-intensity or high-volume training (like CrossFit or intense cardio). Work up to 10-30g a day during an acute immune exposure or high systemic stress, or if you're dealing with active IBD (Crohn's/Colitis).
CAUTION: If you have an active colon or liver cancer (the liver sees glutamine first, via the portal vein), don't titrate glutamine up to the high doses, since cancer cells multiply really fast and can hijack it to fuel that too.
Butyrate & SCFAs: the small intestine runs on glutamine, but the large intestine relies on short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate. You can get these through raw resistant starch fermentation, or from your home-brewed SIBO yogurt. They fuel your colonocytes and lock down the lower gut barrier, and they suppress inflammatory signals across the board.
TCA cycle intermediates (malic & citric acid): organic acids like malate and citrate feed directly into the same mitochondrial energy pathways as glutamine. Clean sour stuff like organic malic acid, tart cherries, green apples and so on gives your gut epithelial cells a rapid alternative fuel, which speeds up healing of the gut lining from a different biochemical angle.
