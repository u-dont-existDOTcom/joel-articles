Mitochondrial and cellular energy starvation is what you get when there's not enough of the fuels each type of cell in your gut prefers (glutamine and butyrate). Your gut barrier needs a lot of energy, to keep its tight junctions intact. If the cells lining your small intestine (called enterocytes) or the colonocytes lining your large intestine don't get that energy, the barrier loosens up. Then lipopolysaccharides (LPS, which come from the walls of bacteria) and other endotoxins leak through those (not-so-tight)-junction gaps into your bloodstream.

L-Glutamine (watch Dr. Rhonda vid) is the main fuel for your enterocytes, and for your immune cells when they're activated. It doesn't have to go through the complicated digestive steps other nutrients do. Inside the mitochondria, it gets converted into alpha-ketoglutarate, or α-KG, which then goes into the citric acid cycle, which is how the mitochondria make their energy. So that's why consuming pure glutamine gives energy to those cells faster than eating food, on top of what they get from their meals. They then use their extra energy to repair the gut lining by pulling the tight junctions together, and that keeps LPS from getting through. Glutamine also provides nitrogen to help build new cells.

Take 5g a day as a baseline, to keep your enterocytes fed. Take it away from meals, so it doesn't have to compete with the amino acids in your food to get absorbed. If you train hard or long, like CrossFit or intense cardio, your gut lining takes damage. While you train, your body sends blood away from your gut to your working muscles, so your enterocytes get less oxygen and fuel. Glutamine taken two hours before training helps with that. In a study of men running for an hour in the heat, their gut lining leaked less, even at the lowest dose tested, about 15g for a 70kg man. If your immune system is dealing with an acute exposure to toxins or pathogens, or your whole system is under a lot of stress, raise it to 10-30g a day, adding 1g a day in case you're sensitive to it. Up to 14g a day is known to be safe for the long run, but the studies of higher doses were too short to say, so come back down once it's over. If you have an active inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), like Crohn's or colitis, don't count on it. In the two small trials in active Crohn's, it didn't help.

CAUTION: a lot of cancers feed on glutamine too, to fuel their growth. So if you have an active cancer, check with your oncologist before you take the 5g, and especially before you go up to the 10-30g. Your enterocytes take in a lot of the glutamine you swallow as soon as it reaches them, but not all of it, and the rest gets into your blood, where a cancer can get to it. They can only take in so much at a time, so the bigger the dose, the more of it gets into your blood.

Your colonocytes are fueled by short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate. Your gut bacteria make SCFAs when they ferment raw resistant starch, and you get SCFAs directly from your home-brewed SIBO yogurt too. Besides feeding the colonocytes, SCFAs lock down the barrier in your lower gut, so dangerous substances can't escape from gut to blood, and your body can turn down the inflammatory signals it would otherwise need to contain the damage.

Some people will tell you to take malic and citric acid (which make green apples and tart cherries sour) as an extra quick energy source for your enterocytes. And they do supply quick energy, because they are made and used by that mitochondrial energy cycle, also called the TCA or Krebs cycle. But glutamine is still the main thing your enterocytes need, because it helps build new cells in addition to giving the needed energy. Those acids do help you absorb iron, even two or three times better according to one study where people ate rice with 1g pure malic acid or citric acid mixed in (Gillooly et al., 1983). That's probably because the acids help keep the iron dissolved so the gut can take it up. So something sour with an iron-rich meal is worth having anyway.
