- Mitochondrial and cellular energy starvation is what you get when there's not enough of the fuels each type of cell in your gut prefers (glutamine and butyrate). Your gut barrier needs a lot of energy, to keep its tight junctions intact. If the cells lining your small intestine (called enterocytes) or the colonocytes lining your large intestine don't do that, the barrier loosens up. Then lipopolysaccharides (LPS, which come from the walls of bacteria) and other endotoxins leak through those (not-so-tight)-junction gaps into your bloodstream.

- L-Glutamine (watch Dr. Rhonda vid) is the main fuel for your enterocytes, and for your immune cells when they're activated. It doesn't have to go through the complicated digestive steps other nutrients do. Inside the mitochondria, it gets turned straight into alpha-ketoglutarate, or α-KG. The α-KG goes into the citric acid cycle, which is how the mitochondria make their energy. So that's why consuming pure glutamine gives energy to those cells faster than eating food. They then use their extra energy to repair the gut lining by pulling the tight junctions together, and that keeps LPS from getting through. Glutamine also provides nitrogen to help build new cells.

- Take 5g a day as a baseline. The point of that baseline is to counter the breakdown of the gut lining that high-intensity or high-volume training causes, like CrossFit or intense cardio. If your immune system is dealing with an acute exposure, if your whole system is under a lot of stress, or if you have active IBD (Crohn's or colitis), raise it gradually to 10-30g a day.

- CAUTION: cancer cells that multiply really fast can grab glutamine too, to fuel their growth. So if you have an active colon or liver cancer, don't raise the dose that high. The liver is on that list because the blood from your gut goes straight there, through the portal vein, so a big dose of glutamine reaches your liver before anywhere else.

- The colonocytes lining your large intestine are fueled by short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like butyrate, while your small intestine runs on glutamine. Your gut bacteria make SCFAs when they ferment raw resistant starch, and you get SCFAs directly from your home-brewed SIBO yogurt too. Besides feeding the colonocytes, SCFAs lock down the barrier in your lower gut, preventing the dangerous escape of substances from gut to blood, which then turns down inflammatory signals that would be needed otherwise to contain the damage.

- Some people will tell you to take malic and citric acid (which make green apples and tart cherries sour) as an extra quick energy source for your enterocytes. And they do supply quick energy, because they are made and used by that mitochondrial energy cycle, also called the TCA or Krebs cycle. But glutamine is still the main thing they need, because it helps build new cells in addition to giving the needed energy. Those acids do help you absorb iron, even two or three times better according to one study where people ate rice with 1g pure malic acid or citric acid mixed in (Gillooly et al., 1983). That's probably because the acids help keep the iron dissolved so the gut can take it up. So something sour with an iron-rich meal is worth having anyway.
