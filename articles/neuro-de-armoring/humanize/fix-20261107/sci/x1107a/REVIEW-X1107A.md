## SB-37: Excitotoxicity is calcium overload, not cells "vibrating to death" (A claim corrected)

*B. The "Overheating" CPU: Glutamate Excitotoxicity, P3*

**Now:**

> The Mechanism: Excess Glutamate over-activates NMDA receptors, acting like a key stuck in the "On" position. This causes "excitotoxicity"--literally vibrating cells to death. Subjectively, this feels like anxiety, rage, or an "itchy brain."

**As it would read:**

> The Mechanism: Excess Glutamate over-activates NMDA receptors, the doors on brain cells that glutamate unlocks, acting like a key stuck in the "On" position, so too much calcium pours into the cells. In extreme cases, like a stroke or a brain injury, that calcium overload kills them, which is what "excitotoxicity" means (Sattler & Tymianski, 2001). The anxiety, rage or "itchy brain" you feel in a glutamate storm is more likely the over-excitement itself than a sign your neurons are dying.

**What changes:** Cells die because over-activated NMDA receptors let too much calcium in, and that happens in extreme cases like a stroke or a brain injury. The feelings stay, said as the over-excitement itself rather than neurons dying. NMDA receptors get a few words of explanation where they first come up in the section.

**Why:** Nothing vibrates: the cell is killed by the calcium the receptor lets in, and it matters in strokes, brain injury and neurodegeneration ([Sattler & Tymianski 2001](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1385/MN:24:1-3:107)). Nothing I found ties anxiety or rage to cells dying, and saying it does tells a reader their anxiety is brain damage.

**What stays:** The key stuck in the "On" position, the word excitotoxicity, and the anxiety, rage and "itchy brain".

**You're deciding:** Whether the paragraph says what the cell death really is, and keeps the feelings as over-excitement.

**My advice:** Approve. It also sets up the calcium for the Lock below.

## SB-38: GluD: a new finding, still argued over, and the armor part said as a guess (A claim corrected)

*B. The "Overheating" CPU: Glutamate Excitotoxicity, P4*

**Now:**

> The Signal Dial (GluD Receptors): Beyond the "voltage spike" of NMDA, Johns Hopkins researchers have recently discovered the brain uses GluD receptors as a master volume knob. While NMDA is the spark, GluD manages the baseline "hum" of the synapse. In an armored state, this dial is often jammed in the "High" position (Cerebellar Ataxia/Hyper-vigilance) or "Low" (Apathy/Schizotypy).

**As it would read:**

> The Signal Dial (GluD Receptors): So if NMDA is the spark, is anything else turning the signal up or down? Well according to a recent finding by Johns Hopkins researchers, GluD receptors, mainly known for helping build synapses, are also channels themselves, gates that let current into the cell, and what opens them is D-serine (one of the tools below) and GABA, the brain's main calming chemical, rather than glutamate (Wang, et al, 2025). Other researchers' tests the year before found no sign of that, so it's still being argued over (Itoh, et al, 2024). A mutation that causes cerebellar ataxia, a movement and balance disorder, leaves the channel leaking when it should be shut, and in schizophrenia GluDs seem to be less active than normal (Johns Hopkins, 2025). Whether an armored state jams it too, on "High" as hyper-vigilance or on "Low" as apathy and schizotypy (odd, schizophrenia-like thinking), nobody has tested, so that part is a hypothesis.

**What changes:** This starts from your version, the one that read Human ([your check](https://www.pangram.com/history/f2a4727d-a109-46d4-a907-40efa6350e96)): your "Well according to", the label and the High/Low ending stay. What changes is what the finding says (GluDs are channels, opened by D-serine and GABA, not a volume knob), that it is still disputed, the two known cases, and that the armor part is a guess nobody has tested. Your version had dropped the link to the news story; I put a citation back, to the paper itself, since the paragraph rests on it.

**Why:** The paper ([Wang et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12520249/)) found purified human GluD2 is a channel that D-serine and GABA open. A year earlier two labs tested the same idea and found no sign of direct gating ([Itoh et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11295041)), so it is new and contested. The known cases: an ataxia mutation makes the channel leak, and in schizophrenia GluDs are less active ([Johns Hopkins](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/news/newsroom/news-releases/2025/09/scientists-find-potential-new-drug-target-for-psychiatric-and-neurological-disorders)). So your speculation makes sense on the Low side (schizotypy, less active); on the High side the only known case is a movement disorder, so hyper-vigilance there is a guess with nothing under it yet. It also gives the section a better reason to mention GluD: D-serine, one of your tools, opens it.

**What stays:** Your opening question's place, your "Well according to", the label, and the High/Low with its four states.

**You're deciding:** Whether the paragraph says the finding as it is, with its dispute, and calls the armor part a hypothesis.

**My advice:** Approve. After that I humanize it from here, keeping your moves; it is longer than your version because the dispute and the two cases take room.

## SB-39: D-cycloserine is a different drug from D-serine, and its boost is small (A claim corrected)

*B. The "Overheating" CPU: Glutamate Excitotoxicity, P5*

**Now:**

> D-serine may support therapeutic memory updating through NMDA-dependent learning mechanisms, while also engaging GluD receptors that regulate synaptic activity/tone. The specific drug form studied is D-cycloserine. which has evidence as an exposure-therapy augmenter because it can facilitate NMDA-dependent consolidation of extinction learning. It does not erase fear directly; it appears to strengthen whatever learning occurs during or after the exposure session. This is why session quality matters: if the exposure produces corrective learning and ends with reduced fear or mastery, DCS (and possibly D-serine also) may help consolidate that new memory; if the exposure is overwhelming or ends badly, it may consolidate the wrong association.

**As it would read:**

> D-serine may help therapy update old memories, because it switches on a spot on the NMDA receptor that this kind of learning needs (Wolosker, et al, 1999), and it may also open the GluD channels. The drug that's actually been tested with exposure therapy (facing the feared thing in small, safe doses) is a different one, D-cycloserine (DCS), which works on that same spot, and across 21 trials with over 1,000 patients it gave exposure therapy a small boost (Mataix-Cols, et al, 2017). It does not erase fear directly; it appears to strengthen whatever learning occurs during or after the exposure session. This is why session quality matters: if the exposure produces corrective learning and ends with reduced fear or mastery, DCS (and possibly D-serine also) may help consolidate that new memory; if the exposure is overwhelming or ends badly, it may consolidate the wrong association (Smits, et al, 2013).

**What changes:** The drug tested with exposure therapy is named as a different one that works on the same NMDA spot as D-serine, not "the drug form" of it, and its effect is given as it was found: a small boost across 21 trials. Exposure therapy gets a few words of explanation. The session-quality rule stays word for word, now with its source.

**Why:** D-cycloserine (DCS) is its own drug that acts at the NMDA receptor's glycine spot, the spot D-serine switches on ([Smits et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3747985); [Wolosker et al. 1999](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC23961)). The best evidence, 21 trials with 1,047 patients, found a small boost ([Mataix-Cols et al. 2017](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2599177)), and it helped after sessions that ended with low fear, not after the others, which is your session rule. I found no trial of D-serine itself with exposure therapy, which your "possibly" already covers.

**What stays:** "It does not erase fear directly" and the whole session-quality sentence, word for word.

**You're deciding:** Whether DCS is named as a separate drug and its effect given as small.

**My advice:** Approve.
