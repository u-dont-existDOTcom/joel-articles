## The short answer

Your hypothesis holds up better than "weeks, not permanent" allowed, but the mechanism runs a different way round than
"cementing". DeltaFosB doesn't look like the thing that *stores* the schema. It looks like the thing that **stops the
schema from updating**. For your argument that is the stronger version, because it does not need the protein to last a
lifetime.

## The mechanism: DeltaFosB blocks the cell from registering anything new

In the dentate gyrus, DeltaFosB builds up in exactly the granule cells that just fired, and what it does there is stop
those cells from switching cFos on again. cFos is the gene a cell needs to turn on to take part in new learning. So the
cells that encoded yesterday are taken out of the running today, and today's experience lands on different cells. The
authors call it timestamping, and they think it is what episodic memory needs.
([Nature Communications 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9606265/))

Read that against your schema: the pile-up is a durable gate on **which cells may encode new experience**. In healthy
use it keeps memories separable. The pathological case is the same gate in the wrong place.

## The closest thing to a direct test, and it goes your way

One study put mice through chronic variable stress and measured fear learning. The stressed mice could not extinguish
fear — the "x is dangerous" association would not update — and that failure went with **DeltaFosB piled up in the
basolateral amygdala and the dentate gyrus**, with the authors proposing the accumulation caused the cFos deficit and so
the extinction failure. ([Behavioural Brain Research 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bbr.2023.114416), PMID
[37003493](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37003493/))

That is your entanglement, named: accumulated DeltaFosB in fear circuitry, and a threat belief that will not revise.
Two caveats, both real. It is one paper, and the link is associational ("was associated with", "may have been
responsible") — they did not knock DeltaFosB down and watch extinction recover. And the model reaches that state partly
through a neonatal anaesthetic exposure, not through trauma, with chronic stress as the second hit.

But it fits the mechanism above exactly: DeltaFosB blocks cFos, cFos is needed to encode the new, extinction *is* the
encoding of new safety learning, so a cell full of DeltaFosB cannot learn that the thing is now safe. Two papers, one
chain.

## This answers the "weeks" objection without needing the protein to be permanent

The protein's half-life runs to about 8 days, and it persists weeks after the stimulus stops. That was my objection to
"permanently", and it stands as a fact about the protein. It does not touch your loop:

- the schema makes the person read the situation as dangerous
- so the stress response fires
- so DeltaFosB is topped back up in those circuits
- so the circuits cannot encode the disconfirming experience
- so the schema is not revised, and the next situation is read the same way

Nothing in that needs one batch of protein to last for years. It needs the cycle to keep running, which is what a
schema does by itself. Your DFB-1 sentence ("a permanent loop until we make conscious efforts to break it from one or
both ends") is the accurate form of this, and "from one or both ends" is doing real work: therapy can attack the
reading, and the protocol can attack the topping-up.

**Not sourced:** nobody has shown this loop in a person. Treat it as a mechanism that fits, not a demonstrated one.

## Two things that cut the other way, and belong in the same breath

- **Where it lands still decides the sign.** In the nucleus accumbens, DeltaFosB in D1 cells goes with resilience and in
  D2 cells with susceptibility, and depressed people have less of it there, not more. The fear-circuit finding above is
  the amygdala and dentate gyrus — different places, different job. "More DeltaFosB = more armor" is still not a claim
  the literature supports on its own. (The other lane's pass, with its sources.)
- **Knocking it down is not safely good.** In chronically epileptic mice, suppressing hippocampal DeltaFosB made
  things worse — more neuroinflammation, mossy fibre loss, granule cell dispersion — and the authors read it as part of
  a homeostatic mechanism protecting the brain.
  ([Frontiers in Molecular Neuroscience 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10810990/)) A review of the
  protein's drug potential lists its domains as memory formation, drug addiction, stress resilience and immune activity
  at once, which is why its authors spend the paper on how a drug could be made cell-type specific.
  ([ACS Chemical Neuroscience 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8879420/))

So the honest shape is: in the fear circuits, accumulation plausibly locks the schema by blocking updates; in the reward
circuit, accumulation tracks the opposite way; and a blanket "degrade it" goal is not supported in either.

## Dynorphin, verified (for the DFB-2 card)

The other lane's reading of Zachariou 2006 is right, and I checked it myself: DeltaFosB in the nucleus accumbens,
"partly through the repression of dynorphin expression, mediates several major features of opiate addiction" — it
increased morphine's rewarding effects and physical dependence while reducing analgesia and speeding tolerance.
([Nature Neuroscience 2006](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1636), PMID
[16415864](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16415864/)) That is opiate work, not trauma work, so it supports "these two
are not one mechanism" and does not support a trauma-specific claim about which way dynorphin moves in the Void.
