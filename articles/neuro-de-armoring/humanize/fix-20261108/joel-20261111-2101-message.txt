Yes. Several studies have tested dietary interventions for PTSD, and some report substantial symptom improvements. But the evidence is much less developed than for depression.

The most interesting finding is that a Mediterranean-diet trial in people with diagnosed PTSD found large improvements in both the Mediterranean group and a group receiving ordinary healthy-nutrition counseling. That is particularly relevant to your hypothesis about self-care and behavioral activation.

## 1. What the human studies found

| Study                                          | Participants and design                                                                                  | PTSD symptom improvement                                                   |
| ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mediterranean diet (2024)                      | 62 World Trade Center responders with chronic PTSD; randomized against standard nutrition counseling     | 11.5 vs 13 points, favoring control numerically; no significant difference |
| Low-glutamate diet (2022)                      | 39 Gulf War veterans with PTSD symptom data; one-month dietary intervention without a diet control group | 10.6-point average improvement                                             |
| Lifestyle and weight-management program (2026) | 174 veterans with PTSD; randomized between two active lifestyle programs                                 | 7.9 vs 7.6 points after six months; no significant difference              |
| Ketogenic diet + ketone salts (2024)           | Three patients, two completing four weeks; no control                                                    | 20-point and 10-point improvements in the two completers                   |

PTSD Checklist outcomes. The trials used related but not identical versions of the questionnaire, so raw changes should not be compared directly as treatment-effect rankings.&#x20;

### Mediterranean diet: a particularly revealing experiment

The [2024 World Trade Center dietary trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38263989/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) randomized 62 responders with chronic PTSD and overweight or obesity.

Participants received either ten weeks of Mediterranean dietary education, motivational texts, cooking activities and physical-activity advice, or ordinary personalized nutritional counseling. Both groups received three individual dietitian visits.

That is quite close to your earlier idea: actively caring for yourself may improve emotional health even when the specific dietary pattern is not essential.

However, because neither group received an inactive control, the trial cannot establish how much improvement came from nutrition, coaching, expectations, other PTSD treatment or natural changes over time.

## 2. Low-glutamate diet: an interesting uncontrolled result

The [2022 Gulf War Illness study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9251130/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) tested a diet that reduced sources of dietary glutamate additives and other excitotoxins while increasing nutrient-rich foods.

Average improvement: 10.6 points, p < 0.001 within the same group.

Those with the highest baseline PTSD symptom severity improved by about 15.2 points, whereas those starting with minimal or moderate symptoms improved little.

That subgroup result is worth following up. But without a randomized diet-control group, differences in baseline severity could also produce regression to the mean.

The researchers subsequently randomized participants to blinded MSG or placebo challenges. MSG did not produce a significant difference in PTSD symptoms (p = 0.840). Therefore, the observed dietary improvement cannot be attributed specifically to removing MSG.

Importantly, this was a study of veterans with Gulf War Illness, not a representative sample of all people with PTSD.&#x20;

## 3. A larger 2026 trial also found improvements in both actively coached groups

The [MOVE!+UP randomized trial](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2847030?utm_source=chatgpt.com) enrolled 174 veterans with PTSD.

Both groups received 16 weeks of structured healthy-eating and physical-activity education, goal setting and self-monitoring. The enhanced group additionally received PTSD-focused behavioral interventions, walking activities and individual dietitian appointments.

At six months, PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 scores had improved by an average of 7.9 points in the enhanced group and 7.6 in the standard program.

The adjusted between-group difference was not significant (p = 0.72); the same was true at 12 months (p = 0.70).&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://jamanetwork.com\&sz=32)

jamanetwork.com



This was primarily a weight-management trial, with PTSD symptoms as a secondary outcome. Still, it provides additional evidence that substantial symptom improvements can occur during structured lifestyle programs, without showing that one particular dietary intervention produced the improvement.

## 4. Ketogenic diets and nutritional supplements

The ketogenic evidence is especially preliminary.

A [2024 Norwegian ketogenic feasibility study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39588043/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) enrolled three women with PTSD. Two completed four weeks of a ketogenic diet combined with ketone-salt supplementation. Their PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 scores fell by 20 and 10 points.

A [2026 individual case report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41783812/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) described a patient with treatment-resistant PTSD whose score dropped from 32 to 2 after a longer ketogenic intervention. That is striking, but a single case cannot estimate treatment efficacy.

A more informative [21-person randomized ketone-salt trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36282607/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) found improvement within the treatment group but no statistically significant benefit over placebo on PTSD symptoms. This was supplementation, not a ketogenic dietary intervention.

For omega-3 fatty acids, a [2025 systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38916944/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) found that three human randomized trials did not demonstrate significant prevention of PTSD onset after traumatic exposure. That concerns prevention after trauma, not the treatment of longstanding PTSD.

The ketogenic and supplement studies discussed here were checked at abstract level for their main results; the full-text methodology of those studies was not formally audited in this pass.

## 5. How strong is the evidence overall?

The Swedish Agency for Health Technology Assessment examined the relevant systematic reviews in May 2025 and found no relevant systematic reviews judged to have low or moderate risk of bias. The two reviews it identified were rated high risk.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.sbu.se\&sz=32)

sbu.se



That assessment predates some 2026 publications and does not mean that every primary trial is invalid. It does reinforce that the evidence is at an early stage.

My assessment is:

| Question                                                                             | What the evidence supports                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Do PTSD symptoms improve during nutrition programs?                                  | Yes, repeatedly observed                                     |
| Does Mediterranean eating outperform ordinary healthy-nutrition counseling for PTSD? | Not demonstrated in the small randomized trial               |
| Does the low-glutamate diet reduce PTSD symptoms?                                    | Promising uncontrolled signal, not a confirmed causal effect |
| Could ketogenic diets help PTSD?                                                     | Plausible, very preliminary human evidence                   |
| How much of the improvement comes from meaningful self-care?                         | Not yet isolated                                             |
| Is any specific diet established as an effective PTSD treatment?                     | No                                                           |

## What I find most important

The PTSD research makes the same unanswered question from SMILES and AMMEND even more apparent.

When people receive a coherent behavioral project involving nutrition, regular attention, specific goals and repeated opportunities to care for themselves, their symptoms sometimes improve substantially.

The World Trade Center study is especially interesting because both nutrition programs produced almost identical PTSD improvement. This is compatible with a shared nutritional effect, a shared psychological effect, or both.

The next decisive study would compare active dietary coaching with equally intensive non-diet self-care coaching and with a credible minimal-support control. That would better distinguish the biological effects of eating differently from the therapeutic effects of taking purposeful care of oneself.

For now, dietary improvement is a reasonable area to investigate as an adjunct to established PTSD care, but the current research cannot justify claiming that a particular diet treats PTSD with anything like the large, replicated effect proposed for depression.
