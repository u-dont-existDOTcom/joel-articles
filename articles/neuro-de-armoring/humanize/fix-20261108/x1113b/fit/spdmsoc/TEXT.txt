DMSO (99.9%): Smell it, as in the Fog table below. In rat brain cells in a dish, DMSO at 0.5 to 1.5% quickly turned down the signals glutamate sends through its NMDA and AMPA receptors, and the calcium they let in. It also kept glutamate from killing the cells (Lu & Mattson, 2001). Back in 1967, doctors in Lima injected it into the muscles of 42 psychiatric inpatients, and nine of them had severe anxiety or obsessive-compulsive disorder. Within days they grew calmer, and the acute cases went home sooner than 16 patients with similar diagnoses who got the usual treatments of the time, such as electroshock, insulin comas and tranquilizers (Ramírez & Luza, 1967). There was no placebo, though, and the patients they were compared with weren't picked at random.

It is used for humans routinely in stem cell transplants, including for children, and by many humans for all kinds of medical problems, from arthritis and bladder pain to brain and spinal cord injuries (Jacob & de la Torre, 2015). There is evidence (Mitrus, et al, 2018) that using too much DMSO (10% vs 5%) in stem cell transfusions increases mild side effects in adult humans. It hasn't been tested (that I know of, yet) specifically for PTSD, so for PTSD it rests on what people report, and many say it helps.

Nebulized DMSO with heparin (a blood thinner) has been shown to cut the lung damage from toxic cotton smoke in sheep (Kimura, et al, 1988). In another study, all seven sheep given the two survived the smoke, while all seven untreated sheep died, and so did four of the six given DMSO alone (Brown, et al, 1988). But it has shown some neurotoxic effects in rodent studies: in mice in their first month of life, even the smallest dose tested set off brain cell death, which worried the authors for children getting it in transplants (Hanslick, et al, 2009). Translating mouse research to humans isn't straightforward.
