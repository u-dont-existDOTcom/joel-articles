D-serine may help therapy update old memories, because it switches on a spot on the NMDA receptor that this kind of learning needs (Wolosker, et al, 1999), and it may also open the GluD channels. The drug that's actually been tested with exposure therapy (facing the feared thing in small, safe doses) is a different one, D-cycloserine (DCS), which works on that same spot, and across 21 trials with over 1,000 patients it gave exposure therapy a small boost (Mataix-Cols, et al, 2017). It does not erase fear directly; it appears to strengthen whatever learning occurs during or after the exposure session. This is why session quality matters: if the exposure produces corrective learning and ends with reduced fear or mastery, DCS (and possibly D-serine also) may help consolidate that new memory; if the exposure is overwhelming or ends badly, it may consolidate the wrong association (Smits, et al, 2013).
