Claude is correct that the studies I previously cited did not directly demonstrate that eating more potassium improves nerve function. However, there is authoritative evidence that adequate dietary potassium is necessary for normal nerve function.

1. NIH explicitly confirms the connection
The NIH Potassium Fact Sheet for Health Professionals explains that potassium maintains the electrical gradients across cell membranes required for nerve transmission.

It also states that approximately 90% of ingested potassium is absorbed and helps maintain normal intracellular and extracellular potassium concentrations. 

Health Professional Fact Sheet

This supports the nutritional claim that adequate potassium intake supports normal nerve function.

2. Does increasing potassium actually improve nerve function?
There is direct evidence in people with potassium disturbances.

For example, a published case report documented paralysis associated with severe hypokalemia that completely reversed following oral potassium replacement. 

pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

But this does not mean someone with normal potassium status will experience improved nerve function from eating more potassium.

Interestingly, a randomized trial involving 42 patients with chronic kidney disease found that reducing dietary potassium slowed the progression of peripheral neuropathy. This illustrates why the starting potassium status matters. 

pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

3. What should your article say?
I would use:

Potassium is essential for normal nerve signaling. Potassium-rich foods, including potatoes and leafy greens, help maintain adequate dietary intake and support normal electrolyte balance. However, whether increasing potassium intake reduces stress-related neuronal hyperexcitability or improves KCNQ channel function remains unproven.

Verdict: Claude is right to demand evidence for a benefit from additional potassium. But if it argues that dietary potassium has no established role in supporting normal nerve function, that contradicts the NIH evidence.

The key distinction is maintaining adequate potassium versus obtaining an additional neurological benefit by increasing intake beyond what is needed.

I reviewed the original eight-page paper by Torres et al. (2008), Dietary electrolytes are related to mood, including its results tables.

A lower sodium and higher potassium intake was associated with better mood.

4. Implication for your article
I would distinguish two claims:

Well supported: Adequate dietary potassium is necessary for normal neuronal electrical signaling, and this study provides some evidence associating higher potassium intake with less fatigue.

Still unproven: Eating additional potassium improves psychological stress regulation, reduces glutamate hyperexcitability, or strengthens KCNQ-mediated inhibition in people whose potassium levels are already normal.

So Claude is right that the experiment does not establish an independent mood benefit from increasing potassium. It is wrong if it uses this paper to conclude that additional potassium has no mood benefit and sodium reduction alone explains the findings.

The appropriate conclusion is that the individual contributions of sodium and potassium remain unresolved, not that potassium's contribution has been ruled out.
