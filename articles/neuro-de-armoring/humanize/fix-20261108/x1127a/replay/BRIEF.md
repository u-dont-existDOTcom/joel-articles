# Blind rewrite: two warnings, from the lessons alone

You are rewriting two short passages of a self-help article by Joel, a Substack writer (informal, first person, plain
words; "I" is Joel). The article is a protocol for adults with trauma that uses supplements; both passages sit among the
warnings at its top. Each passage below is a draft that reads partly like a model wrote it. Rewrite each one the way the
lessons say, changing as little as they call for. Keep every fact and every instruction; add no fact, no example and no
detail of your own. Keep these words as they are: "Post-ashwa", "(no joy or desire)", "naked and exposed", Nicholas
Epley's credit with his book Mindwise and the "Illusion of Transparency", the heart-rate test ("back down to your usual
resting rate, and under 90 BPM") and the pointer to the "Metabolic Red Line" below.

## The lessons (from his fixes of other paragraphs)

1. **A "but" answers what it turns from.** After a concession ("X is rare", "X is safe for most people"), the half after
   "but" says what it changes about the first half, not only something true next to it. (From another article: after a
   sentence on magnesium and weak kidneys, "But they should be far more worried about the MDMA." stood alone; his "But
   they should be far more worried about taking the MDMA which depletes it" says what the MDMA has to do with the
   magnesium.)
2. **A person does what a person does.** When a body part or an organ (the brain, the nervous system, the gut) is the one
   that judges, projects, feels, decides or wants, give the act to the person ("you ..."), with the body part's state as
   the circumstance if it matters. A brain that fires, releases or adapts is physiology and stays. (The same tell as an
   abstract noun acting: "safety gives the space for desire".)
3. **A paragraph that adds to the one before opens on a plain joiner that says so, and says what will happen as often as
   it really happens.** "Also" buried in mid-sentence leaves the link to the reader; a tendency said as a certainty ("you
   will ...") reads flatter than the truth ("you'll tend to ..."). (A judgment keeps the doubt it really has: in another
   article, a paragraph with "I'd guess you just haven't ..." read human, and the same paragraph said flat read AI.)
4. **Where a phrase leaves open something a reader could get wrong, add the one word that settles it.** (From another
   article: "the evidence for it" became "the evidence for this med", and "that's their last dose" became "that should be
   their last dose".)

## Passage 1 (the warning about two herbs)

I'm not advising lion's mane or ashwagandha, even though they're popular herbs for focus and stress, because some people report anhedonia (no joy or desire) and sexual dysfunction long after they stop taking them. Post-ashwa and post-lion's mane syndrome are rare, but it's the same kind of thing SSRIs and finasteride can do, so if you're already on either herb, I'd quit it as soon as you notice any of that.

## Passage 2 (two paragraphs: the warning about feeling exposed, and the one after it)

As you de-armor, you will feel naked and exposed, and you will assume everyone can see your anxiety, which they mostly can't. Nicholas Epley, Mindwise, calls this the "Illusion of Transparency."

You will also think people are judging you when they're mostly just thinking about themselves, because your armored brain projects the threat it feels onto neutral faces. So during this protocol, do not trust your social intuition: assume you are misreading every room, at least until your heart rate is back down to your usual resting rate, and under 90 BPM, since a racing heart means your reasoning brain is offline (see the "Metabolic Red Line" below).
