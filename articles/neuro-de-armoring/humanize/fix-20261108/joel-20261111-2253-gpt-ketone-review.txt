# Ketogenic diets and ketone salts for PTSD: comprehensive evidence review

The evidence is promising enough to justify serious clinical trials, but not yet strong enough to establish that ketogenic diets or ketone salts treat PTSD.

There is a striking contrast between the two evidence streams:

- Ketogenic diets: Several dramatic individual improvements, including near-complete disappearance of reported PTSD symptoms, but no adequately controlled PTSD treatment trial demonstrating efficacy.
- Ketone salts: One small placebo-controlled PTSD symptom trial, which did not demonstrate a statistically significant benefit over placebo.

The distinction matters because ketogenic eating changes much more than blood ketone concentrations. It also changes glucose and insulin metabolism, food composition, eating habits and often the amount of professional support people receive.

## 2. The ketone-salt randomized trial: weaker evidence than the abstract initially suggests

I obtained and audited the full five-page publication of [Youssef et al. (2022)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36282607/?utm_source=chatgpt.com).

It randomized people with PTSD to placebo or Prüvit KETO//OS MAX, taken at 7 g of beta-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) twice daily, totaling 14 g/day for six weeks. BHB is a ketone molecule the body can use as fuel and that may also participate in cell signaling.

These groups began with different median symptom severity. The changes in their medians are not a valid substitute for the randomized statistical comparison.

The investigators highlighted that the ketone group improved significantly from baseline (p = 0.0003), while the placebo group did not. However, the actual group-by-time interaction was p = 0.7988, and the post-treatment between-group comparison was p = 0.602.

That is a critical distinction. A significant improvement in one group and a nonsignificant improvement in another do not establish that the groups improved by significantly different amounts.

Other important methodological problems:

- Twenty-one participants were recruited, but the study's allocation table accounted for 20, and reported follow-up means involved only eight ketone recipients and six placebo recipients.
- Baseline PTSD severity was substantially imbalanced.
- Blood BHB was not measured, so the researchers could not establish what ketone exposure the supplement actually produced.
- The manufacturer, Prüvit Ventures, partly funded the study, and an investigator disclosed Prüvit research support.

The result is inconclusive, rather than proof that ketone salts do nothing. But it offers little affirmative controlled evidence for treating PTSD.

### Was there a second randomized trial?

The [2026 integrative review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13598748/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) identifies a separate publication by Locklin and colleagues reporting blood-pressure and laboratory outcomes from a six-week ketone-salt trial.

The review itself warns that this may be another report of the same participant cohort as Youssef's study. The location, study length, intervention and participant counts overlap. Differences between the reports prevent definitive confirmation.&#x20;

Therefore, it would be misleading to count them as two independent demonstrations of ketone-salt efficacy.

The researchers measured blood D-BHB three times per day, defining a day in ketosis as averaging at least 0.5 mmol/L.

Two completers showed PTSD score improvements of 20 and 10 points. A third participant withdrew after two weeks.&#x20;

This is a genuine positive clinical observation with unusually good metabolic exposure monitoring.

But there was no randomized control and no way to separate the dietary effect from the supplemented BHB, regular professional contact, or continuing psychiatric treatment.

The trial also recorded adverse reactions on 70% of intervention days, most commonly headache and fatigue. Those are days, not a percentage of people with adverse reactions.

## 4. The strongest positive ketogenic cases

2025: PTSD Checklist 80 → 0

Bellamy and Laurent, case report

The individual had multiple psychiatric conditions, including PTSD, bipolar II disorder and binge-eating disorder. The ketogenic program included substantial dietary modification, intermittent fasting, professional support and peer engagement.

The person reported that mental health was best when blood BHB measured approximately 3–5 mmol/L, and that symptoms worsened following interruptions in ketosis.

This possible within-person relationship is important for hypothesis generation. Yet food intake, stress, expectations, fasting and ketone levels changed together, and the measurements were not blinded.

[Full case report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40626225/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

2026: PTSD Checklist 32 → 2

Laurent, case report

This individual had longstanding treatment-resistant PTSD and showed substantial improvements during ketogenic therapy.

One detail needs careful interpretation: the paper discusses an earlier PCL-5 score of 64, but that score came from approximately two years before ketogenic treatment. The score at the start of the supervised intervention was 32.

In addition, carbohydrate restriction began nine days before the formal baseline assessment. Nutritional supplements, changes involving low-dose naltrexone, and later exercise training complicate attribution.

These are not reasons to discard the observed improvement. They are reasons not to label the entire historical change as an effect of ketogenic treatment.

[Full case report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41783812/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The two cases are striking, especially given the individuals' psychiatric histories. But they are also highly selected published successes. We cannot calculate the probability that another person with PTSD will obtain anything similar.

## 5. What might be happening biologically?

There is a plausible biological basis for pursuing this research, particularly for BHB.

| Proposed pathway         | What has been observed                                                        | What remains unknown                                                                  |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Alternative brain energy | Ketones can be used as metabolic fuel                                         | Whether PTSD symptoms improve because of improved brain energetics                    |
| Neuroinflammation        | BHB injection in a stressed-rat model reduced some inflammatory measures      | Whether these changes mediate improvement in human PTSD                               |
| Stress response          | Animal interventions sometimes alter corticosterone and fear-related behavior | Whether results generalize to chronic human trauma symptoms                           |
| Neural signaling         | BHB influences pathways relevant to neuronal function and plasticity          | Whether a particular human ketone exposure produces useful PTSD effects               |
| Sleep and arousal        | Some human case and community reports describe improvements                   | Whether ketosis itself is responsible rather than other dietary or behavioral changes |

A 2020 rat experiment found that injected BHB reduced anxiety-like behavior after experimentally modeled prolonged stress, with partial suppression of inflammatory TNF-alpha. A newer 2026 rat experiment found oral medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) oil increased BHB and reduced anxiety-like behavior, but did not significantly reduce the measured inflammatory cytokines.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov



This is interesting because it leaves open a route through metabolism or stress regulation that does not depend on a large detectable reduction in the measured inflammatory markers.

Still, these are animal behavioral models, not clinical PTSD remission. Injected BHB, orally supplied ketone salts and dietary ketosis are also different exposures.

### Could ketone salts replace a ketogenic diet?

Not established. Exogenous ketones may raise circulating BHB without reproducing the sustained carbohydrate restriction and wider metabolic changes from ketogenic eating.

Conversely, some benefits of ketogenic eating might arise from food composition, steadier glucose availability, avoidance of particular foods, or behavior changes rather than from ketones themselves.

The reports suggesting 3–5 mmol/L as an optimal psychiatric BHB range come from individual experiences. That is not an established therapeutic PTSD target.

## 6. Safety and feasibility

For a metabolic intervention intended to continue over months, safety deserves attention beyond the initial adaptation period.

A 2026 systematic analysis of ketogenic-diet adverse events across several medical indications found that at least one adverse event was reported in approximately 43% of participants, with substantial differences among dietary protocols and clinical populations. This is not a PTSD-specific risk rate. Gastrointestinal symptoms, fatigue and metabolic complications were recurring issues.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

a systematic literature analysis



The PTSD-relevant reports also provide a concrete biochemical warning. A published case described low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol increasing from 156 to 209 mg/dL and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), a liver enzyme, increasing from 18 to 119 U/L during a ketogenic period. The later recovery involved several other changes, so the case cannot establish that diet alone caused or reversed the abnormalities.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov



Ketogenic regimens can also create practical problems with hydration, electrolytes, blood glucose and food restriction, particularly in medically vulnerable people. Ketone salts add minerals such as sodium, potassium or calcium depending on the formulation, which matters in people with kidney disease or medications affecting electrolyte balance. Long-term controlled safety data specifically in PTSD remain insufficient.

The Norwegian study's small sample and absence of serious events should not be interpreted as proof of long-term safety.

## 7. What are people reporting outside studies?

I examined public Reddit discussions from r/CPTSDNextSteps and r/keto, covering 26 comments in one accessible thread and 12 in another.

There were reports of improved anxiety, more stable mood, fewer nightmares, better energy and less fear. One person described longstanding relief from night terrors while following a very low-carbohydrate eating pattern.

There were also accounts of no obvious improvement in core CPTSD despite improved general anxiety, difficulty sustaining keto, symptoms returning during stressful periods, and restrictive eating producing shame or other emotional distress.

The [CPTSD experiences discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/CPTSDNextSteps/comments/nuo05q/people_here_with_cptsd_have_you_noticed_any/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) was particularly relevant to your earlier self-care hypothesis: some participants said ordinary whole-food eating and preparing nourishing meals felt as helpful for mood as strict dietary rules.

These are self-selected reports, often without measured BHB, formal PTSD assessments or an unchanged treatment context. They identify interesting potential responders and nonresponders, not effectiveness rates.

## 8. The next study to watch

[German ketogenic-diet PTSD feasibility study](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07415590?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The registry lists six actual participants, with study completion in August 2026 and no posted results as of November 11, 2026. It is a single-arm feasibility study, so any future symptom improvements will still be uncontrolled.

Results pending

A decisive later trial would need to compare a ketogenic program against an equally credible and intensive healthy-eating program, preferably with a parallel BHB-salt-versus-placebo comparison. It should measure blood D-BHB, core trauma symptoms, sleep, functioning, medication changes, adherence and adverse outcomes.

That design would also test whether BHB itself is the active factor or merely a biomarker accompanying several other therapeutic changes.

## Final assessment

| Question                                                           | Assessment                                                               |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Can ketone salts reliably treat PTSD?                              | Not demonstrated; controlled symptom trial inconclusive                  |
| Does ketogenic eating have promising human signals?                | Yes, particularly in individual cases                                    |
| Is ketogenic-diet efficacy established by randomized PTSD trials?  | No                                                                       |
| Does more blood BHB predict better PTSD outcomes?                  | Interesting individual observations, unproven                            |
| Are effects likely to be specific to PTSD?                         | Unknown; many improvements overlap with mood, anxiety and sleep symptoms |
| Can the optimal diet, BHB dose or treatment duration be specified? | No, not from the present evidence                                        |

The evidence is sufficiently interesting to merit further work, particularly because both human case reports and multiple animal experiments point in a potentially consistent direction. But the most direct controlled human test of ketone salts did not confirm a treatment effect. The gap between those positive observations and established efficacy remains substantial.
