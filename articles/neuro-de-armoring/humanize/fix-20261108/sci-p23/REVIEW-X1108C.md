## SC-97: Protein and ketosis: little of it becomes sugar; the limit is how much you can take

*Fixing ATP Signaling if Cortisol Isn't High, P4*

**Now:**

> For the long run there's Strategy B, the "Resilience Mode", a keto diet with carbs under 20g/day so your liver makes the ketones itself. That only works if fat becomes your fuel, so eat fat until you're full. Don't fill up on lean protein like chicken breast instead, because your body turns the extra protein into sugar through gluconeogenesis, and that keeps you out of ketosis. Switching over often brings on the keto flu, and to avoid it, refer to my Keto Adaptation Guide.

**As it would read:**

> For the long run there's Strategy B, the "Resilience Mode", a keto diet with carbs under 20g/day so your liver makes the ketones itself. That only works if fat becomes your fuel, so eat fat until you're full. Don't fill up on lean protein like chicken breast instead, since your body can only take so much protein: past about a third of your calories it can make you sick (Bilsborough & Mann, 2006), so the rest of your energy has to come from fat. Switching over often brings on the keto flu, and to avoid it, refer to my Keto Adaptation Guide.

**What changes:** "because your body turns the extra protein into sugar through gluconeogenesis, and that keeps you out of ketosis" becomes the reason that holds: your body can only take so much protein, so the rest of your energy has to come from fat.

**Why:** Very little of the protein you eat becomes sugar: after 23 g of egg protein, only about 3.9 g of the 50.4 g of glucose the body made over the next 8 hours came from it, even after an overnight fast ([Fromentin, et al, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3636601)). What does limit protein is the liver's capacity to clear its nitrogen: past about 35% of your calories it brings nausea and diarrhea, the "rabbit starvation" of lean-meat diets ([Bilsborough & Mann, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16779921/)). So your instruction stands, for the reason the studies give (flag F-1). A fresh reader said the keto-flu sentence wasn't needed; it's yours and this card doesn't touch it.

**What stays:** Strategy B, its carb limit, "eat fat until you're full", the instruction itself, and your keto flu sentence with its link.

**You're deciding:** Whether the instruction keeps the reason the protein studies give, instead of gluconeogenesis.

**My advice:** Approve.

## SC-106: Potassium and the KCNQ brakes: low potassium makes nerves harder to fire, not easier

*1. Electrolytes (Choose Your Path), P5*

**Now:**

> The Science: Your neurons have "brakes" called KCNQ channels that stop them from firing uncontrollably. These brakes run on Potassium ions. If you are low on potassium (common in high stress), your brakes fail. You can push the pedal (breathe), but the car won't stop.

**As it would read:**

> The Science: Your neurons have "brakes" called KCNQ channels, which keep a neuron from firing over and over by letting potassium out of it. Stress does lower the potassium in your blood, since adrenaline pushes it into your cells (Brown, et al, 1983), but with less potassium outside them, nerve cells rest farther from firing, not closer (Kuwabara, et al, 2002).

*1. Electrolytes (Choose Your Path), P14*

**Now:**

> The Strategy: Eat 2 large potatoes and 2 cups of greens every single day. This saturates your cells, lowering your "resting voltage" so you become physically harder to trigger.

**As it would read:**

> The Strategy: Eat 2 large potatoes and 2 cups of greens every single day.

**What changes:** The brakes-fail story goes from blocks 106 and 115: what KCNQ channels do stays, with what stress does to blood potassium and which way low potassium moves nerve cells; "This saturates your cells, lowering your resting voltage so you become physically harder to trigger" is cut.

**Why:** KCNQ channels are potassium channels that keep a neuron from firing over and over, so "brakes" holds. The direction doesn't: during low blood potassium, human nerves rest more negative and are harder to excite ([Kuwabara, et al, 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12210395/)), and more potassium outside cells moves them toward firing ([Campese & Adenuga, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6340902)). So "if you are low on potassium, your brakes fail" and "lowering your resting voltage so you become harder to trigger" are both reversed. Stress does lower blood potassium: adrenaline pushes it into cells ([Brown, et al, 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6314140/)). Blocks 104 and 105 ("Install the Brake Pads", "Potassium Channels (KCNQ)") rest on the same story, and the target (block 107) matches the FDA's Daily Value; the read of the whole protocol takes those up with your answer here (flag F-11).

**What stays:** "The Science:", "brakes" and KCNQ; the target, the food-only rule, the menu and "Eat 2 large potatoes and 2 cups of greens every single day."

**You're deciding:** Whether the potassium section stops saying low potassium takes the brakes off.
