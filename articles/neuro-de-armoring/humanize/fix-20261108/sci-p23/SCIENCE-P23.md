| 97 | lean protein "turns into sugar through gluconeogenesis, and that keeps you out of ketosis" | Very little dietary protein becomes glucose: after 23 g of labelled egg protein, 3.9 g of the 50.4 g of glucose the body made over 8 hours came from it, even after an overnight fast ([Fromentin et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3636601), doi 10.2337/db12-1208). What limits protein is the liver's capacity to clear its nitrogen: past about 35% of energy, nausea, diarrhea and the "rabbit starvation" of lean-meat diets ([Bilsborough and Mann 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16779921/), doi 10.1123/ijsnem.16.2.129). The instruction (eat fat, not lean protein) stands, for that reason | SC-97 |
| 106 | KCNQ "brakes run on potassium"; "if you are low on potassium (common in high stress), your brakes fail" | KCNQ (Kv7) channels are potassium channels that keep a neuron from firing over and over, so "brakes" holds. The direction doesn't: during low blood potassium, human nerve axons rest more negative and are harder to excite ([Kuwabara et al. 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12210395/), doi 10.1002/mus.10169). Stress does lower blood potassium: adrenaline at stress levels pushes it into cells through beta-2 receptors ([Brown et al. 1983](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6314140/), doi 10.1056/NEJM198312083092303) | SC-106 |
| 115 | eating potassium "saturates your cells, lowering your resting voltage so you become physically harder to trigger" | More potassium outside excitable cells moves their resting potential toward firing, not away ([Campese and Adenuga 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6340902), doi 10.1016/j.kisu.2016.01.003) | SC-106 |
