MARKED:
- S1: 4, 21 — "relational density" is a label for the thing: the reader can't tell what is counted (how many people, how many hours with them, how close) or what the sixteenth is a sixteenth of. His way would say the thing itself in the reader's terms (the company we get against the company a body like ours grew up expecting), with the number attached to what it counts.
- S1: 11 — it opens on the measure ("operating on about one-sixteenth of ...") and never gets to what the reader takes from it, which is that their own days hold far too few people. His way would put that first and the figure and Perry after it as what it rests on.
- S2: 18 — a six-word line standing alone between longer ones, and it labels ("is real") without saying anything only this writer would. His way would join it to S1 or carry it on with what the feeling is.
- S2: 12 — nothing in it says how it follows from S1. The reader has to guess that the "Void" is what living on a sixteenth feels like. His way would tie them ("so the Void you feel is that missing company", or S1 running on into it).
- S2: 10 — "real" could mean the feeling is truly felt, or that something is truly missing; and "Void" in capitals and quote marks points at a term this draft never gives. His way would say what it is the feeling of.
- S3: 3 — "cannot simply return" corrects a move nobody has proposed yet; the sentence could say the thing itself (what happens to a de-armored person in a lonely life).
- S3: 14 — a flat rule against what a person would naturally do (go back to the life they had), with no word on why they would want to or what the problem is. His way would grant the pull first, then say the problem.
- S3: 10 — "cannot" reads two ways: you won't be able to, or you mustn't. It is a statement that is really a warning or an instruction. His way would say which ("going back to a lonely life will hurt more than it did", or "don't go back to ...").
- S3: 27 — the don't (no going back to a lonely life) comes before the do it sits beside (S4). His way would give what to do first, then what not to.
- S4: 5 — it opens on a rule, "You must", with the reader outside it. His way would start from where the reader is (newly open, and with too few people around) and get to the act from there.
- S4: 13 — the act comes before what calls for it; the reason only arrives after "or". His way would open on the reason (without enough people, the armor comes back) and then give the act.
- S4: 21, 4 — "engineer a 'High-Density' social environment (A Tribe)" is two labels for one thing and neither says what counts: how many people, how often, living how close. "Aggressively" doesn't say how either. His way would name the thing (people you see most days, and roughly how many).
- S4: 15 — as written nobody could carry it out: "engineer a social environment" gives no first step. His way would give something a person can actually do this month.
- S4: 1 — a coined term, a second term in brackets, an instruction and a threat with a metaphor are packed into one sentence. His way would give the act and what happens otherwise each their own clause.
- S4: 10 — "the brain" (whose? "your brain"), "the armor" as something a brain reaches for, and "the cold" (the loneliness of S3, or the Void of S2?) each leave the reader to work out what is meant. His way would name it with words the passage already has ("the lonely life").
- S4: 6 — it ends on a line that sounds wise ("just to survive the cold"). His way would end on what to do, or on the plain consequence (you armor up again).
