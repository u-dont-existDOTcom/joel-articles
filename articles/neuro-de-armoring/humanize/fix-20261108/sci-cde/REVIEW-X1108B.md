## SC-47: Prozac in veterans: one small trial, and mixed results since

*C. The "Hardened" Circuit: Dynorphin & DeltaFosB, P2*

**Now:**

> Why Standard Meds Failed You If you have tried SSRIs (antidepressants) and failed, you are not crazy. Ipser & Stein 2012 study showed that Prozac had zero effect on male combat veterans with calcified PTSD. And the side effects are well-known, including anhedonia, zombification, and sexual dysfunction.

**As it would read:**

> Why Standard Meds Failed You If you have tried SSRIs (antidepressants) and failed, you are not crazy. In a small trial of male combat veterans with severe, long-term PTSD, Prozac did no better than a placebo (Hertzberg, et al, 2000). Trials in veterans have been mixed since, and the VA still recommends sertraline and paroxetine (VA/DoD, 2023), so they help some people, just not everyone. And the side effects are well-known, including anhedonia, zombification, and sexual dysfunction.

**What changes:** "Ipser & Stein 2012 study showed that Prozac had zero effect on male combat veterans with calcified PTSD" becomes the trial it comes from, and a sentence that SSRIs help some people.

**Why:** The no-better-than-placebo result is [Hertzberg, et al, 2000](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/759904): twelve male Vietnam combat veterans for twelve weeks. A [2008 review for the VA](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/11955/chapter/15) found SSRIs looked ineffective in mostly male veterans with chronic PTSD, but veterans recruited outside the VA later improved as much as civilians, and the [2023 VA/DoD guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38408360/) recommends paroxetine, sertraline and venlafaxine. Ipser & Stein 2012 is a review I couldn't open. "Calcified PTSD" isn't a term in the literature. A fresh reader flagged the trial sentence and the side effects as not needed; I kept both (the trial is what your paragraph was built on, and the side effects are what the heading promises) and cut only a figure I had added.

**What stays:** "If you have tried SSRIs (antidepressants) and failed, you are not crazy", and your side-effects sentence word for word.

**You're deciding:** Whether the paragraph says what the Prozac trial was, and that SSRIs help some people.

**My advice:** Approve.

## SC-48: Why SSRIs fail some people: nobody knows; why ketones: a mouse finding

*C. The "Hardened" Circuit: Dynorphin & DeltaFosB, P3*

**Now:**

> Why don't these drugs work on calcified PTSD? Because standard serotonin drugs treat the "software" (mood), but they cannot penetrate the "hardware" (the structural changes in the amygdala caused by severe trauma). This protocol uses Ketones and HDAC Inhibitors to remodel the physical structure of the brain that resists standard medication.

**As it would read:**

> Why don't these drugs work for everyone? Nobody knows for sure. This protocol tries something else. The ketone BHB blocks enzymes called HDACs (Shimazu, et al, 2013), and in mice that couldn't hold on to extinction learning (learning that a cue is now safe), an HDAC blocker made the new learning last (Whittle & Singewald, 2014). Whether ketosis does that in people hasn't been tested.

**What changes:** The software and hardware explanation goes. In its place: nobody knows why these drugs fail some people, and why the protocol tries ketones, with that reason's limit.

**Why:** "They cannot penetrate the hardware" doesn't hold: a year of paroxetine grew the hippocampus about 5% in people with PTSD ([Vermetten, et al, 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3233762)). Nothing shows ketones or HDAC inhibitors remodel the amygdala in people. What there is: the ketone BHB blocks HDAC enzymes in mice ([Shimazu, et al, 2013](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4w889738)), and an HDAC blocker made extinction learning last in mice that couldn't keep it ([Whittle & Singewald, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3961057)). Your note on SB-38 said not to argue with the guide, so the paroxetine finding is here and not in the text.

**What stays:** The question that opens it, and "This protocol" as the turn.

**You're deciding:** Whether the paragraph drops the software and hardware claim and gives the mouse finding with its limit.

**My advice:** Approve.

## SC-50: DeltaFosB fades, slowly; what iboga and psilocybin were shown to do

*C. The "Hardened" Circuit: Dynorphin & DeltaFosB, P5*

**Now:**

> The Fix (The Unlock): You cannot "wait" for this to pass. We use "System Reformats" (Shrooms/Iboga) to stimulate GDNF, which physically rewires the brain around these hardened loops.

**As it would read:**

> The Fix (The Unlock): DeltaFosB does fade on its own, but slowly, and the same fears keep making more. So we use "System Reformats" (Shrooms/Iboga). Iboga raised GDNF, a growth factor that protects dopamine neurons, in rats (He, et al, 2005), and one dose of psilocybin grew new connections in mouse brains that lasted a month (Shao, et al, 2021). Whether either loosens DeltaFosB's hold hasn't been tested.

**What changes:** "You cannot 'wait' for this to pass" becomes "DeltaFosB does fade on its own, but slowly, and the same fears keep making more". "To stimulate GDNF, which physically rewires the brain" becomes what each was shown to do, and that it's untested for this.

**Why:** DeltaFosB's half-life in the brain is up to about eight days ([Robison & Nestler, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8879420)), so it does fade; your block 46 says the same fears keep making more, and the timeline (block 182) has it degraded by week 8. "You cannot wait" contradicted both. Iboga raised GDNF in rats ([He, et al, 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1193648)); psilocybin grew new spines in mice that lasted a month ([Shao, et al, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34228959/)), which isn't GDNF (flag F-8).

**What stays:** "The Fix (The Unlock)" and "System Reformats" (Shrooms/Iboga).

**You're deciding:** Whether the paragraph says DeltaFosB fades slowly, and what each tool was shown to do.

## SC-52: Brain fog: some regions burn less glucose in PTSD

*D. The "Lagging" System: Toxicity & Hypometabolism (The Fog), P1*

**Now:**

> The Problem: "Brain Fog" is often a fuel crisis. Trauma causes cerebral insulin resistance--your neurons are swimming in glucose (sugar) but cannot burn it. The engine is flooded.

**As it would read:**

> The Problem: "Brain Fog" may partly be a fuel problem. In brain scans of people with PTSD, some regions burn less glucose (sugar), and the less they burn, the worse the symptoms (Jeong, et al, 2019).

**What changes:** "Trauma causes cerebral insulin resistance--your neurons are swimming in glucose (sugar) but cannot burn it. The engine is flooded." becomes what scans of people with PTSD show.

**Why:** Brain insulin resistance is reported after [head injury](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6114023/), not psychological trauma, and nothing shows neurons "swimming in glucose". In people with PTSD, parts of the insula, prefrontal cortex and striatum take up less glucose, the less the worse the symptoms ([Jeong, et al, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6820768)) (flag F-9).

**What stays:** "The Problem: 'Brain Fog'" and the fuel idea.

**You're deciding:** Whether the fog paragraph rests on the PTSD scans instead of insulin resistance.

**My advice:** Approve.

## SC-53: Ketones and PQQ, as far as they're shown; the shower sentence out

This turns the lights back on immediately. We alternate hot and cold showers to boost p131 and heat shock proteins and reduce inflammation.

**As it would read:**

> The Fix: If the brain is burning less sugar, we give it another fuel. Ketones are its backup fuel, and brains that take up less glucose (as in early Alzheimer's) still take up ketones normally (Cunnane, et al, 2016). PQQ made mouse cells build more mitochondria, the parts of the cell that burn fuel (Chowanadisai, et al, 2010); whether it does that in the human brain hasn't been shown.

**What changes:** Ketones and PQQ are said as far as the studies go; "This turns the lights back on immediately" and the hot and cold showers sentence come out.

**Why:** Brains that take up less glucose (in mild cognitive impairment and early Alzheimer's) still take up ketones normally ([Cunnane, et al, 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/molecular-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnmol.2016.00053/text)). PQQ made mouse cells build mitochondria ([Chowanadisai, et al, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2804159)); nothing shows it in the human brain, and nothing shows "immediately". The PI31 study raised it by gene therapy in mice; nothing tests showers, and the heat-shock protein studies keep people in hot water 40 to 60 minutes (flag F-10). The shower routine in Part 3 comes up again in the read of the whole protocol.

**What stays:** "The Fix" and the switch of fuels.

**You're deciding:** Whether the shower sentence leaves this paragraph, and ketones and PQQ are said as far as shown.

## SC-56: The Jammer: fewer flashbacks, not a permanently reduced sting

*E. The "Save Button": Memory Reconsolidation, P2*

**Now:**

> The Fix ("The Jammer"): Memories require visual processing power to be restored. If you occupy the Visual-Spatial Working Memory (e.g., playing Tetris) during the critical window after a flashback, the brain fails to "save" the visual details. The memory is restored, but the emotional "sting" is permanently reduced.

**As it would read:**

> The Fix ("The Jammer"): Memories require visual processing power to be restored. If you occupy the Visual-Spatial Working Memory (e.g., playing Tetris) during the critical window after a flashback, the brain fails to "save" the visual details as strongly, and the memory flashes back less often. In the lab, a reminder of a day-old memory followed by Tetris cut its flashbacks, and neither one alone did (James, et al, 2015).

**What changes:** "The memory is restored, but the emotional 'sting' is permanently reduced" becomes "the memory flashes back less often", with the study that shows it.

**Why:** What the Tetris studies measure is how often a memory intrudes: a reminder followed by Tetris cut flashbacks of a day-old memory, and neither alone did ([James, et al, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26133572/)); after real car crashes, a reminder and about 20 minutes of Tetris cut intrusive memories over the next week, but not other symptoms or the count at one month ([Iyadurai, et al, 2018](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:111fe953-8e08-465a-b490-a596b9f0e7e0)). Nothing shows a permanent change in the sting. A fresh reader said the car-crash sentence wasn't needed, so it's here and not in the text; the lab study is the closer one for an old memory.

**What stays:** "Memories require visual processing power to be restored" and the Tetris instruction.

**You're deciding:** Whether the Jammer promises fewer flashbacks rather than a permanently reduced sting.

**My advice:** Approve.
