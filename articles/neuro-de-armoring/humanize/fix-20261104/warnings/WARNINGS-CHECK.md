## Agmatine, paragraph by paragraph

The agmatine check's verdicts, against every place the article has agmatine (paragraph numbers as the marking page
numbers them):

- **What agmatine does** (A Complete Guide to Bio-De-Armoring P8; Glutamate Excitotoxicity P9): supported. Keep.
- **The interactions** (P9, your own version): keep opioids and kratom ("will reduce tolerance" is what the animal work
  found), clonidine, guanfacine and blood-pressure-lowering medications, and lower-end blood pressure. Fold nitrates and
  PDE-5 inhibitors into "blood pressure lowering medications", which already covers them, or cut them. Cut MAOIs (MAO doesn't
  break agmatine down, and in rodents agmatine reduced harmaline's tremor), bipolar disorder, unstable panic and relapse
  (in animals agmatine eased withdrawal and cut opioid self-administration). Psychosis: keep, or soften. Your call:
  pregnancy and breast-feeding, kidney or liver issues, and "complex psych stacks", which can carry the general caution.
- **When to stop**, and the idea that agmatine can make you flat (A Complete Guide P10; Agmatine vs PS vs Phosphatidylcholine
  P4; the Glutamate Shield's dose, poor-fit and cycle lines, P4, P6 and P7; the Timeline's P4, P5 and P10). Only the stomach
  side effects showed up in the human trials, and in animals agmatine acted against depression. Whether flatness and the
  rest of the list stay is question 4: they stay if you, or reports you trust, have seen them.
- **The dose cap** ("cap at 1 g/day unless clinician-supervised", the Glutamate Shield P4 and the Timeline P4): the studied
  doses were higher (1.3 to 3.6 g a day for up to three weeks). The cap is the protocol's choice; "unless
  clinician-supervised" could go. Your call.
- **Not on D-serine or DCS days** (Glutamate Excitotoxicity P10; the Glutamate Shield P8): the protocol's logic, since
  D-serine and DCS boost NMDA receptors and agmatine blocks them. Keep.

## Warning by warning

### 5-HTP

1. **The interaction list** (W013, W014: "Do not combine it with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, linezolid, lithium, tramadol,
   methadone, fentanyl, DXM, MDMA, St. John's wort, SAM-e, L-tryptophan, or other serotonin-raising stacks without
   clinician review"). Supported as a class rule. Human reports with 5-HTP itself that I found: mania when 5-HTP was added
   to an MAOI ([Pardo 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.genhosppsych.2011.08.014)) and serotonin syndrome with linezolid in a
   patient who had taken 5-HTP ([Ostabal Artigas 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.medcli.2015.03.010)). In the FDA's
   reporting system, oxitriptane (5-HTP) and tryptophan are among the ten substances with the highest serotonin syndrome
   reporting ratios, with MAO inhibitors and linezolid
   ([Elli 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00228-023-03596-z)). For the rest the support is the drug class: tramadol,
   fentanyl, methadone and dextromethorphan are the opioids most tied to serotonin syndrome
   ([Baldo & Rose 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2019.08.010);
   [Rickli 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/bph.14105)); fatal MDMA with the MAOI moclobemide
   ([Pilgrim 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forsciint.2011.04.008)); St. John's wort with fluoxetine and triptans
   ([Bonetto 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2982.2007.01430.x)); SAMe in one serotonin-syndrome-like case in a trial
   ([Sarris 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroneuro.2018.07.098)). Lithium is the weakest item: a review lists it as a
   contributor ([Badar 2024](https://doi.org/10.4103/jfcm.jfcm_236_23)), and I found no lithium and 5-HTP report. I also
   found no 5-HTP and SSRI case. **Keep.** Lithium could go without loss.
2. **Ayahuasca and harmalas** (W015). Supported. The MAO-inhibiting harmalas with SSRIs are a known route to serotonin
   syndrome ([Callaway & Grob 1998](https://doi.org/10.1080/02791072.1998.10399712)), and a 2026 case report has severe
   serotonin syndrome from ayahuasca plus over-the-counter dextromethorphan
   ([Pack 2026](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.114211)). Ibogaine part: see the iboga item below. **Keep.**
3. **Same-day psychedelics, and "nowhere near" ayahuasca or iboga** (W016, W049, W063, W083). The ayahuasca, MDMA, DXM and
   tramadol parts are covered above. For classic psychedelics (psilocybin, LSD) with 5-HTP, **No study found**; it is a
   mechanism-based caution. **Keep, or soften**: say "ayahuasca, MDMA, DXM, tramadol" firmly and keep "same-day
   psychedelics" as a conservative choice.
4. **"5-HTP is serotonergic because it is a direct serotonin precursor"** and that it "can change brain sensitivity
   quickly" (W012). The first half is basic and reviewed ([Turner et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pharmthera.2005.06.004)).
   "Repair-oriented levers that change brain sensitivity quickly" for 5-HTP and uridine: **No study found.**
   **Keep, or soften**: drop the second half or mark it as the protocol's idea.
5. **Who it is not for** (W039: "If you are already agitated, manic, on serotonergic meds, or using serotonergic
   psychedelics/entactogens, this is not a casual experiment"). Supported by the mania case on an MAOI (Pardo 2011) and the
   interaction reports above. **Keep.**
6. **Side effects** (W043: nausea, stomach discomfort, loose stools, headache, vivid dreams, insomnia, sweating, tremor,
   anxiety, irritability, "too-bright / hypomanic"). The serotonin-excess ones (sweating, tremor, agitation, diarrhea) match
   the known syndrome ([Bodner et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.1212/wnl.45.2.219)); hypomania has the MAOI case. The
   abstracts I opened do not list nausea, headache or vivid dreams for 5-HTP. **Keep, or soften**: shorten to the ones that
   point to too much serotonin.
7. **Stop signs** (W044, W045: "agitation + sweating + diarrhea + tremor/clonus + fever, confusion, fast heart rate, or
   unstable blood pressure ... can indicate serotonin toxicity"). Matches the clinical picture: confusion, agitation,
   myoclonus, shivering, diarrhea, diaphoresis, fever, autonomic instability (Bodner 1995), and linezolid cases with high
   blood pressure and fast heart rate ([Shahbazi & Shojaei 2025](https://doi.org/10.2174/0115748863299100240507052341)).
   **Keep** both.
8. **"Hard interaction rule" and "case literature warns ... mostly a multi-drug stack problem"** (W046, W047). Supported:
   most serotonin toxicity comes from a combination of two or more serotonergic drugs (Baldo & Rose 2019). **Keep.**
9. **"See the dietary supplement case report and MedlinePlus"** (W048). The case reports above fit. I could not open the
   MedlinePlus page, so check that link yourself. **Keep.**
10. **Starting dose, 50 mg at night then 100 mg** (W041). A design choice; no dose limit comes from the studies I opened.
    **The protocol's own logic.**

### Iboga and ibogaine

11. **"Never combine Iboga with most medications (especially heart & psych meds)"** and "check all the dangerous iboga
    drug interactions" (W002, W070). Supported for heart and psych drugs. Ibogaine is broken down mainly by CYP2D6, drugs
    that share that enzyme can change its levels, it blocks the heart's hERG channel, prolongs QT and has caused sudden
    deaths (27 reported in one review), including in people without known heart disease
    ([Litjens & Brunt 2016](https://doi.org/10.3109/15563650.2016.1138226)). Noribogaine keeps QT prolonged for days: one
    patient had QTc 647 ms with torsades 12 days on ([Henstra 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/15563650.2017.1287372)); in
    human heart cells both slow repolarization ([Rubi 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12012-016-9366-y); lab only); and
    adverse events continue at the doses used ([Schep 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2016.07.005)). W070 **Keep.**
    W002 **Keep, or soften**: "most medications" is wider than the evidence; name QT-prolonging, CYP2D6 and serotonergic
    drugs.
12. **Ibogaine and noribogaine "interact with serotonin transport biology"** (W015). Litjens & Brunt say they act on the
    serotonin system among others; I did not find a transporter-specific source I opened. Covered by item 2. **Keep.**

### Uridine (UMP)

13. **Dream vividness, visual sensitivity, craving and psychedelic tolerance** (W017) and "if UMP makes dreams vivid or
    suddenly lowers psychedelic tolerance, treat that as a safety signal" (W037). **No study found** of uridine and dreams,
    psychedelics or tolerance. **Cut** both, or keep only as your own experience reports.
14. **"Choline headache"** (W033). **No study found.** The mechanism (UMP speeds membrane building while choline is short)
    is the protocol's guess; I did not verify the Wurtman papers it leans on. **Cut**, unless you or people you trust have
    seen it.
15. **Other stop signs** (W034: insomnia, jitteriness, racing thoughts, irritability, flatness, nausea, cramps, loose
    stools; W035, W036: "if it makes you wired, numb, or weirdly irritable, stop. Do not push through"). **No study
    found** for oral uridine side effects. The advice itself is sound common sense. **Your call.**
16. **Not on psychedelic days** (W038, W062, W082). Without a study behind the safety reason, this is a rule to keep one
    variable at a time. **The protocol's own logic.**
17. **"Who this is for" and the evidence hedge** (W030, W031). Design and honest caveat, not warnings. I did not re-check
    the cited rat work. **The protocol's own logic.**
18. **"Do not start both together, and do not use either to force stronger psychedelic effects"** (W068). Same: one
    variable at a time. **The protocol's own logic.**

### Phosphatidylserine (PS)

19. **300 to 600 mg, "only if you fit the high-cortisol profile"** (W072). Partly supported. In stress tests, 400 mg of a
    PS and phosphatidic acid complex blunted the cortisol response, while 600 and 800 mg did not
    ([Hellhammer 2004](https://doi.org/10.1080/10253890410001728379)); 400 mg worked only in chronically stressed men
    with an over-reactive stress response, 200 mg did nothing
    ([Hellhammer 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/1476-511X-13-121)). So "high-stress only" holds up, but the range is
    wider than what worked. All small studies in men. **Keep, or soften**: "around 400 mg".
20. **Over-suppression or "flat" signs** (W076, W028: "If PS leaves you flat OR more wired, you're not a high-cortisol
    responder - drop it"; "don't raise the dose to push through - stop"). **No study found** of PS causing flatness. A
    trial of omega-3 PS found it restored the cortisol response of low responders rather than lowering it further
    ([Hellhammer 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nutres.2012.03.003)). The same paragraph's "low blood pressure,
    water-weight gain, restless legs ... cortisol going too low" has no source either (**Cut** that sentence). The stop
    rule itself is sensible. **The protocol's own logic.**
21. **Drop or taper PS in the Void phase** (W079, W080, W085). **No study found** that PS deepens numbness. A phase
    design choice. **The protocol's own logic.**

### D-serine

22. **"Direct D-Serine carries a higher renal load than L-Serine"** (W064). The kidney concern is real but from rats:
    D-serine kills proximal tubule cells at 250 mg/kg and above
    ([Williams 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tox.2004.08.023)). A safety review says rat damage needs doses above about
    500 mg/kg, has not been seen in mice or rabbits, and across all human studies only one person had abnormal kidney
    values, which cleared in days ([Meftah 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2021.726365)). I found no direct human
    comparison with L-serine. **Keep, or soften**: "a kidney caution, based on rat studies".
23. **"Do not exceed 30 mg/kg"** (W065). The highest human dose tested is 120 mg/kg (Meftah 2021), so 30 mg/kg is a
    cautious choice, not a studied limit. **Your call.** "Ensure high water intake" has no source either.
24. **Keto or high protein compounds the kidney load** (W066). **No study found.** Keto diets raise kidney stone risk in
    epilepsy ([Feng 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2025.1723370)), which is a different problem. **Cut.**
25. **Related, "conversion is inefficient ... D-serine has higher risk/benefit"** (W022). A design remark; the safety
    side is item 22. **The protocol's own logic.**

### Potassium and salt

26. **"You must hit 4,700 mg of Potassium daily"** (W056). The 2019 U.S. National Academies value is an adequate intake, a
    target for healthy people, and falling short "does not indicate K deficiency risk"
    ([Kumssa 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu13041369)). I could not open a source that states the number itself.
    **Keep, or soften**: "aim for", not "must".
27. **"Supplements are dangerous and limited to 99mg"** (W057). Too strong. A 2025 randomized trial of 1,200 people with
    implanted defibrillators used potassium supplements, sometimes with a drug, to hold potassium in the high-normal range
    and cut the main risk outcome (about 23% vs 29%), with similar hospital stays for high or low potassium
    (the POTCAST trial; its prespecified analysis, with the same figures: [Danielsen et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/europace/euag272)). That was supervised. I found no source I opened for the
    99 mg cap or for kidney-disease risks. **Keep, or soften**: "unsupervised potassium pills are not worth the risk if you
    have kidney disease or take ACE inhibitors, and the 99 mg is a label norm".
28. **"DO NOT load extra salt (Risk of High Blood Pressure)"** (W053). **No study found** in my searches for this keto
    setting. A keto review says electrolyte supplements have a physiological rationale but lack trials
    ([Skartun 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2025.1538266)). **Your call.**
29. **"Consult a doctor if you have kidney disease or heart conditions"** (W001). Generic label line. **Your call.**

### Heat and tuna

30. **No hot showers if resting heart rate is up or HRV is down** (W069, W071, W084). **No study found** that tests an
    HRV rule for showers. A direct test: 60 minutes in 40 C water raised heart rate and blood pressure less than the same
    time of exercise in heat ([Campbell 2022](https://doi.org/10.1113/EP089992)), healthy people, much longer than a
    shower. A conservative self-check. **The protocol's own logic.**
31. **"Don't lean on tuna daily - mercury"** (W027). Supported. In a 2026 market study of 20 fish, tuna had the highest
    mercury, and its methylmercury risk score was twice the safe value
    ([Polak-Juszczak & Barcz 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fct.2026.115974)). **Keep.**
