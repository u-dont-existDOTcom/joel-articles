## Part 2: Reducing Anxiety & Depression

Lower the Cortisol Floor (Phosphatidylserine)

Before ketones and before D-Serine, deal with cortisol directly, because cortisol is what sensitizes the brain to Glutamate and locks the armor in place (see the Isolation Trap above). The single most useful nutrient I’ve found for this is Phosphatidylserine (PS), a phospholipid that blunts the HPA-axis cortisol response to stress.

It’s for people running high or *reactive* cortisol, the “wired and tired” type who can’t downshift and keeps waking-at-3-a.m. That includes many people with slow COMT (the Val158Met variant that clears catecholamines slowly, so stress hormones linger and you stay on high alert). My friend Scott has exactly this profile, and he got more out of PS than anything else in this protocol. To be honest about the evidence, though, the cortisol-lowering effect is best documented in *chronically stressed* people, and the slow-COMT angle is mechanism plus my own observation; it hasn’t been shown in a controlled study.

The dose is 300–600 mg/day, earlier in the day. Below ~300 mg the cortisol effect is unreliable, and above ~800 mg it adds nothing.

PS is also one of the few supplements you can eat your way to. Atlantic mackerel (~480 mg/100g) and herring (~360 mg) are richest, and tuna is lower (~194 mg). A 200g mackerel fillet ≈ 960 mg PS, which is above clinical doses. (Don’t lean on tuna daily, because of the mercury.)

⚠️ But again, PS is only for genuinely high cortisol, and there are two ways to find out you guessed wrong. PS works by *blunting* cortisol, so if yours isn’t actually elevated, lowering it pushes you down the other side of the curve, where the symptoms look almost identical to high cortisol. Watch for either signal:

Flat / numb / no drive: you’ve over-suppressed, the classic too-little-cortisol picture.

More wired, not less: racing thoughts, 3 a.m. waking, irritability, vivid or stressful dreams. This one surprises people, but when cortisol drops too far, the body leans on adrenaline to hold up blood pressure and alertness, and you end up feeling *more* stressed.

Either one means PS is the wrong tool for you *right now*. Don’t raise the dose to push through; stop. If you want to know before you start, a 4-point salivary cortisol test tells you whether you’re actually high. (A minority also report low blood pressure, water-weight gain, or restless legs, all consistent with cortisol going too low.)

If you can’t test, a rough rule: wired, tired, can’t switch off, 3 a.m. waking means it’s probably high, and PS is worth a trial. Numb, low-drive and flat means it’s probably *not* high, and PS will likely make it worse, so skip it or test first.

### Agmatine vs PS vs Phosphatidylcholine

Do not confuse these three tools:

Phosphatidylserine (PS) is the cortisol-floor tool, only for the high-cortisol “wired and tired” profile.

Phosphatidylcholine (PC) is a membrane/choline nutrient. It may support general cell-membrane and acetylcholine biology, but it doesn’t specifically lower cortisol or put the brakes on glutamate.

Agmatine is different from both. It’s an optional glutamate/NO/imidazoline “voltage governor,” and it belongs in the Shield section; it isn’t a replacement for PS or PC. Like PS, it can overcorrect the system: PS can push low-cortisol people into flatness, and agmatine can push already-numb people into more emotional blunting.
