## Psychology is the Software; Biology is the Hardware.

Most therapy works on the “code” (thoughts/behaviors) and leaves the “motherboard” alone, though the brain’s physical structure and chemistry are often overheating or damaged. So this protocol starts with four specific neuro-biological mechanisms, which must be addressed *before* the psychological tools can be effective.

Dr. Bruce Perry compares treating trauma to building a house. CBT & EMDR style therapies try to “decorate” the house by changing thoughts/behaviors in the Cortex, while the foundation (the Brainstem) is crumbling underneath. Good luck hanging curtains in a house that’s falling down.

The Modern “Role-Self” vs. The Ancient “True-Self”

But we can’t just blame our immature parents for this Role Self. It really is true what they say: “It takes a village to raise a child,” and a big modern society where one or two people look after the children is nowhere near enough. As Dr. Bruce Perry explains, our brains evolved in hunter-gatherer bands with a ratio of 4 developmentally mature adults to 1 child.

Today, our “enriched” environments offer 1 immature adult to 4 children. That’s less than 1/24th of the relational density our biology is built for, so when people tell me about the “Void” they feel, I take it literally. Once you’ve de-armored, you can’t go back to a lonely life: you must aggressively engineer a “High-Density” social environment (a tribe), or the brain will eventually reach for the armor again just to survive the cold.

As we grow up, our “Role-Self” becomes calcified by Dopamine and DeltaFosB. It becomes a prison. Dr. Perry also says we need to temporarily give up on the “hour-long breakthrough” that therapy is supposed to deliver. So we go for tiny doses of safety instead, like 30 seconds of regulated breathing, twenty times a day. That’s closer to physical therapy for a broken nervous system, where frequency counts for more than intensity.

### Part 1: THE SCIENCE (Why We Do This)

Before applying the tools, you must understand the specific “Hardware Failures” that occur during trauma, withdrawal, or chronic stress.

#### A. The Metabolic Red Line

We tend to assume we can “think” our way out of a trigger, and the data says otherwise: anatomy overrides intellect. Dr. Perry describes a “Metabolic Red Line” for the traumatized brain. When the resting heart rate is elevated or heart-rate-variability reduced (a state of “Fear”), the brain physically shunts blood flow away from the Cortex (Logic/Reason) and down to the Brainstem (Survival).

Functional IQ drops substantially in that state. Worse, it floods the Nucleus Accumbens with the exact cocktail of catecholamines that *transcribes* new DeltaFosB proteins (the “armor”). So trying to process trauma while you’re living above this red line actively re-enforces the armor.

#### B. The “Overheating” CPU: Glutamate Excitotoxicity

Glutamate is the brain’s primary excitatory neurotransmitter, and under stress the brain floods with it, the “voltage spike” where the engine is redlining.

On the Metabolic Red Line, the more careful version is this: when trauma survivors become highly dysregulated, prefrontal cortical functioning often decreases while survival-oriented neural systems become more dominant. In this state, learning and memory processes may favor reinforcement of existing fear networks rather than integration of traumatic material, which is why many trauma therapies emphasize staying within a manageable “window of tolerance” instead of overwhelming activation. You can often tell when you’re dysregulated by noticing if your heart is beating faster or if your heart-rate variability declines. DeltaFosB may be the master switch, but the research isn’t settled.

Excess Glutamate over-activates NMDA receptors, like a key stuck in the “On” position, and causes “excitotoxicity”, which literally means vibrating cells to death. From the inside it feels like anxiety, rage, or an “itchy brain.”

There’s also a second dial, the GluD receptors. Beyond the “voltage spike” of NMDA, [Johns Hopkins researchers have recently discovered](https://scitechdaily.com/scientists-find-how-to-dial-brain-signals-up-or-down-to-treat-mental-and-neurological-disorders/) the brain uses GluD receptors as a master volume knob. If NMDA is the spark, GluD sets the baseline “hum” of the synapse, and in an armored state this dial is often jammed in the “High” position (Cerebellar Ataxia/Hyper-vigilance) or “Low” (Apathy/Schizotypy).

D-serine may support therapeutic memory updating through NMDA-dependent learning mechanisms, while also engaging GluD receptors that regulate synaptic activity/tone. The drug form that has been studied is D-cycloserine, which has evidence as an exposure-therapy augmenter because it can facilitate NMDA-dependent consolidation of extinction learning. It doesn’t erase fear directly. It seems to strengthen whatever learning happens during or after the exposure session, which is why the quality of the session matters so much: if the exposure produces corrective learning and ends with reduced fear or mastery, DCS (and possibly D-serine also) may help consolidate that new memory, and if it’s overwhelming or ends badly, it may consolidate the wrong association.

The Fix (”Lock, Stock, and Barrel”):The Vacuum (NAC): Clears the flood by exchanging glutamate for cystine.

The Lock (Zinc/Magnesium): Physically blocks the receptor channel to stop the voltage.

The Repair (Leucine/L-Serine): L-Serine acts as a precursor to D-Serine to recalibrate the GluD ‘Volume Dial’ to enhance therapeutic sessions that end with low fear/high mastery (although conversion to D-serine is inefficient, which is why some people use D-serine itself which has higher risk/benefit). Leucine facilitates structural repair and also tunes synaptic gain, which takes the edge off the ‘itchy brain’ sensation.

The Governor (Agmatine): Agmatine is the optional “voltage governor.” It may help when the problem goes beyond excess glutamate in the synapse to NMDA/GluN2B over-reactivity, the hot, itchy, reactive, pain-y, rumination-heavy version of the Glutamate Storm. It’s a state-dependent brake, so it doesn’t belong with the daily foundation nutrients.

Important conflict: do not use agmatine as a same-session companion to D-Serine/DCS-style exposure or EMDR work. The D-Serine/DCS-style tools are there to support NMDA-dependent learning and memory updating, while agmatine partially brakes NMDA activity. Agmatine is for storm control, and it shouldn’t be taken right before a session where the goal is deliberate reconsolidation.

#### C. The “Hardened” Circuit: Dynorphin & DeltaFosB

To protect itself from the Glutamate storm, the brain eventually releases Dynorphin, a chemical blanket that suppresses dopamine. That creates the “Void”, a state of apathy and grayness where you can’t feel pleasure.

If you’ve tried SSRIs (antidepressants) and they failed you, you’re not crazy. In Ipser & Stein’s [2012 study](https://academic.oup.com/ijnp/article/15/6/825/761323?login=false), Prozac had zero effect on male combat veterans with calcified PTSD, and the side effects are well-known, including anhedonia, zombification, and sexual dysfunction.

They don’t work on calcified PTSD because standard serotonin drugs treat the “software” (mood) and can’t get through to the “hardware”, meaning the structural changes in the amygdala caused by severe trauma. This protocol uses Ketones and HDAC Inhibitors to remodel that physical structure, the part of the brain that resists standard medication.

The hardening itself comes from chronic cycles, which lead to the accumulation of DeltaFosB, a protein switch that permanently alters gene expression to cement the trauma circuitry ([Vialou, et al, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20473292/)).

## Part 2: Reducing Anxiety & Depression

Lower the Cortisol Floor (Phosphatidylserine)

Before ketones and before D-Serine, deal with cortisol directly, because cortisol is what sensitizes the brain to Glutamate and locks the armor in place (see the Isolation Trap above). The single most useful nutrient I’ve found for this is Phosphatidylserine (PS), a phospholipid that blunts the HPA-axis cortisol response to stress.

It’s for people running high or *reactive* cortisol, the “wired and tired” type who can’t downshift and keeps waking-at-3-a.m. That includes many people with slow COMT (the Val158Met variant that clears catecholamines slowly, so stress hormones linger and you stay on high alert). My friend Scott has exactly this profile, and he got more out of PS than anything else in this protocol. To be honest about the evidence, though, the cortisol-lowering effect is best documented in *chronically stressed* people, and the slow-COMT angle is mechanism plus my own observation; it hasn’t been shown in a controlled study.

The dose is 300–600 mg/day, earlier in the day. Below ~300 mg the cortisol effect is unreliable, and above ~800 mg it adds nothing.

PS is also one of the few supplements you can eat your way to. Atlantic mackerel (~480 mg/100g) and herring (~360 mg) are richest, and tuna is lower (~194 mg). A 200g mackerel fillet ≈ 960 mg PS, which is above clinical doses. (Don’t lean on tuna daily, because of the mercury.)

⚠️ But again, PS is only for genuinely high cortisol, and there are two ways to find out you guessed wrong. PS works by *blunting* cortisol, so if yours isn’t actually elevated, lowering it pushes you down the other side of the curve, where the symptoms look almost identical to high cortisol. Watch for either signal:

Flat / numb / no drive: you’ve over-suppressed, the classic too-little-cortisol picture.

More wired, not less: racing thoughts, 3 a.m. waking, irritability, vivid or stressful dreams. This one surprises people, but when cortisol drops too far, the body leans on adrenaline to hold up blood pressure and alertness, and you end up feeling *more* stressed.

Either one means PS is the wrong tool for you *right now*. Don’t raise the dose to push through; stop. If you want to know before you start, a 4-point salivary cortisol test tells you whether you’re actually high. (A minority also report low blood pressure, water-weight gain, or restless legs, all consistent with cortisol going too low.)

If you can’t test, a rough rule: wired, tired, can’t switch off, 3 a.m. waking means it’s probably high, and PS is worth a trial. Numb, low-drive and flat means it’s probably *not* high, and PS will likely make it worse, so skip it or test first.

### Agmatine vs PS vs Phosphatidylcholine

Do not confuse these three tools:

Phosphatidylserine (PS) is the cortisol-floor tool, only for the high-cortisol “wired and tired” profile.

Phosphatidylcholine (PC) is a membrane/choline nutrient. It may support general cell-membrane and acetylcholine biology, but it doesn’t specifically lower cortisol or put the brakes on glutamate.

Agmatine is different from both. It’s an optional glutamate/NO/imidazoline “voltage governor,” and it belongs in the Shield section; it isn’t a replacement for PS or PC. Like PS, it can overcorrect the system: PS can push low-cortisol people into flatness, and agmatine can push already-numb people into more emotional blunting.

### Dopamine Receptor Burnout / DAWS Pattern: Uridine Monophosphate

Uridine is for the specific “burned-out dopamine receptor” pattern, so it is not for everyone with depression. That pattern is gray anhedonia, weak dreams, weak psychedelic visuals, needing unusually high doses to feel anything, compulsively reaching for ketamine/LSD/novelty just to get a hit, and especially a history of dopamine agonist withdrawal from drugs like pramipexole or ropinirole. The medical literature calls this [dopamine agonist withdrawal syndrome](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23686524/) (DAWS) and describes anxiety, panic, dysphoria, depression, agitation, fatigue, autonomic symptoms, pain, and drug cravings after dopamine-agonist tapering. With Scott, the pattern looked like DAWS with D2/D3 reward-system downregulation, and then frequent LSD microdosing and ketamine attempts to force dopamine hits made it worse. So I’m talking about a specific phenotype here, and I’m not claiming everyone needs more dopamine.

The tool is Uridine monophosphate (UMP). There’s no study showing “UMP cures DAWS”; the evidence is more basic than that. Uridine, choline, and DHA are circulating precursors for brain phosphatide / synaptic membrane synthesis, and Wurtman’s group has shown that [DHA + uridine + choline can increase synaptic membrane components and synaptic proteins](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4011061/), that [DHA-induced dendritic spine formation is enhanced by UMP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2140951/) in animals, and that [dietary UMP increased potassium-evoked dopamine release and neurite outgrowth in aged rats](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16055952/). So the mechanism is plausible, but using it for DAWS is still a cautious human extrapolation plus anecdote.

Scott already had choline from eggs and DHA/EPA from fish oil, and after adding standard-dose UMP he got a striking response within a day: vivid dreams returned, real childhood memories came back in dreams, his psychedelic tolerance seemed to drop, and his ketamine/LSD craving fell hard. I wouldn’t conclude that UMP rebuilt D3 receptors overnight, but it does suggest that, in the right person, UMP can be a very clean signal that the synaptic-membrane / dopamine-receptor-recovery axis was starved.

To trial it: UMP 150–300 mg in the morning with food for 3–7 days. Keep omega-3s in place, especially DHA/EPA, and make sure there’s enough choline from eggs, phosphatidylcholine, CDP-choline, or another choline source you tolerate. Do not start UMP on the same day you start 5-HTP, agmatine, D-serine, keto, iboga, shrooms, LSD, ketamine, or any other major variable, since you’re trying to see whether UMP itself changes the signal.

The most common problem is the “choline headache” or a spaced-out brain fog: if UMP accelerates membrane synthesis while choline availability is low, the system can feel under-supplied. Other stop signs include insomnia, jitteriness, racing thoughts, irritability, emotional flatness, nausea, stomach cramps, or loose stools. If it makes you wired, numb, or weirdly irritable, stop. Do not push through.

If UMP makes your dreams vivid or suddenly lowers your psychedelic tolerance, treat that as a safety signal; that’s the psychedelic caution here. Do not take UMP on psychedelic days. And if you use psychedelics later, assume your previous dose may be too strong now, and that chasing the old visual threshold can overshoot.

### Serotonin Bottleneck Pattern: 5-HTP + Low-Dose P5P

5-HTP is for the low-serotonin-looking pattern, not for every depressed person: poor sleep initiation, low mood, low warmth, night anxiety, repetitive rumination, and a genetic or clinical reason to suspect a bottleneck in serotonin synthesis. Scott’s genetic data suggested a TPH2 bottleneck, and 5-HTP helped his sleep and mood. But if you’re already agitated, manic, on serotonergic meds, or using serotonergic psychedelics/entactogens, this is not a casual experiment.

5-HTP bypasses the tryptophan-to-5-HTP step and gives the body a more direct serotonin precursor. A [broad review of 5-HTP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7796270/) discusses its role in serotonin biology and its use in mood, anxiety, sleep, obesity, myoclonus, and serotonin-toxicity contexts. The evidence for depression and sleep is mixed, and weaker than people online make it sound, but the mechanism is real enough that responders can feel it quickly.

Scott’s version is 100 mg 5-HTP sublingually at night, with 10–20 mg P5P sublingually in the morning. The P5P is there as the active B6 cofactor for aromatic amino-acid decarboxylase, and it isn’t meant as a stimulant. He’s slow-COMT and sensitive to too much P5P, so the low dose matters, and more is not better.

To trial it, start lower if you’re sensitive: 50 mg 5-HTP at night for 2–3 nights, then 100 mg only if the lower amount is clearly helpful and not activating. P5P is optional, 10–20 mg in the morning, especially if 5-HTP feels like it “almost works” but isn’t converting cleanly. Do not combine the trial with UMP, agmatine, D-serine, psychedelics, or medication changes. When it’s the right tool, the first signal is usually in sleep quality, dream tone, mood warmth, or less rumination at night.

Side effects and stop signs: nausea, stomach discomfort, loose stools, headache, vivid or intense dreams, insomnia, sweating, tremor, anxiety, irritability, emotional agitation, or a too-bright / hypomanic feeling. Stop immediately if you get agitation + sweating + diarrhea + tremor/clonus + fever, confusion, fast heart rate, or unstable blood pressure, because that pattern can indicate serotonin toxicity, which can become dangerous.

The hard interaction rule is that 5-HTP should not be mixed casually with serotonin-raising drugs or supplements. Case literature and clinical reviews warn about serotonin syndrome risk when 5-HTP is combined with antidepressants or MAOIs, and serotonin syndrome is most often a multi-drug / stack problem (see the [dietary supplement interaction case report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5580516/) and the general serotonin-syndrome overview from [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/007272.htm)). For this readership, that means: avoid 5-HTP on psychedelic days, and be especially strict around [ayahuasca / harmala MAOI chemistry](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7678905/) and iboga / ibogaine.

If you’re low on potassium, which is common in high stress, your brakes fail. You can push the pedal (breathe), but the car won’t stop.
