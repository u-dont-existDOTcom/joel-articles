# EAET, experiential dynamic therapies, and missing approaches in the somatic-therapies research

**Research update: 26 November 2026.** This is a research brief and an editorial proposal, not replacement article prose. It compares treatment methods and evidence for particular indications. It does not identify a universally best therapy.

## Main finding

Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy (EAET) deserves serious consideration in the article. It combines active emotional work with a specific focus on persistent pain and physical symptoms. The 2025 systematic review explicitly includes it within experiential dynamic therapies (EDTs). Its most useful distinction from AEDP, ISTDP, and Affect Phobia Therapy is its clinical target and structured integration of pain education with emotional processing.

I did not identify a direct randomized trial comparing EAET with AEDP, ISTDP, or Affect Phobia Therapy. The evidence supports comparing their procedures and the strength of evidence for particular uses; it does not establish an efficacy ranking among them. [S1–S7]

The broader discovery pass also identified Pain Reprocessing Therapy, imagery rescripting, and cognitive functional therapy as consequential additions to the research. Emotion-Focused Therapy supplies another relevant experiential tradition. Written Exposure Therapy and DBT-PTSD are useful comparators when evaluating claims about trauma processing and preparation.

## How actively they challenge, and how they facilitate

Here, **challenge** means actively identifying and addressing an obstacle to the agreed therapeutic goal. **Facilitation** means what the therapist does to help an emotional or behavioral experience happen during the session. Neither is a validated numerical scale, and a warm therapist can be highly directive.

The following is a qualitative comparison of published treatment descriptions. It describes emphases, with substantial variation between clinicians, patients, formats, and phases of treatment.

| Approach | What the therapist actively challenges | How the therapist helps experience happen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **EAET — Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy** | Avoided feelings and interpersonal situations; habitual inhibition; beliefs about symptoms and about the consequences of emotional expression. | Pain education, identifying stress–symptom connections, expressive writing, imagery, chair/role-play work, bodily and vocal expression, and practice communicating differently in relationships. Often delivered through a structured course. [S2, S3, S7] |
| **ISTDP — Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy** | Defenses as they appear moment by moment: explaining instead of feeling, distancing, self-attack, or passive compliance. Standard formats can explicitly challenge a clarified defense. | Repeated invitations to notice and experience feelings, tracking bodily anxiety, examining what occurs with the therapist, and building capacity when anxiety becomes too disruptive. The graded format may use little or no direct defense challenge. [S4] |
| **APT — Affect Phobia Therapy** | Learned fear, shame, or guilt about feeling; avoidance and self-attacking responses that prevent emotional engagement. | Graded emotional exposure through memories, imagery, and relationships; anxiety regulation; interruption of avoidance; practice expressing feelings and receiving care. It explicitly combines dynamic formulation with exposure principles. [S5] |
| **AEDP — Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy** | Avoidance, self-negation, and difficulty receiving support or allowing emotional change. It can name defenses and examine their costs directly. | Slowing down, bodily tracking, attunement, shared emotional regulation, imagery/parts work, and examining the felt experience of relief, connection, and change afterward. The therapist's accompaniment is particularly prominent. [S6] |
| **Emotion-Focused Therapy — Greenberg's approach** | Self-criticism, emotional avoidance, and maladaptive emotional patterns. This is a neighboring humanistic/experiential tradition; it should not automatically be counted as a psychodynamic EDT. | Empathic exploration, bodily felt experience, two-chair or empty-chair tasks, and imagined encounters that develop new emotional responses to unmet needs. [S8] |

A simple ISTDP-high / AEDP-low ranking would obscure important clinical differences. Abbass explicitly describes supportive, graded ISTDP. AEDP's official intervention framework includes direct defense work alongside patient choice. EAET's manual can be quite demanding. The question for an actual therapist is how they choose and adjust their intervention, not merely which name appears on their training certificate.

### A concrete illustration

Suppose a person notices bodily tension after speaking with a parent, then spends the session explaining why the parent behaved that way. The following are **invented illustrations of the published methods, not transcripts or instructions for conducting treatment**:

- An **EAET** therapist might explore whether the tension accompanies an avoided emotional response, invite an imagined conversation, and help the person practice a different response in an actual relationship.
- An **ISTDP** therapist might call attention to the shift into explanation, examine what happens when the person turns toward their feelings, and adjust the work according to the anxiety that emerges.
- An **APT** therapist might investigate what feels dangerous about having or expressing the feeling, then arrange a tolerable emotional experiment.
- An **AEDP** therapist might help the person stay with the bodily experience while feeling accompanied, then explore what changes when someone understands and responds.
- An **Emotion-Focused Therapy** therapist might guide a chair dialogue that makes the person's criticism, hurt, unmet need, or protective response more accessible.

These are overlapping possibilities. They are useful for asking what a therapist actually does; they cannot predict which treatment will work best.

## What is distinctive about EAET?

The developers describe an integration of pain reappraisal, emotional exposure, psychodynamic defense work, experiential techniques, writing, assertiveness, and rescripting. That mixture makes EAET relevant to an article about how emotional experience and physical symptoms interact. It also makes the label “purely bottom-up” inaccurate. [S7]

Three questions should remain separate when discussing it:

1. Does the whole treatment improve a particular clinical outcome?
2. Which components contribute to that improvement?
3. Is the developers' explanation of the underlying condition correct?

A trial that answers the first question does not automatically answer the other two. Pain improvement after emotional work does not establish that suppressed emotion originally caused that person's pain, nor that all persistent pain has that cause.

### The anger issue

The historical group manual places considerable emphasis on anger. It describes a frequent progression from protective/angry feelings toward connecting feelings and compassion, and some exercises involve intense imagined expression. It also distinguishes therapy-room expression from more restrained, consequential communication in daily relationships. [S2]

That makes the user's objection relevant to treatment fit. The trials do not prove that anger is universally the deepest feeling, that everyone must uncover it, or that this particular emotional sequence is necessary. An article can report the treatment's effects while identifying its emotion theory as a theoretical commitment.

My practical criterion would be whether a therapist can investigate an emotion hypothesis without demanding that the patient confirm it. Disagreement with a therapist's interpretation should not become automatic evidence that the interpretation is true.

## EAET evidence: substantial promise, with mixed results

| Study | Design and treatment | Finding and interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fibromyalgia, 2017** | 230 participants in 40 groups randomized to EAET, CBT, or education; eight 90-minute sessions; six-month primary endpoint. | EAET did **not** significantly outperform CBT or education on the primary mean pain-severity outcome at six months. It had advantages on some secondary outcomes; at least 50% pain reduction occurred in 22.5% versus 8.3% for CBT. This is a mixed positive trial, not across-the-board superiority. [S9] |
| **Older veterans, 2024** | 126 participants, predominantly men, mean age about 72; EAET versus CBT; one individual and eight group sessions. | Model-estimated rates of at least 30% pain reduction were 63.5% versus 17.1% after treatment and 40.3% versus 14.2% six months later. Mean pain improvement favored EAET by 1.59/10 after treatment and 1.01/10 at follow-up. The selected population and therapist differences limit generalization. [S3] |
| **Internet EAET, 2026** | 154 people with persistent physical symptoms randomized to guided or unguided ten-week internet EAET. | No significant primary continuous-outcome difference. A secondary completer analysis favored guidance: 47.2% versus 29.6% achieved a three-point PHQ-15 reduction (one-sided p = .048); that difference was not maintained at follow-up. Thirteen participants had at least 50% symptom worsening. Both arms received EAET, limiting causal interpretation of changes over time. [S10] |

The veterans trial excluded several specific pain pathologies and significant cognitive impairment. Its pain findings should not be generalized to all chronic pain, brain-injury recovery, or primary PTSD treatment. [S3]

The 2026 trial tested the added value of weekly written therapist guidance within an online program. It did not compare self-help with standard individual or group EAET, or establish equivalence between delivery formats. [S10]

### How this compares with the evidence for the other EDTs

| Evidence | What it contributes | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **2025 EDT meta-analysis** | 57 RCTs and 4,330 participants. Against active treatments, the pooled advantage was small and nonsignificant at treatment end (g = 0.17 in EDT's favor), increasing to about 0.40 at follow-up. Heterogeneity was substantial; 40.4% of trials were classified as low quality. [S1] | A ranking among EDT brands; an AEDP-specific effect; independent replication of EAET studies already included in the review. |
| **AEDP practice research** | A 2020 naturalistic study of 62 self-referred adults found improvement after 16 sessions; a later report followed the practice-network cohort. [S11, S12] | Causal superiority over another treatment. I did not identify a published randomized trial of standalone, standard face-to-face AEDP in this update. AEDP-informed internet or combined psychedelic protocols require separate identification. |
| **ISTDP, Halifax depression trial** | Sixty people with treatment-resistant depression were randomized to 20 sessions of ISTDP or routine secondary care. Depression outcomes favored ISTDP at six months. [S13] | Superiority over EAET, AEDP, or another equally intensive psychotherapy; transfer of depression results to pain without separate evidence. |
| **McCullough-derived dynamic therapy / APT lineage** | A 50-person trial in cluster-C personality disorders compared 40 sessions of dynamic and cognitive therapy. Both improved; between-group differences were not significant. [S14] | That APT is superior, inferior, or exactly equivalent to EAET. |
| **Emotion-Focused Therapy** | A small randomized depression study assigned 38 patients to EFT or client-centered therapy; symptom improvement favored EFT. [S15] | A comparative ranking against the EDTs or an effect for Emotional Freedom Techniques/tapping. |

The useful conclusion is **greater evidential specificity**, not a universal winner. EAET has direct comparative evidence for selected pain presentations. AEDP has a close conceptual fit with attachment and embodied emotional experience but less direct causal outcome evidence. ISTDP and APT have randomized evidence in other clinical settings. Those distinctions should guide what the article says each therapy is supported for.

## Further therapies the discovery pass brought into view

### Pain Reprocessing Therapy: compare directly with EAET's rationale

PRT targets the interpretation of certain persistent pain as dangerous. Its protocol combines pain education with experiential changes in attention, fear, and behavior. Emotional work can occur, but its published emphasis differs from EAET's focus on avoided interpersonal emotion.

In a 151-person randomized study of primary chronic back pain, 66% assigned to PRT reported pain of 0–1/10 after treatment, versus 20% assigned to an open-label placebo injection and 10% to usual care. Those controls were not full courses of another psychotherapy. [S16]

A five-year report followed the same cohort: 113 participants responded; 21/38 PRT respondents, 10/39 placebo respondents, and 13/36 usual-care respondents reported pain of 0–1/10. These are respondent percentages and long-term follow-up, not a new independent replication. [S17]

**Editorial value:** place EAET and PRT near one another so readers can see that emotional conflict and fear of bodily sensations are distinct possible treatment targets. Their different trials do not determine which approach is better for the same patient.

### Imagery rescripting: a concrete connection to reparenting

Imagery rescripting revisits a difficult memory imaginatively while changing what happens in the scene to meet needs for protection, care, or agency. In one tested protocol, the therapist initially intervenes in the imagined scene; later the patient's adult self does so.

An international trial randomized 155 adults with childhood-trauma-related PTSD to imagery rescripting or EMDR, for up to twelve 90-minute sessions. Both improved, with no significant between-treatment differences on standardized measures and 7.7% treatment dropout. Nonsignificance alone does not prove exact equivalence. [S18]

**Editorial value:** this supplies a named, research-linked intervention adjacent to the article's Nurturer/Protector and inner-child material. Its trial does not validate every practice called reparenting or establish the historical accuracy of an imagined scene.

### Cognitive functional therapy: bodily learning through movement

CFT is an individualized physiotherapy approach addressing pain-related beliefs, emotion, movement, and behavior. Its relevance is strongest where the article includes persistent pain and returning to bodily activity.

The 492-person RESTORE trial found that CFT, with or without movement biofeedback, improved activity limitation more than usual care at 13 weeks, with similar effects at one year. A separate 152-person sham-controlled trial found an advantage of 1.8/10 in pain and 9.9/100 in disability after treatment, sustained through six months. [S19, S20]

**Editorial value:** include it as a bounded pain-rehabilitation approach. It broadens the meaning of active body work beyond emotional expression or discharge. These studies do not establish it as a general treatment for developmental trauma.

### Emotion-Focused Therapy: explain the acronym carefully

Greenberg's Emotion-Focused Therapy belongs in the experiential comparison because its therapist actively guides emotional tasks and bodily awareness. Its theoretical emphasis is changing maladaptive emotional responses through new emotional experience. [S8]

The article already uses **EFT** for **Emotional Freedom Techniques**, or tapping. Introducing Emotion-Focused Therapy without spelling out that distinction would confuse two separate interventions.

### Two useful comparators, without automatically adding full sections

**Written Exposure Therapy (WET).** A 178-veteran randomized noninferiority trial found a brief writing-based trauma protocol noninferior to prolonged exposure for PTSD symptoms, with lower dropout (12.5% versus 35.6%). The writing deliberately engages traumatic thoughts and feelings. This is evidence about a structured clinical protocol, not generic journaling. [S21]

**DBT-PTSD.** In 193 women with childhood-abuse-related PTSD and substantial borderline features, a lengthy specialized DBT-PTSD protocol outperformed cognitive processing therapy by a modest between-group effect (d = 0.33). Both improved. This is a useful example when considering how validation, behavioral change, preparation, and trauma processing can be combined. [S22]

These comparators matter when evaluating broad claims about what “cognitive,” “talk,” or “memory” therapies actually do. Their names alone do not tell us whether the treatment creates an emotionally engaged experience.

### Relevant methods with weaker verified outcome evidence

**Hakomi** explicitly uses mindful attention and experiential bodily experiments to investigate organizing beliefs. It merits recognition in a coverage map; this pass did not verify controlled patient-outcome evidence sufficient for an efficacy ranking. Its developer account supports a description of its method. [S23]

**NARM — NeuroAffective Relational Model.** The retrieved effectiveness paper surveyed 76 trained providers about perceived effectiveness. It was not a randomized patient-outcome study. Provider endorsement percentages must not be reported as patient improvement rates. [S24]

Sensorimotor Psychotherapy and Focusing were deliberately left out of the existing working article. This research pass records that editorial boundary and does not treat those exclusions as authorization to reinstall them.

## Proposed article changes

These are editorial recommendations, not a validated treatment hierarchy or approved revisions.

| Proposed addition | Proposed location/function |
| --- | --- |
| A concise introduction to embodied emotional psychotherapy, with ISTDP, APT, AEDP, and Emotion-Focused Therapy distinguished by therapist behavior | Near the discussion of working with difficult feelings, with cross-references to regulation and integration. These approaches often span several functions in the article's map. |
| An EAET/PRT comparison | A bounded discussion of persistent pain and physical symptoms, explaining their different proposed targets and reporting matched trial outcomes. |
| Imagery rescripting | Near memory work and the already-present protective/nurturing inner-child material. |
| CFT | A short optional entry if bodily rehabilitation and chronic pain remain within article scope. |
| WET and DBT-PTSD | Comparative evidence for claims about trauma processing and preparation; not necessarily separate recommendations. |
| Hakomi and NARM | Coverage notes with explicit evidential limits; conceptual relevance does not justify presenting them as equally well established. |

I would prioritize the missing emotional-psychotherapy family, EAET/PRT, and imagery rescripting. The principle is to explain which therapeutic function a method performs, what the clinician actually does, and what outcomes have been tested. An author's useful experiential sequence can remain a heuristic without being described as a universal sequence proven by these trials.

## Search scope, provenance, and remaining limits

This was a targeted research expansion through 26 November 2026, not a registered systematic review of every psychotherapy. Discovery covered psychodynamic emotional processing, humanistic experiential work, pain reinterpretation, imagery/memory work, bodily rehabilitation, trauma exposure, and somatic-relational methods. Existing article modalities were not all re-audited.

The treatment-style comparison comes from manuals and developer descriptions; the efficacy comparison comes from primary trials and the EDT systematic review. Developer accounts are appropriate for identifying what a therapy intends to do, but do not independently establish its causal mechanism.

Direct-comparison searches included the exact combinations “EAET versus ISTDP,” “EAET versus AEDP,” and “EAET versus affect phobia,” plus each full therapy name with “randomized comparison.” Retrieved comparisons were mainly against CBT, education, waitlists, or routine care. Combined interventions do not constitute head-to-head comparisons.

A 2026 EAET migraine paper was located (Kaufmann et al., DOI 10.1093/pm/pnag034), but primary numerical results could not be retrieved after publisher, repository, and scholarly-retrieval attempts. No migraine effect claim is made here. This access gap prevents an exhaustive indication-specific EAET review; it does not supply evidence for a ranking against other EDTs.

Several publisher and PubMed pages blocked direct retrieval while indexed primary text remained available. Access levels are recorded below. References with unverified live destinations are given as identifiers rather than unverified clickable links. Follow-up and process papers from an existing cohort are not counted as independent trials. Much EAET/PRT research includes developers; multiple papers and institutions should not automatically be treated as independent investigator replication.

No treatment recommendation for an individual's TBI or pain follows from this article-focused review.
