# Introduction

The body was involved in surviving what happened to you, and it has its own innate resolution process. Peter Levine, the founder of Somatic Experiencing therapy, in his book, [*In An Unspoken Voice*](https://www.amazon.com/Unspoken-Voice-Releases-Restores-Goodness/dp/1556439431), explains that his accidental realization about the need to release bodily trauma was corroborated by a park biologist in Malawi. He told Levine that captured animals who hadn’t trembled and breathed spontaneously before being released were unlikely to survive in the wild.

<!-- PRESERVE LIVE INTRO IMAGE AT THIS POSITION -->

But discovering the body’s programming isn’t simply something you can read a book for. Some bodies are frozen and need to shake. Some are dissociated and need to feel, cry, laugh, self-massage, breathe more, less, in new ways, etc. Learning to feel the body, connect with it, and let the energy flow the way it needs to, is like learning to ride a bike. You can read about it and follow the practices, and even pay lots of money to a Bike Expert, but ultimately, you have to feel it out and see what works.

Still, I do find that lasting complex trauma recovery tends to follow a general, kind of obvious sequence:

1. Stay present so you can stay with what happens and choose what comes next.
2. Regulate or discharge accumulated activation energy.
3. Work with trauma feelings before the story comes in.
4. Work with memories you can actually point to: the actual events and the triggering events.
5. Integrate, differentiate, and build a life of meaning. Decide your boundaries, direction, relationships, and overall identity.

Because specific somatic therapies are on a spectrum of physical<->emotional (or conscious<->subconscious), as well as a spectrum of intensity and specificity, we can often guess which one might be right for someone at which time. These don’t all have the same level or type of evidence supporting them, and there’s definitely no scientific roadmap that could survive peer review, but ultimately everyone somehow builds their own map of what makes sense, and this is mine.

I use these therapies partly to make the body safer to inhabit before going into deeper [inner-child therapy](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com), where we intentionally re-expose ourselves to traumatic memories in an effort to re-parent the little one-one inside who was hurt.

# The Dominant Trauma-Memory Therapies Are Missing Something

Currently, as a PTSD or cPTSD patient, your health insurance (if you're lucky enough to have it) might cover the most scientifically validated trauma therapies: Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) and trauma-focused Cognitive Behavioral Therapies.

All of these focus on the trauma memory itself, reactivating it in order to change the meaning and/or the processing and response to it. Their logic is simple: slowly increase your exposure to painful memories until you can fully re-live the experience without overwhelming your nervous system. The CBT trauma therapies also use regular CBT to re-narrate the meaning of the experiences, so that, for example, being abused as a child no longer creates a story that "I deserve abuse."

Those therapies do show meaningful impact for many people in terms of reducing hyperarousal, and sometimes dissociation. But there are two main problems with them that Peter Levine, et al. have [explained](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00093/full). On the one hand, as Levine points out, exposure desensitization therapy can itself be re-traumatizing, which isn't well-captured in the positive evidence, because reasons for dropout and adverse events were often poorly or inconsistently reported. On the other hand, even when these trauma-memory approaches "work," they are working against the body's understanding of things by creating new cognitive-behavioral patterns that suppress the old conditioned fear responses as they come up. That can produce a reduction of symptoms without solving the root of them.

The philosophy behind somatic therapies starts from the other end. It assumes that the body actually knows how to release the tension it has stored. Instead of treating dissociation and hyperarousal as symptom endpoints to reduce, they use them to diagnose the structure of nervous system dysregulation. In Levine's SIBAM model ([*In An Unspoken Voice*](https://www.amazon.com/Unspoken-Voice-Releases-Restores-Goodness/dp/1556439431), ch. 7), we can understand how dissociation and hyperarousal actually work by splitting up our ordinary holistic experience into five layers. Dissociation is when these layers are not well-connected. Hyperarousal is when channels are so tightly linked that one rapidly activates the others:

- **Sensation:** feelings inside the body, like tension, balance, heat, etc.
- **Image:** what you see, remember, dream
- **Behavior:** voluntary, involuntary, and crucially, actions that got aborted mid-stream during the traumatic incident.
- **Affect:** emotions, like fear, anger, grief, disgust, and joy. Also bodily feelings like vulnerability, hyper-vigilant panic, or profound relief.
- **Meaning:** the story that explains all of these, containing beliefs and judgements.

With this therapeutic model, one can direct their attention toward individual layers of experience that are either flattened/ignored, or hogging the show.

Levine and many other somatic theorists believe that once the body can fully feel itself and unleash the energy that got stuck, the meaning layer that CBT addresses often handles itself. EMDR also works at the bodily level and allows the meaning level to handle itself, but it uses the body more as a barometer and metabolizer of distress encoded in memory networks rather than as the primary instrument of finishing the action that got blocked by trauma.

However, other somatic therapists and even Levine himself (in academic writing like the article linked), agree that re-evaluating the meanings and narratives around trauma can still be useful for many people, especially if the bottom-up process is honored first or concurrently.

# Stage 1: Stay Present and Keep Choice

## [Somatic Experiencing](http://traumahealing.org/)

Somatic Experiencing makes sense to me as an early option when the main problem is that activation runs away with you. Instead of forcing a detailed trauma narrative, it pays attention to sensation, orientation, impulse, defensive response, and pacing. The basic skill is noticing activation without immediately drowning in it.

This is where the usual language about expanding your “window of tolerance” becomes practical. Titration, pendulation, and orientation are all ways of asking: can you touch something difficult in a small enough dose, notice what happens, and come back? If you are stable and have one clear target, you may already be ready for focused trauma work. Capacity-building should not become an endless waiting room where the actual trauma is never treated.

If somebody is flooded, almost completely identified with the child-state, or cannot access a Nurturer or Protector at all, I would start much smaller: look around the room, get outside help, borrow an adult perspective, find a neutral witness, or take one ordinary Protector action. Gentle self-hypnosis can fit here too when it stays in the present instead of pulling you deep into memories or child dialogue.

## Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

Yoga can fit early too, but I mean a very particular kind of yoga. No power yoga. No hot yoga. Nobody announcing, “Open your hips and release your trauma.” The useful version is slow, choice-based, and non-invasive, with body awareness, flexibility, enjoyment, and agency as the point.

I like yoga here partly because the interoception has somewhere to go. How does this feel when I move, and can it help me relax better, sit straighter, meditate better, get in shape at the same time?

Good options include trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, and very gentle yin when it is propped, short, and easy to leave. The language matters too. “You might try…” is very different from “stay there.” “You can keep your eyes open.” “You can come out whenever you want.” “Notice whether this feels useful, neutral, or too much.” A posture can have several valid versions; choose one that actually fits your body.

I would avoid intense stretching, long passive holds, forced breathwork, breath retention, hands-on adjustments without clear consent, aggressive hip openers, or anybody telling you to push through sensation. If the point is to rebuild choice, the practice should contain actual choices.

## Gentle Shaking / [TRE](https://traumaprevention.com/frequently-asked-questions/)

Shaking can fit here too, but I mean a small dose. Basic Trauma Release Exercises, light neurogenic tremoring, standing and gently shaking, even a short playful movement that you can stop immediately. You should finish more settled, not blasted open.

<!-- PRESERVE TRE YOUTUBE EMBED AT THIS POSITION -->

If the shaking becomes compulsive, frightening, dissociative, or hard to stop, back off. And if tremoring reaches your neck while you are lying down, do not let your head whip around unsupported. Stop or reduce the movement, support your head—or have an experienced provider help you support it—and get guidance before continuing; [forceful, rapid back-and-forth neck movement can injure the neck](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/whiplash/symptoms-causes/syc-20378921).

# Stage 2: Regulate or Discharge Accumulated Activation Energy

A good therapy session can only do so much if activation keeps building again during the rest of the week. What can I do so the pressure does not keep accumulating faster than I can process it?

## EFT / Tapping

EFT fits well here because it is quick, private, repeatable, and concrete enough to use while real life is happening. Conflict. A shame spiral. Social fear. Abandonment panic. Freeze. Anger. An intrusive fragment of memory that suddenly has your whole body involved.

It can fit before a difficult conversation, after getting triggered, before or after a therapy session, or when the mind is looping and the body is obviously in the loop too. It can also bridge body-based and cognitive work. I would not make it the only treatment for severe trauma, and I would not use it to avoid deeper work that obviously still needs doing.

## [**Shaking Qigong**](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) / Shaking Medicine

> Shaking Qigong is basically shaking plus qigong. I’m linking to a $10-per-month Skool class my friend Louka took because he had already tried many different healing and somatic modalities, including basic TRE, without getting much help. This was the one that finally helped him.

Here I am using it for regular autonomic discharge and energetic decompression. The smaller Stage 1 version stays mild and exploratory; here it can become a regular discharge practice, as long as you can still stop, orient, and settle afterward. A regular practice might run 10–45 minutes. I think of the use cases as chronic freeze, non-verbal emotional processing, stress that accumulates between therapy sessions, and leftover activation after a deeper session.

You do not have to accept the qigong explanation to see ordinary reasons shaking might help: rhythmic movement, less muscular armoring, more awareness of bodily sensation, a possible shift out of fight-or-flight, release of chronic tension, and perhaps completion of defensive impulses that got interrupted.

Qigong has a different explanatory language. *Qi* means vital energy, and from that perspective the same practice may be described as removing stagnation, increasing qi circulation, dissolving stored emotional charge, and reconnecting breath, body, and movement. These are different explanations for the same practice; one does not prove the other.

What I would not do is turn shaking into a catharsis contest, treat it as the primary trauma-processing modality for everybody, or use it as a substitute for skilled help when somebody is highly dissociative or unstable.

## The Discharge → Settle Stack

One daily sequence I like is simple:

1. [**Shaking Qigong**](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) **/ TRE** to discharge excess activation.
2. **Restorative or gentle yin yoga** to let the body soften, settle, and rebalance energy flow.
3. **EFT tapping** if there is still emotional activation or mental looping.

The reason for the stack is practical. I do not want to shake a lot of material loose and then walk away raw. Yin can help here, but I keep the same caveat from Stage 1: prop it, keep the holds shorter when necessary, and leave the pose if it starts feeling coercive. A shorter hold your body can actually tolerate may be more useful than an ambitious five-minute shape.

In my experience, EFT, Somatic Experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, gentle shaking or qigong, non-forced longer exhales, and gentle Buteyko-style breathing can all help the body loosen a little. I especially notice this around the solar plexus or abdomen. When that area softens, heart-chakra love can become easier for me to access, which can make the inner-child work much easier.

You do not have to use that opening to go straight into pain. Bring to mind a pet, a baby, a friend, a partner, or anybody you naturally love. Feel the love that is already there and see whether a little of it can move from the heart toward the younger part around the solar plexus, then back up again in a loop.

# Stage 3: Work With Trauma Feelings Before the Story Comes In

Some trauma gives you a scene you can point to. Some of it gives you a body reaction, an impulse, an emotion, an image, a fragment, or just the sense that something enormous is happening before there is a coherent story attached to it.

## Brainspotting

Brainspotting is one option I would look at for this kind of material. The method uses gaze position, focused attention, bodily awareness, and therapist attunement to stay with emotionally charged material that may be difficult to narrate.

Brainspotting can also prepare for inner-child reparenting or run alongside it, depending on how it is used. A resource-oriented or carefully titrated session is different from using Brainspotting to enter highly charged developmental material.

<!-- PRESERVE BRAINSPOTTING YOUTUBE EMBED AT THIS POSITION -->

Brainspotting interests me especially for diffuse developmental trauma, pre-verbal or body-held material, trauma that has not responded well to purely cognitive approaches, and those emotional knots where the body seems to know more than the story. You do not necessarily need to explain every detail for the session to move.

**Further reading:** [D’Antoni et al., 2022 — Comparative study of EMDR, Brainspotting, and related methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8835026/)

## After Brainspotting

I would resist the urge to turn the rest of the day into another processing marathon. If spontaneous shaking comes, let it happen gently. If the body still wants discharge, use gentle [**Shaking Qigong**](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/). If you feel floaty or far away, walking meditation may be better. Restorative, gentle yin, or somatic yoga can help if what you want is rest, stretching, toning, or reconnection. EFT can help if emotional activation is still high.

EMDR may fit better after some basic inner-child reparenting for people with diffuse developmental or relational trauma. Having at least some access to a Nurturer and Protector can make it easier to hold what memory work opens. That is not a universal rule: a stable person working with one discrete traumatic event may be ready for EMDR without completing a long preliminary sequence.

# Stage 4: Work With Memories You Can Actually Point To

This is the part of the map I think of as targeted memory work or reconsolidation. The problem is less “my whole body seems to carry something I cannot quite name” and more “this event, this flashback, this trigger network is still alive.”

## EMDR

EMDR is often a very good fit here: specific traumatic events, accidents, assault memories, military trauma, distinct flashbacks, and residual triggers that remain after other somatic work.

<!-- PRESERVE EMDR YOUTUBE EMBED AT THIS POSITION -->

I still care about regulation and what happens after the session. EMDR can be highly effective, but if somebody has diffuse developmental trauma and an unstable nervous system, I do not automatically assume it should be the first thing they try. If you cannot identify any clear targets and most of the material is bodily, pre-verbal, or relational, Brainspotting may fit better. A stable person with one discrete event is a different case and may be ready for EMDR much earlier.

I would save deep hypnosis, immersive child dialogue, suggestive exploration, and other deep memory work for the point where you can stay present, stop voluntarily, and return to ordinary life afterward.

## Neurological De-Armoring Support

Sleep, substance use, medication effects, pain, nutrition, and general physiological stability can all change how much therapy you can tolerate. I have a separate [sleep guide here](https://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/).

This is also one place where neurological de-armoring support may matter. EMDR directly engages memory reprocessing, so anything that changes fear learning, inflammation, dissociation, glutamate/GABA balance, or autonomic tone may change what reconsolidation feels like. I go into that separately in my [de-armoring supplement guide](http://dearmor.u-dont-exist.com/).

## After EMDR

Afterward I would keep things simple: walk, eat, hydrate, sleep early if you are tired, do restorative yoga, journal only if it grounds you, maybe use a little EFT if activation is still there.

I would not casually stack Brainspotting, EMDR, intense shaking, and deep yin on the same day unless you already know your system handles that much and you have skilled support. More processing can simply be more processing. Sometimes the missing piece is integration.

# Stage 5: Integrate, Differentiate, and Build a Life of Meaning

## Narrative and Cognitive Integration

CBT can show up much earlier than this. Stage 5 is where I mean the bigger narrative stuff: revisiting old beliefs, building a coherent life story, separating past danger from present reality, reclaiming agency, and figuring out what I actually want my life to be now—my values, boundaries, relationships, identity, direction, and sometimes forgiveness if that is relevant. [Inner-child reparenting](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/) can deepen here too.

# How I Judge Whether Any of This Is Helping

Yoga moves through the map: early for awareness, flexibility, enjoyment, and agency; after shaking to help the body settle and rebalance; or after deeper processing for stretching and reconnection. You do not need every modality or a fixed schedule.

Assess the dose immediately, the next morning, and over the following two or three days. A calm ending does not prove the dose was right. Watch sleep, irritability, dissociation, pain, compulsive processing, relationships, and ordinary functioning before deciding whether to repeat or intensify it.

I would also watch what the therapy itself is doing to my life. If I am functioning better but spending more and more of my time thinking about healing, that matters too. Sometimes the useful move is to stop processing and go to yoga, work, see people, or do regular life for a while. And sometimes “stop analyzing” is exactly the excuse I use when there is something painful I actually need to face.

Keep the explanation as modest as the evidence. Research support, common clinical use, my experience, community reports, and qigong or energetic explanations may point in the same direction without proving one another.

Some advanced shaking, qigong, breathwork, and somatic communities make very large claims about releasing decades of trauma, awakening kundalini, clearing stored emotion, or rapidly healing the whole nervous system. Treat those claims as hypotheses and community signal, not guarantees.

The amount of published research behind a modality matters, but it is not a personal treatment oracle. A thin literature does not prove a method ineffective; a large literature does not prove it is the right first method for you. Start from first principles, look at what seems to help people with similar patterns, try one change at a time, and judge it by what happens in your actual life.

# Optional High-Intensity State-Shift and Release Practices

[Sky Hypnosis](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/try-this-10-second-sky-hypnosis-bodymind) and [Vagal Blitz](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/vagal-blitz-breathwork-gives-best) do not have to wait until the end of the map. Stable people may find them helpful even before doing other therapies. They are optional advanced state-shift and release practices, not ordinary regulation exercises or prerequisites for healing.

Physical safety matters. Sky Hypnosis can produce lightheadedness, and Vagal Blitz is a lying-down-only practice with its own cardiovascular and mental-health cautions. Use the complete guide for the practice, begin with the least intense version that produces a useful shift, and stop when balance, orientation, or recovery becomes unreliable. Do not chase fainting or loss of consciousness.

These practices can also produce enough bliss, relief, or altered-state intensity that somebody feels healed while the same trauma is still running relationships and behavior. Judge the practice by what happens afterward. Does it give you more capacity to meet pain, set boundaries, and participate in life? Or has it become a way to avoid grief, conflict, practical problems, or deeper relational healing?

Advanced release does not establish readiness for deep memory work. Reassess orientation, ability to stop, sleep, physical recovery, and whether you are more willing to approach the actual issue. The benefits described in the linked guides include my experience and community signal; their proposed physiological mechanisms need to be judged separately.
