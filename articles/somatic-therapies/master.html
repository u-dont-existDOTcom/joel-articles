Introduction

Trauma doesn’t only live in the story you tell about what happened. Your body learned things too. It learned to brace, collapse, freeze, disconnect, scan for danger, or flood, and it can keep doing those things long after the original danger is over.

For many people, especially people with complex or developmental trauma, lasting recovery follows a broad sequence: establish enough safety and nervous-system regulation, release some of the accumulated stress and survival responses, and then go deeper into trauma processing.

So before I go digging for the deepest memory, I want to know something much more basic: can I stay here while I’m doing it, can I stop, and can I come back to ordinary life afterward? If getting close to the trauma makes you disappear, stay highly activated, or lose the ability to function, going deeper is not automatically progress.

This is why I find it useful to put Somatic Experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, EFT, shaking practices, Brainspotting, EMDR, and cognitive integration on one loose map. They are not all doing the same thing, and they are not all even the same kind of thing. Some are therapies, some are adjuncts, and some are experiential practices that may be very useful for particular people without having anything like the same scientific support.

They can also overlap with deeper inner-child therapy. Sometimes the body needs to become a little easier to inhabit before you can approach the younger parts of yourself without simply getting swallowed by them.

This Is a Map, but You Are the Explorer

The five-job map I’m using here is meant to solve a practical problem: people can easily end up doing yoga, tapping, shaking, Brainspotting, breathwork, EMDR, maybe hypnosis too, without being clear about what they are trying to accomplish with any of it. The more useful question is: what job does my nervous system need help with right now?

The rough order below makes the most sense to me when somebody is repeatedly overwhelmed, severely dissociative, actively unsafe, unable to settle after activation, or simply does not have enough support to tolerate deeper trauma work. But this is my synthesis from first-principles reasoning and community signal. It is not a scientifically validated five-stage protocol, and there is no evidence that everyone has to finish Somatic Experiencing, yoga, EFT, and shaking before they are “allowed” to begin EMDR.

Even if somebody eventually ran a beautiful trial and found an average best sequence, you would still have to look at your own response. Can you stay present enough to do the practice? Can you stop? Can you get back to ordinary life afterward? Is it actually reducing symptoms and giving you more freedom, or are you becoming more activated and more obsessed with processing?

Capacity matters, but I do not want “capacity building” to become an endless waiting room where the trauma itself never gets treated. I care more about what happens to your sleep (see my sleep guide), relationships, functioning, freedom, and symptoms than how dramatic the session felt.

And none of these modalities permanently belongs to one box. Gentle shaking may be Job 1 for one person and Job 2 for another. Present-focused self-hypnosis is very different from immersive memory work. Resource-oriented Brainspotting is different from going straight into highly charged developmental material. You can also have several jobs at once: daily discharge, one discrete traumatic memory that keeps firing, and basic inner-child reparenting can all be real needs in the same week.

I’m also moving between different kinds of evidence here: published research, common clinical practice, my own experience, community reports, and traditional or energetic explanations. I’ll try to keep those categories visible instead of letting them blur together. A practice can be useful without every story about why it works being true.

The Map at a Glance

  1. Job 1: Stay Present and Keep Choice. Build enough safety, orientation, stopping ability, and return capacity for whatever comes next.

  2. Job 2: Regulate or Discharge Accumulated Activation. Keep pressure from building between deeper sessions.

  3. Job 3: Work With Diffuse, Bodily, or Hard-to-Narrate Material. Approach sensations, impulses, emotions, images, or fragments that do not yet make a clean story.

  4. Job 4: Work With a Discrete Memory or Trigger Network. Go after specific traumatic events, flashbacks, and stubborn residual triggers.

  5. Job 5: Integrate, Differentiate, and Build a Life. Update beliefs, identity, relationships, values, and direction once survival mode is taking up less of the room.

Somatic Work and Inner-Child Reparenting Can Overlap

Inner-child work does not have to wait until Job 5. Lighter reparenting can begin early and run alongside somatic work.

If somebody is flooded, almost completely identified with the child-state, or cannot access a Nurturer or Protector at all, I would start much smaller: look around the room, get outside help, borrow an adult perspective, find a neutral witness, or take one ordinary Protector action. Gentle self-hypnosis can fit here too when it stays in the present instead of pulling you deep into memories or child dialogue.

In my experience, EFT, Somatic Experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, gentle shaking or qigong, non-forced longer exhales, and gentle Buteyko-style breathing can all help the body loosen a little. I especially notice this around the solar plexus or abdomen. When that area softens, heart-chakra love can become easier for me to access, which can make the inner-child work much easier.

You do not have to use that opening to go straight into pain. Bring to mind a pet, a baby, a friend, a partner, or anybody you naturally love. Feel the love that is already there and see whether a little of it can move from the heart toward the younger part around the solar plexus, then back up again in a loop.

Brainspotting can also sit on either side of this boundary. A resource-oriented or carefully titrated session can prepare you for deeper work; going into highly charged developmental material is a different dose. With diffuse relational trauma, I sometimes think EMDR fits better after at least a little inner-child reparenting, because access to a Nurturer and Protector gives you something to hold the material with. A stable person working on one discrete event may not need a long preliminary sequence at all.

I would save deep hypnosis, immersive child dialogue, suggestive exploration, and other deep memory work for the point where you can stay present, stop voluntarily, and return to ordinary life afterward. The useful question is always what the practice is doing right now: orientation, regulation, discharge, resourcing, processing, or integration.

Job 1: Stay Present and Keep Choice

Somatic Experiencing

Somatic Experiencing makes sense to me as an early option when the main problem is that activation runs away with you. Instead of forcing a detailed trauma narrative, it pays attention to sensation, orientation, impulse, defensive response, and pacing. The basic skill is noticing activation without immediately drowning in it.

This is where the usual language about expanding your “window of tolerance” becomes practical. Titration, pendulation, and orientation are all ways of asking: can you touch something difficult in a small enough dose, notice what happens, and come back? Some people may spend three to six months mainly building those capacities, and severe developmental trauma can take longer. I would treat that as orientation, not a countdown. If you are stable and have one clear target, you may be ready for focused trauma work much sooner.

Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

Yoga can fit early too, but I mean a very particular kind of yoga. No power yoga. No hot yoga. Nobody announcing, “Open your hips and release your trauma.” The useful version is slow, choice-based, and non-invasive, with body awareness, flexibility, enjoyment, and agency as the point.

Good options include trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, and very gentle yin when it is propped, short, and easy to leave. The language matters too. “You might try…” is very different from “stay there.” “You can keep your eyes open.” “You can come out whenever you want.” “Notice whether this feels useful, neutral, or too much.” A posture can have several valid versions; choose one that actually fits your body.

I would avoid intense stretching, long passive holds, forced breathwork, breath retention, hands-on adjustments without clear consent, aggressive hip openers, or anybody telling you to push through sensation. If the point is to rebuild choice, the practice should contain actual choices.

Gentle Shaking / TRE

Shaking can also live in Job 1, but here I mean a small dose. Basic Trauma Release Exercises, light neurogenic tremoring, standing and gently shaking, even a short playful movement that you can stop immediately. You should finish more settled, not blasted open.

If the shaking becomes compulsive, frightening, dissociative, or hard to stop, back off. And if tremoring reaches your neck while you are lying down, do not let your head whip around unsupported. Stop or reduce the movement, support your head—or have an experienced provider help you support it—and get guidance before continuing; forceful, rapid back-and-forth neck movement can injure the neck.

Job 2: Keep the Pressure Low Between Deeper Sessions

A good therapy session can only do so much if activation keeps building again during the rest of the week. Job 2 is the ordinary-life part: what can I do between deeper sessions so the pressure does not keep accumulating faster than I can process it?

EFT / Tapping

EFT fits well here because it is quick, private, repeatable, and concrete enough to use while real life is happening. Conflict. A shame spiral. Social fear. Abandonment panic. Freeze. Anger. An intrusive fragment of memory that suddenly has your whole body involved.

It can fit before a difficult conversation, after getting triggered, before or after a therapy session, or when the mind is looping and the body is obviously in the loop too. It can also bridge body-based and cognitive work. I would not make it the only treatment for severe trauma, and I would not use it to avoid deeper work that obviously still needs doing.

Shaking Qigong / Shaking Medicine

Shaking Qigong is basically shaking plus qigong. I’m linking to a $10-per-month Skool class my friend Louka took because he had already tried many different healing and somatic modalities, including basic TRE, without getting much help. This was the one that finally helped him.

Here the job is daily autonomic discharge and energetic decompression. For Job 1, shaking stays mild and exploratory. Here it can become a regular discharge practice—as long as you can still stop, orient, and settle afterward. A regular practice might run 10–45 minutes. I think of the use cases as chronic freeze, non-verbal emotional processing, stress that accumulates between therapy sessions, and leftover activation after a deeper session.

You do not have to accept the qigong explanation to see ordinary reasons shaking might help: rhythmic movement, less muscular armoring, more awareness of bodily sensation, a possible shift out of fight-or-flight, release of chronic tension, and perhaps completion of defensive impulses that got interrupted.

Qigong has a different explanatory language. Qi means vital energy, and from that perspective the same practice may be described as removing stagnation, increasing qi circulation, dissolving stored emotional charge, and reconnecting breath, body, and movement. These are different explanations for the same practice; one does not prove the other.

What I would not do is turn shaking into a catharsis contest, treat it as the primary trauma-processing modality for everybody, or use it as a substitute for skilled help when somebody is highly dissociative or unstable.

The Discharge → Settle Stack

One daily sequence I like is simple:

  1. Shaking Qigong / TRE to discharge excess activation.

  2. Restorative or gentle yin yoga to let the body soften, settle, and rebalance energy flow.

  3. EFT tapping if there is still emotional activation or mental looping.

The reason for the stack is practical. I do not want to shake a lot of material loose and then walk away raw. Yin can help here, but I keep the same caveat from Job 1: prop it, keep the holds shorter when necessary, and leave the pose if it starts feeling coercive. A shorter hold your body can actually tolerate may be more useful than an ambitious five-minute shape.

Job 3: When the Trauma Is More Body Than Story

Some trauma gives you a scene you can point to. Some of it gives you a body reaction, an impulse, an emotion, an image, a fragment, or just the sense that something enormous is happening before there is a coherent story attached to it. This is often called “subcortical” processing. I use the word as shorthand for material that shows up through sensation, impulse, emotion, image, or fragments before it becomes a clear verbal story—not as a claim that one precise brain region contains it.

Brainspotting

Brainspotting is one option I would look at for this kind of material. The method uses gaze position, focused attention, bodily awareness, and therapist attunement to stay with emotionally charged material that may be difficult to narrate.

Before going deep with it, I would want enough nervous-system safety to remain present, at least one way to regulate ordinary triggers, some way to settle or discharge afterward, and enough comfort with bodily sensation to notice when the dose is getting too high. Those are capacities, not prerequisite therapies. How you built them is secondary.

Brainspotting interests me especially for diffuse developmental trauma, pre-verbal or body-held material, trauma that has not responded well to purely cognitive approaches, and those emotional knots where the body seems to know more than the story. You do not necessarily need to explain every detail for the session to move.

After Brainspotting

I would resist the urge to turn the rest of the day into another processing marathon. If spontaneous shaking comes, let it happen gently. If the body still wants discharge, use gentle Shaking Qigong. If you feel floaty or far away, walking meditation may be better. Restorative, gentle yin, or somatic yoga can help if what you want is rest, stretching, toning, or reconnection. EFT can help if emotional activation is still high.

Job 4: When There Is a Memory You Can Actually Point To

This is the part of the map I think of as targeted memory work or reconsolidation. The problem is less “my whole body seems to carry something I cannot quite name” and more “this event, this flashback, this trigger network is still alive.”

EMDR

EMDR is often a very good fit here: specific traumatic events, accidents, assault memories, military trauma, distinct flashbacks, and residual triggers that remain after other somatic work.

I still care about regulation and what happens after the session. EMDR can be highly effective, but if somebody has diffuse developmental trauma and an unstable nervous system, I do not automatically assume it should be the first thing they try. If you cannot identify any clear targets and most of the material is bodily, pre-verbal, or relational, Brainspotting may fit better. A stable person with one discrete event is a different case and may be ready for EMDR much earlier.

Neurological De-Armoring Support

Sleep, substance use, medication effects, pain, nutrition, and general physiological stability can all change how much therapy you can tolerate. I have a separate sleep guide here.

This is also one place where neurological de-armoring support may matter. EMDR directly engages memory reprocessing, so anything that changes fear learning, inflammation, dissociation, glutamate/GABA balance, or autonomic tone may change what reconsolidation feels like. I go into that separately in my de-armoring supplement guide.

After EMDR

Afterward I would keep things almost boring: walk, eat, hydrate, sleep early if you are tired, do restorative yoga, journal only if it grounds you, maybe use a little EFT if activation is still there.

I would not casually stack Brainspotting, EMDR, intense shaking, and deep yin on the same day unless you already know your system handles that much and you have skilled support. More processing can simply be more processing. Sometimes the missing piece is integration.

Job 5: Make Sense of What Changed

Light CBT / Narrative Integration

Eventually there is a point where cognitive work becomes much more useful. Trying to reframe a danger while the body still experiences the threat as present often does not get very far.

Once the body is less trapped there, light CBT and narrative work can help you revisit old beliefs, build a coherent life story, separate past danger from present reality, clarify values and boundaries, reclaim agency, and deepen inner-child reparenting. This is also where identity, differentiation, forgiveness when it is actually relevant, and life direction may become easier to work with.

Thinking becomes more useful once the body is no longer reacting as though the old threat is still happening.

How I Judge Whether Any of This Is Helping

If I had to compress the map into one line, it would be:

Regulation → discharge → processing → integration → meaning.

But even that line is only a map. Yoga can show up early for body awareness and agency, after shaking because you need to settle, or after deeper processing because stretching and reconnection feel good. You do not need every modality.

Check the dose more than once. How do you feel immediately afterward? What happened to your sleep that night? What about the next morning, and the next two or three days? A calm ending to a session does not prove the dose was right. I would watch irritability, dissociation, pain, compulsive processing, relationships, and ordinary functioning before deciding to repeat or intensify something.

Keep the explanation as modest as the evidence. Published research, common clinical use, my own experience, community reports, and qigong or energetic explanations can all point toward the same practice without proving one another. Some shaking, qigong, breathwork, and somatic communities make huge claims about releasing decades of trauma, awakening kundalini, clearing stored emotion, or rapidly healing the entire nervous system. Treat those claims as hypotheses and community signal, not guarantees. Judge the practice by what changes in actual life.

A thin research literature does not prove a method useless, and a large research literature does not tell you which therapy your own nervous system needs first. I would start from first principles, pay attention to people with patterns similar to mine, change one thing at a time when possible, and keep asking the unglamorous question: am I actually doing better? Go slowly enough to see what the practice actually did, and do not confuse intensity with healing.

Optional High-Intensity Practices: Sky Hypnosis and Vagal Blitz

Sky Hypnosis and Vagal Blitz do not have to wait until the end of the map. Stable people may find them useful before doing any of the other therapies. I treat them as optional high-intensity state-shift and release practices, not ordinary regulation exercises and definitely not prerequisites for healing.

They also deserve their own physical-safety rules. Sky Hypnosis can make you lightheaded. Vagal Blitz is a lying-down-only practice with cardiovascular and mental-health cautions. Use the complete guide, start with the least intense version that gives you a useful shift, and stop when balance, orientation, or recovery becomes unreliable. Do not chase fainting or loss of consciousness.

There is also a psychological trap here: a practice can produce so much bliss, relief, or altered-state intensity that you feel healed while the same trauma is still running your relationships and behavior. So I look at what happens afterward. Am I more able to meet pain, set boundaries, deal with conflict, and participate in life? Or did the practice become a way to avoid grief, conflict, practical problems, or deeper relational healing?

And a huge release does not automatically mean you are ready for deep memory work. I would still check orientation, ability to stop, sleep, physical recovery, and whether I am actually more willing to approach the real issue. The benefits I describe in the linked guides include my experience and community signal. The proposed physiological mechanisms are a separate question.
