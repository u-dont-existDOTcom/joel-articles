# The Dominant Top-Down Exposure Therapies Are Missing Something

Currently, as a PTSD or cPTSD patient, your health insurance (if you're lucky enough to have it) might cover the most scientifically validated trauma therapies: Eye-Movement Desensitization Reprocessing (EMDR) and trauma-focuseed Cognitive Behavioral Therapies. All of these are exposure therapies. Their logic is simple: slowly increase your exposure to painful memories until you can fully re-live the experience without overwhelming your nervous system. The CBT trauma therapies then go further after that into regular CBT, to re-narrate the meaning of the experiences, so that, for example, being abused as a child no longer creates a story that "I deserve abuse."

Those therapies do show meaningful impact for many people in terms of reducing dissociation and hyperarrousal. But there are two main problems with them that
Peter Levine, et al. have (explained)[[https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00093/full](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00093/full)]. On the one hand, exposure therapy can itself be re-traumatizing, which isn't captured in the positive evidence, because drop-outs from the studies aren't studied. On the other hand, even when these top-down approaches "work," they are working against the body's understanding of things by suppressing, ignoring, or arguing away feelings. That can produce a reduction of symptoms without solving the root of them.

The philosophy behind somatic therapies starts from the other end. It honors the wisdom of the body and it assumes that the body actually knows how to release the tension it has stored. Instead of treating dissociation and hyper-arrousal as symptom endpoints to reduce, they treat them as root causes of dysfunction to work with directly. In Levine's SIBAM model, we can understand how dissociation and hyper-arrousal actually work by splitting up our ordinary holistic experience into five layers. Dissocation is when these layers are not well-connected. Hyper-arrousal is when they're too fused:
Sensation: feelings inside the body, like tension, balance, heat, etc.
Image: what you see, remember, dream
Behavior: voluntary, involuntary, and crucially, actions that got aborted mid-stream during the traumatic incident.
Affect: emotions, like fear, anger, grief, disgust, and joy. Also bodily feelings like vulnerability, hyper-vigilant panic, or profound relief.
Meaning: the story that explains all of these, containing beliefs and judgements.

With this therapeutic model, one can direct their attention toward  individual layers of experience that are either flattened/ignored, or hogging the show.

Levine and many other somatic theorists believe that once the body can fully feel itself and unleash the energy that got stuck, the meaning layer that CBT addresses often handles itself. EMDR also works at the bodily level and allows the meaning level to handle itself, but it uses the body more as a barometer of distress rather than as the intelligent instrument of self-healing.

However, other somatic therapists and even Levine himself (in academic writing like the article linked), agree that re-evaluating the meanings and narratives around trauma can still be useful for many people, especially if the bottom-up process is honored first or concurrently.
