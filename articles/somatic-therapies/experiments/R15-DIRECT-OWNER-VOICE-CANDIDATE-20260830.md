# Introduction

Trauma can be finished in real life while your body is still doing it. You know you are safe and meanwhile you are bracing, scanning, going numb, collapsing, freezing, or suddenly flooded. That leftover bodily reality is what I mean by somatic here.

With complex or developmental trauma I am not eager to begin with the deepest memory. Can I stay here when something comes up? Can I stop? Am I more or less myself afterward? If the honest answer is no, overwhelming myself does not become deep processing because I gave it that name.

Sometimes I need regulation. Sometimes the old survival activation needs to move and I am not going to reason my way out of a body that still thinks the danger is here. Sometimes I really am stable enough to go straight to EMDR or another focused trauma method. I don't see those as rungs on a ladder. They are different answers to different problems.

This is especially important to me with [inner-child therapy](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/). I can understand perfectly well what a younger part needs and still be unable to reach it because everything in me is clenched around the trauma. Sometimes my body has to become a slightly easier place to live before inner-child work stops feeling like I am being swallowed by the child-state.

## Your Physical State Can Change What Therapy Does

I don't put “therapy” on one side and the physical state of the nervous system doing the therapy on the other. Sleep. Pain. Substances or medication. Nutrition, fear learning, inflammation, dissociation, glutamate/GABA balance, autonomic tone, and how physiologically stable I am that day. The same session can land very differently depending on all of that.

This is important to me because I have seen friends get almost nothing from these therapies until what I understood as physical neuroinflammation was addressed, using things like low-dose naltrexone, phosphatidylserine, DMSO, and so on. That is an observation about my friends, not me establishing the mechanism of their treatment.

My own version is less tidy. I can't do any real healing work when I feel like I have toxins in my gut. Ozonated zeolite helps me a lot with that. Ozonated charcoal water also helps a lot when I feel surrounded by chaotic energy fields, whether I interpret them as EMF or auric.

I call this neurological de-armoring. It isn't another trauma exercise; it is the ground I am asking the exercise to work on. I wrote separate guides on [sleep](https://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/) and [neurological de-armoring](http://dearmor.u-dont-exist.com/) in case the ground is the problem.

# Where I Would Start

I don't have a preferred ladder. If one event is obviously the problem and I can touch it without disappearing, I may go straight to EMDR. If touching trauma makes me flood or vanish or spend the rest of the day wrecked, then no, I am not going to make myself do the hottest thing first.

There is a trap on the gentle side too. “Building capacity” can become an avoidance project where I regulate forever because nobody wants to treat the thing that hurts. Away from the therapy, am I sleeping? Functioning? Relating better? Getting freer? Sometimes sleep is the whole bottleneck, which is why I have a separate [sleep guide](http://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/).

The name of a method tells me less than its intensity and how I am using it. Present-focused self-hypnosis is not immersive memory work. A little shaking is not forced catharsis. Brainspotting can help me orient and resource, or it can take me into material that is much hotter. Same label, very different experience.

# Somatic Work and Inner-Child Reparenting

The gentler body work matters to me because it can make reparenting possible. Regulation itself is not the grand finale. Also, relaxation does not magically solve every problem I have with the child.

For example, sometimes it isn't that I can't access love. The love seems unsafe. The inner child is sitting there looking at the grown-up self and basically saying, “Yeah, that doesn't look like things went well for us.” Then the grown-up can become resentful at the younger version who didn't do what they were supposedly meant to do. Now there is an internal war: the older version is making vows of protection and nurturing, and the child is thinking, big fuckity whoopty doo, what are you going to do for me?

That child has a point. The Protector creates real-world safety. It says no, leaves the situation, finishes the avoided task, gets sleep, cleans the room, keeps a small promise. One real action can say more than another internal speech about safety.

When I am completely flooded I am basically the child-state, though, and those adult functions may not be available yet. In my inner-child work I call them the Nurturer and Protector. A neutral witness still counts. Notice the room, the body, and the younger state; the fact that something can notice is enough adult presence for a first step. I can also borrow one sentence, boundary, or direction from somebody safe and carry one small part myself. That is what I mean by borrowed adulthood.

Gentle [self-hypnosis](http://hypnosis.u-dont-exist.com/) can help with regulation, dialogue, reparenting, clarity, and present safety. I don't mean using it to force memories, prove what happened, or pull myself deep into child dialogue when I can't stay here.

Body tension can be the more immediate blockage. I notice this around the abdomen or solar plexus. EFT, Somatic Experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, gentle shaking or qigong, a longer exhale that stays passive and unforced, and gentle Buteyko-style breathing have all helped me soften there. A little mantra or meditation can help too. I am distinguishing that from an intensive retreat or deep hypnosis. The body work can come before inner-child work or keep going beside it.

And the heart-to-solar-plexus practice is not an afterthought for me. One hand goes on the center of the chest, the heart chakra, to connect with love. The other goes on the solar plexus just under the chest, where I feel the vulnerable inner child. The solar plexus really is vulnerable—one slap there hurts quite a lot—so it makes sense to me that the vulnerable child would be felt there.

If love is hard to summon on command, I think of somebody real: a pet, a baby, a friend, a partner, anyone I genuinely love. Then I send some of that love from the heart to the inner child, receive love back from the child, and send it back to the heart. An infinite beautiful loop!

Brainspotting can be part of the reparenting as well. Using it for a resource or going in carefully is nothing like using it to enter highly charged developmental material. With diffuse developmental or relational trauma I lean toward waiting on EMDR until there is at least some Nurturer and Protector available to hold what gets opened. I would not turn that into a universal rule. Someone stable with one discrete event may be ready much sooner.

Deep memory work, immersive child dialogue, deep hypnosis, suggestive exploration: I save those for when staying present, stopping voluntarily, and coming back are things I can actually do.

# Building Enough Safety to Stay Present

## Somatic Experiencing

[Somatic Experiencing](http://traumahealing.org/) is something I reach for when merely approaching the material sends my body over the edge. There is sensation, orientation, impulse, defensive response, and pacing to work with. I do not need the whole story first.

Titration and pendulation make sense because there is no prize for beating trauma in a fight. I touch as much activation as I can honestly tolerate, notice what happens, and come back toward something steadier. Some people spend three to six months building that capacity. Severe developmental trauma can take longer. I use that as orientation, not a countdown, and I see no reason for somebody already stable with a clear target to wait out arbitrary months before adding focused trauma work.

## Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

By yoga I do not mean power yoga, hot yoga, or somebody announcing, “Open your hips and release your trauma.” I mean slow and choice-based: trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, maybe very gentle yin. For yin I want props, shorter holds, and an easy exit.

The instructions should sound more like:

- “You might try…”
- “You can keep your eyes open.”
- “Come out of the shape whenever you want.”
- “Is this useful, neutral, or too much?”
- “There can be several right versions. Pick the one that fits your body.”

Intense stretching, breath retention, forced breathwork, aggressive hip opening, long passive holds, hands-on adjustments without clear consent, pushing through because suffering is supposedly therapeutic—I am not recommending any of that. Once I can't choose, it isn't the practice I mean anymore.

## Gentle Shaking / TRE

Here I mean a little shaking. Not an attempt to blast trauma out of my body.

Basic [TRE](https://traumaprevention.com/frequently-asked-questions/), very light neurogenic tremoring, gentle shaking while standing, even a short bit of playful movement may be plenty. Once it feels compulsive, frightening, dissociative, or difficult to stop, it is no longer what I mean by gentle.

# What I Use Between Deeper Sessions

A wonderful therapy session is not worth much if I spend the following week rebuilding all the pressure. I need at least one thing that still works while ordinary life is happening.

## EFT / Tapping

EFT is very nice for regulation. It is a distraction, and my hands and attention have something to do other than ride the reaction all the way down.

I think of the different tapping points as activating different parts of the brain. That is my thought; I am not presenting it here as a neuroscience result I proved. I move through the points and see how I feel at each one. It is also a little massage. Actually, shaving my head and massaging it works really well for me—maybe even better than EFT.

The big advantage is that EFT travels. Before a difficult conversation. Immediately after somebody triggers me. In the middle of a thought loop that has recruited my whole body. It can take the pressure down. I don't confuse that with finishing the deeper trauma work.

## [Shaking Qigong](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) / Shaking Medicine

My friend Louka is why I link this class. He had tried many healing and somatic things, including basic TRE. TRE did nothing for him. Nothing helped much until he got into this class, which costs $10 a month on Skool.

The class is much more than just TRE. It has all kinds of movements and positions, and I think that matters for some people because they may need the right position or movement together with the tremor before they find where the blockage is.

By blockage I mean what energy is stuck in you. I often have the natural kind of shaking animals have rather than noticing much benefit from intentional therapeutic techniques. Especially on psychedelics, it is as if my body knows exactly which part to shake and how. It isn't simply “shaking = mental health.” When the right thing is shaken the right way, things loosen up as they should and the energy flows.

TRE is a system they are trying to teach en masse. Of course it had to be distilled into something that seems to work most of the time for most people. I suspect some people would have a better chance if they could approach the stuck place from more angles. “Just try lots of stuff till it works” is how my expertise runs on this—and also how you get less chance of being called an expert, lol.

I didn't try the Shaking Qigong class. My firsthand part is qigong, and qigong has been very helpful for me in moving energy. So I can understand why the two would naturally go together. Guided shaking plus qigong also seems like a middle ground between a fully predictable TRE structure and completely unstructured shaking.

That could suit somebody who wants guidance but gets nothing from standard TRE. It is social too. Many people do better when they see other people getting results instead of doing the whole practice alone.

This is a different dose from the tiny exploratory shaking above. I could imagine 10–45 minutes as a regular practice for chronic freeze, non-verbal emotional processing, stress that accumulates between therapy sessions, or activation left over after deeper work. I did not learn that range from the linked class. Whatever dose I use, I still need to be able to stop and settle afterward.

What deserves the credit? I don't know. Maybe movement lets muscles and defensive impulses do something they never finished. Maybe it changes arousal because I am moving and paying close attention to the body. Qigong would say stuck qi moved. Those are different explanations. I don't need to force one of them to prove the others.

I stop trusting the practice when it becomes a catharsis competition, or a supposed complete treatment for severe PTSD. If somebody is highly dissociative or unstable, experimentation is not a replacement for skilled help.

## A Discharge → Settle Sequence

Sometimes I want the shaking to end in settling instead of keeping the system revved up. One combination I like is:

1. [**Shaking Qigong**](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) **/ TRE** — let some of the activation move.
2. **Restorative or gentle yin yoga** — soften, settle, rebalance energy flow.
3. **EFT tapping** — bring emotional calm and cognitive orientation back if activation is still high.

Same yin rules as before: props, choice, no endurance contest. Long passive holds can get surprisingly evocative, so especially early on I prefer shorter holds to ambitious five-minute shapes. Yoga can make sense before shaking too, or after deeper processing. This sequence is something I use, not a law.

# When the Body Knows More Than the Story

Sometimes there is no clean memory. There is a body reaction, image, impulse, emotion, or fragment with no coherent explanation attached. People call this *subcortical* processing. I only mean that the material arrived before the story. I am not claiming that one precise brain region contains the trauma.

## Brainspotting

I used my finger to direct my gaze slowly around my field of vision. I wanted to see how different memories felt, or what memories came up, at different angles. It was intuitive. I figured out pretty quickly that I could do a simple version myself.

In my own system, looking up-left often feels more like remembering and up-right more like constructing or thinking. People have made that into a general NLP rule, and the rule did not hold up in controlled research, so I don't look at somebody else's eyes and pretend I know what they are doing. I use the pattern because it is consistent for me. There is a broader real relationship between gaze, eye movement, and memory retrieval; that is not the same claim as the fixed map.

What I like is the edging. I go toward the uncomfortable place, feather-touch the boundary of it, and come back out instead of jumping into the deep end. Maybe edging is not the right word. In somatic language it is close to titration and pendulation. It feels better and more effective to me than hammering the distress.

I also give Brainspotting a lot of points for being democratic like EFT. I do not need an expensive expert in order to try a simple version, and it is available at any moment. A formal session can still add therapist attunement, focused attention, and body awareness around the gaze position.

# When I Can Point to the Memory

## EMDR

EMDR is more like: there it is; that is the memory. The recall procedure is clearer and the target is clearer. Brainspotting is easier to feel my way into when I don't know exactly what I am looking for. That is the main distinction in my mind.

An accident, assault memory, military trauma, distinct flashback, or specific trigger network is what I naturally think of for EMDR. It can be highly effective. If somebody is stable and there is one clear event, I am not going to make them complete months of preliminary body work because a tidy sequence says so.

I am more careful when the trauma is diffuse, developmental, mostly bodily or pre-verbal, relational, or the nervous system is still unstable. Then I want at least some access to a Nurturer and Protector before opening highly charged material. Brainspotting may fit earlier.

After EMDR I want the rest of the day to be almost boring. Walk. Eat. Hydrate. Sleep early if I am tired. Do restorative yoga if that feels good. Journal only if it grounds me. Maybe use a little EFT if there is still activation. I would not casually stack Brainspotting, EMDR, intense shaking, and deep yin on the same day unless I already know my system handles that much and I have skilled support.

Sometimes the useful thing after processing is simply to stop processing.

# Making Sense of What Changed

## Light CBT / Narrative Integration

When my body is no longer insisting “this is happening now,” talking to the belief becomes more useful. Before that I can win the argument intellectually and still lose it physiologically.

Then I can revisit old beliefs, make a more coherent life story, separate past danger from present reality, clarify values and boundaries, and get some agency back. This can feed straight into [inner-child reparenting](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/). The adult is more available. I can tell the adult and child-states apart more easily. Forgiveness—when forgiveness is actually relevant—and life direction stop being quite so abstract.

# How I Know Whether It Actually Helped

My outcome measure is boring. Does it still hit as hard later? By *restimulation* I mean what happens when I remember the event again: does the same charge come back into my body and mind?

While working on this article I was still affected by something that happened with my housemate the day before. I was washing all her moldy clothes for her, one of the dresses got discolored, and she yelled at me. I was still stewing over how she treated me.

So I did a quick bit of self-Brainspotting. I moved my finger and eyes slowly around while thinking about the event. Farther to either side, as I moved into more peripheral vision, I noticed blissful tingles. There is a popular simple claim that peripheral vision activates the parasympathetic nervous system. I do not take my experience as proof of that; the research is more complicated.

Subjectively I could move from the settled side with the blissful tingles to the distressed side, moment by moment, and kind of move the bliss into the distress while I thought about the event. Afterward it felt as if the event held less negative energy, or less blocking energy, in my mind.

The important version of me is the one an hour later, not the one enjoying the final minute of the exercise. If I am still stewing just as hard, perhaps the effect was temporary and I should try again. If I can remember what happened and feel less restimulated, that is more durable. I don't expect one treatment to erase everything; one EMDR session is often not enough either.

A bigger intervention leaves a wake, and sometimes I only notice it the next day. What happened to sleep? Am I more irritable, more dissociated, in more pain? Do I feel compelled to keep processing? How am I treating people? A calm final ten minutes does not prove the dose was right.

Research matters to me. I just don't use the size of a literature as a ranking algorithm for my nervous system. A shaking, qigong, breathwork, or somatic community may say that a practice releases decades of trauma, awakens kundalini, clears stored emotion, or rapidly heals the whole nervous system. My result cannot prove all those explanations. I look at people whose patterns resemble mine, change one thing at a time when I can, and keep returning to the unglamorous question: am I actually doing better?

[Sky Hypnosis](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/try-this-10-second-sky-hypnosis-bodymind) and [Vagal Blitz](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/vagal-blitz-breathwork-gives-best) are different animals. They shift state hard. I have put them outside the main route because they are not prerequisites for healing, although a stable person may still find them useful much earlier.

Sky Hypnosis can make me lightheaded. Vagal Blitz is for lying down only and comes with cardiovascular and mental-health cautions. I use the complete guide, begin with the least intense version that produces a useful change, and stop if balance, orientation, or recovery starts becoming unreliable. Fainting and loss of consciousness are not goals.

I don't count “wow” as a result either. A huge release, bliss, an altered state—it can all feel profound while the actual problem sits there untouched. Later, can I handle pain, boundaries, conflict, and ordinary life any better? If the extraordinary practice is mainly helping me dodge grief, conflict, practical problems, or deeper relational healing, its intensity does not rescue it.
