Introduction

Trauma can be over and the body can still be doing it. You know you are safe, but you are bracing, scanning, going numb, collapsing, freezing, or suddenly flooding anyway. That is the part of trauma I am interested in here.

With complex or developmental trauma I usually do not begin by asking for the deepest memory. I want to know whether I can stay with what comes up without disappearing, whether I can stop, and whether I can be basically myself again afterward. If not, I would rather work on that than call overwhelm “deep processing.”

Sometimes that means regulation. Sometimes it means giving accumulated survival activation some way to move instead of trying to think your way out of a body that is still acting as if the danger is present. And sometimes there is enough stability to go straight to EMDR or another trauma-focused method. These are not steps in a program; they solve different problems at different times.

The inner-child connection is one reason I care about the body so much. Sometimes you can understand exactly what a younger part needs and still be unable to reach it because your whole system is clenched around the trauma. The body may need to become a little easier to inhabit before inner-child therapy stops feeling like being swallowed by the child-state.

Your Physical State Can Change What Therapy Does

I also do not draw a hard line between “therapy” and the physical state of the nervous system doing the therapy. Sleep, substances or medication, pain, nutrition, fear learning, inflammation, dissociation, glutamate/GABA balance, autonomic tone, and just general physiological stability can all change what the same session does to you. What follows is narrower: what I have seen in friends, and my own experience, including how I interpret it energetically.

I have seen friends get very little from these therapies until they addressed physical neuroinflammation with things like low-dose naltrexone, phosphatidylserine, DMSO, and so on. In my own experience, I cannot do real healing work when I feel like I have toxins in my gut. Ozonated zeolite helps that a lot. Ozonated charcoal water also helps me around what I think of as chaotic energy fields, whether EMF or auric.

I think of this broader physical ground as neurological de-armoring: not another trauma technique, but part of what the techniques are working on. I have separate guides on sleep and neurological de-armoring if that seems relevant to you.

Where I Would Start

I do not have a preferred ladder. If one event is clearly the problem and you can stay present while touching it, I may go straight to EMDR. If touching trauma makes you disappear, flood, or stay wrecked for the rest of the day, I would rather use something gentler first.

Regulation can become avoidance too. I do not want someone spending forever “building capacity” because nobody is willing to treat the thing that is actually hurting them. I care about what happens in ordinary life: are you sleeping better, functioning better, relating better, getting freer? If sleep itself is the bottleneck, I have a separate sleep guide.

The intensity matters as much as the name of the method. Present-focused self-hypnosis is not the same thing as immersive memory work. A little shaking is not forced catharsis. Brainspotting can be used to resource and orient, or it can be used to enter material that is much hotter.

Somatic Work and Inner-Child Reparenting

My main reason for caring about the gentler body work is not that regulation is an end in itself. It can make reparenting possible.

If I am so flooded that I am basically the child-state, there may not be much adult perspective left online to comfort or protect that child. In my inner-child work I call those functions the Nurturer and Protector. Sometimes the first thing available is only a neutral witness, or what I call borrowed adulthood: taking the stance of a steady adult when you cannot yet find that steadiness inside yourself. Even one ordinary protective action can matter. Gentle self-hypnosis can help too when it stays present-focused instead of pulling you deeply into memories or child dialogue.

These practices do different things, but in my experience EFT, Somatic Experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, gentle shaking or qigong, non-forced longer exhales, and gentle Buteyko-style breathing can help the body relax and soften tension around the solar plexus or abdomen so heart chakra love becomes easier to access. They can prepare for inner-child work or continue alongside it.

Brainspotting can sit inside the reparenting work too. A resource-oriented or carefully titrated session is very different from using it to enter highly charged developmental material. With diffuse developmental or relational trauma, I am more inclined to use EMDR after there is at least some access to a Nurturer and Protector who can help hold what the memory work opens. That is not a universal rule; a stable person with one discrete event may be ready much earlier.

And once the body softens even a little, I do not have to use the opening to go looking for pain. I can bring to mind a pet, a baby, a friend, a partner, or anyone I naturally love. Feel the real love that is already there, and see whether a little of it can move from the heart toward the younger part around the solar plexus and then back up in a loop.

I would save deep memory work, immersive child dialogue, deep hypnosis, and suggestive exploration for when you can stay present, stop voluntarily, and come back afterward.

Building Enough Safety to Stay Present

Somatic Experiencing

Somatic Experiencing is one of the things I would reach for when even getting near the material sends the body over the edge. It works with sensation, orientation, impulse, defensive response, and pacing without requiring the whole story first.

Titration and pendulation are useful because you are not trying to win a contest with the trauma. You touch a tolerable amount of activation, notice what happens, then come back toward something steadier. Some people spend three to six months building that capacity, and severe developmental trauma can take longer. I would treat that as orientation, not a countdown. If you are already stable and have a clear target, there may be no reason to wait that long before adding trauma-focused work.

Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

When I say yoga here, I do not mean power yoga, hot yoga, or somebody announcing, “Open your hips and release your trauma.” I mean slow, choice-based work: trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, or very gentle yin. With yin I would keep it propped, short, and easy to leave.

Good trauma-sensitive instructions sound like:

“You might try…”
“You can keep your eyes open.”
“You can come out of the shape at any time.”
“Notice whether this feels useful, neutral, or too much.”
“A posture can have several correct versions; choose one that fits your body.”

What I do not want is intense stretching, forced breathwork, breath retention, aggressive hip openers, long passive holds, hands-on adjustments without clear consent, or the idea that you should push through sensation because suffering through it is somehow therapeutic. The whole point here is that choice stays available.

Gentle Shaking / TRE

At this point I am talking about a little shaking, not trying to blast trauma out of the body. Basic TRE, very light neurogenic tremoring, gentle standing shaking, or short playful movement can be enough. If it becomes compulsive, frightening, dissociative, or hard to stop, it has gone past what I mean by gentle.

One practical warning matters here. If tremoring reaches your neck while you are lying down, do not let your head whip around unsupported. Stop or reduce the movement, support your head—or have an experienced provider help you support it—and get guidance before continuing. Forceful, rapid back-and-forth neck movement can injure the neck.

What I Use Between Deeper Sessions

One good therapy session does not help much if I spend the rest of the week rebuilding the same pressure. I want at least one thing I can actually use while life is happening.

EFT / Tapping

EFT is very nice for that. Tapping gives my hands and attention something to do besides ride the whole reaction.

I think of tapping different points as activating different parts of the brain, so it is interesting to notice how I feel as I move through them. And it is a little bit of a massage. Actually, I mentioned earlier that shaving my head and massaging it works really well for me. Sometimes I feel like that works even better than EFT.

Where EFT earns its keep is portability. I can use it before a hard conversation, right after somebody triggers me, or when my mind is looping and my body has joined the loop. I think of that as taking the pressure down. I do not mistake it for having finished the deeper trauma work.

Shaking Qigong / Shaking Medicine

My friend Louka is why I am linking this $10-per-month Skool class. He had tried many healing and somatic modalities, including basic TRE; TRE did nothing for him, and nothing helped much until he got into the class. It has many movements and positions, which is what I mean by more than TRE. My hypothesis is that some people may need the right position or movement, combined with tremoring, to find where their blockage is.

By “blockage” I mean stuck energy. I often have the natural shaking animals have, rather than getting much from intentional therapeutic techniques. Especially on psychedelics, it is as if my body knows exactly which part to shake and how. To me, it is not simply “shaking = mental health”: when the right thing is shaken the right way, things loosen and the energy flows.

TRE has to be teachable en masse, so it distills what works most often for most people. More angles may mean more chances of success—and fewer chances of being called an expert. “Just try lots of stuff till it works” is how my expertise runs on this. I did not try the linked class; my firsthand part is qigong, which has helped me move energy. What interests me is its guided but not completely standardized middle ground between one fixed TRE protocol and unstructured shaking, plus the social setting. That may help someone who needs guidance, gets nowhere with standard TRE, or benefits from seeing others get results.

I could imagine 10–45 minutes of regular shaking or qigong for chronic freeze, non-verbal emotional processing, stress accumulating between therapy sessions, or activation left after deeper work. I did not learn or test that range in the linked class. It is a different dose from the exploratory shakes above, with a simple limit: I still need to be able to stop and settle afterward.

I do not know what deserves credit when shaking helps. Movement may let muscles and defensive impulses do something they never got to finish, or alter arousal through movement and close attention to the body. Qigong would say stuck qi is moving again. I leave those as different possibilities rather than forcing one to prove the other.

The place I would stop is when shaking turns into a catharsis competition, or when somebody starts treating it as a complete treatment for severe PTSD. If a person is highly dissociative or unstable, this is not where I would substitute experimentation for skilled help.

A Discharge → Settle Sequence

When I want the shaking to end in settling rather than just keep revving the system up, one combination I like is:

Shaking Qigong / TRE — let some of the activation move.
Restorative or gentle yin yoga — soften, settle, and rebalance energy flow.
EFT tapping — re-establish emotional calm and cognitive orientation if activation is still high.

With yin I would keep the same rules as above: propped, non-coercive, and not an endurance contest. Long passive holds can get surprisingly evocative, so especially early on I would rather use shorter holds than ambitious five-minute shapes. Yoga can also make sense before shaking or after deeper processing; I am not treating this sequence as a law.

When the Body Knows More Than the Story

Sometimes there is no clean memory to work on. There is just a body reaction, an image, an impulse, an emotion, or a fragment that does not come with a coherent explanation. People sometimes call this subcortical processing. I only use the word to mean that the material is arriving before the story does, not that one precise brain region “contains” the trauma.

Brainspotting

I used my finger to direct my gaze slowly around my field of vision and noticed that different memories felt different, or different memories came up, at different angles. That was basically how I figured out very quickly that I could do a simple version myself.

I notice one consistent quirk in myself: looking up-left often feels more like remembering, while up-right feels more like constructing or thinking something through. NLP famously claimed something similar as a general rule. That general rule has not held up in controlled research, so I do not read other people’s eyes that way. I just use what my own system seems to do reliably. Gaze and eye movement do interact with memory retrieval more broadly.

What I really like about Brainspotting is the edging. I can go toward the uncomfortable place, feather-touch the boundary, then come back out instead of diving straight into the worst of it. In somatic language that is close to titration and pendulation. It feels more effective to me than just hammering the distress.

I also give it points for being democratic like EFT. You can experiment with a simple version yourself. A formal Brainspotting session adds therapist attunement, focused attention, and bodily awareness around the gaze position.

The fact that you can experiment yourself does not mean I would dive into the deepest trauma alone. If you cannot tell when you are getting overwhelmed, stay present, or stop when you need to, that is the limit.

Brainspotting interests me especially for diffuse developmental trauma, pre-verbal or body-held material, trauma that has not responded well to purely cognitive approaches, and those knots where the body seems to know more than the story. I do not necessarily need to know exactly what I am targeting before the session starts moving. That is also where it connects naturally with inner-child reparenting: something younger can show up through the body before I have a neat memory to narrate, and the Nurturer or Protector can join the work when it does. For further reading, see D’Antoni et al., 2022, a comparative study involving EMDR, Brainspotting, and related methods.

Afterward I want to give it room to land. If my body still wants gentle discharge, Shaking Qigong can fit. If I feel floaty or far away, I would rather walk and get oriented to ordinary life.

When I Can Point to the Memory

EMDR

EMDR feels more like: there it is, that is the memory. The recall procedure is clearer and the target is clearer. Brainspotting is easier to feel your way into without knowing exactly what you are looking for. That is the main difference in my mind.

An accident, assault memory, military trauma, distinct flashback, or specific trigger network is the kind of thing I naturally think of for EMDR. It can be highly effective. If somebody is stable and there is one clear event, I would not make them complete months of preliminary body work just because a sequence looks tidy on paper.

I am more cautious when the trauma is diffuse, developmental, mainly bodily or pre-verbal, relational, or the nervous system is still unstable. There I want at least some access to a Nurturer and Protector before opening very charged material, and Brainspotting may fit better earlier.

After EMDR I want the rest of the day to be almost boring. Walk. Eat. Hydrate. Sleep early if you are tired. Do restorative yoga if that feels good. Journal only if it grounds you. Maybe use a little EFT if there is still activation. I would not casually stack Brainspotting, EMDR, intense shaking, and deep yin on the same day unless I already knew my system handled that much and I had skilled support.

Sometimes the useful thing after processing is simply to stop processing.

Making Sense of What Changed

Light CBT / Narrative Integration

Once my body is no longer insisting “this is happening now,” talking to the belief starts to make more sense. Before that I can win the argument intellectually and still lose it physiologically.

Then I can revisit old beliefs, build a more coherent life story, separate past danger from present reality, clarify values and boundaries, and reclaim agency. That can feed straight back into inner-child reparenting: the adult is more available, the child-state is easier to differentiate from, and questions like forgiveness—when forgiveness is actually relevant—and life direction become less abstract.

How I Know Whether It Actually Helped

When there is a discrete event, my outcome measure is boring: does the thing still hit as hard later? By restimulation I mean what happens when I remember the event again. Does the same charge come back into the body and mind, or not?

While I was working on this article I was still upset about an argument with my housemate. I had been washing her moldy clothes for her, one of the dresses got discolored, and she yelled at me about it. I was still stewing over how she treated me.

So I did a quick bit of self-Brainspotting. I moved my finger and eyes slowly around while thinking about the event. When my gaze went farther to either side I noticed blissful tingles as I moved into more peripheral vision. I do not take that as proof of the simple claim that “peripheral vision activates the parasympathetic nervous system”; the research is more complicated than that.

Subjectively, I could move from the more settled side with the blissful tingles to the distressed side from moment to moment and kind of move the bliss into the distress while I was thinking about the event. Afterward it felt as though the event held less negative energy, or less blocking energy, in my mind.

I care more about the hour-later version of me than the session-ending version. If I am still stewing just as hard, the shift was probably temporary and I may need to work with it again. If I can remember the event and it simply does not grab me as hard, that is much more interesting. I do not expect every useful method to erase a trigger in one pass; EMDR often takes more than one session too.

That recall test only fits when there is an event I can point to. When the material is diffuse or there is no clean memory, I watch the relevant body pattern and what happens in ordinary life instead.

With a bigger intervention I watch the wake it leaves behind. What happened to sleep that night? Am I more irritable the next day? More dissociated? In more pain? Do I suddenly feel compelled to keep processing? Am I treating people better or worse? A calm last ten minutes of a session does not prove the dose was right.

I do not ignore the research, but I also do not use the size of the literature as a ranking of what my own nervous system should try first. When a shaking, qigong, breathwork, or somatic community says it can release decades of trauma, awaken kundalini, clear stored emotion, or rapidly heal the whole nervous system, I treat that as a claim to test against life. I pay attention to people whose patterns resemble mine, change one thing at a time when I can, and keep coming back to the same unglamorous question: am I actually doing better?

Optional High-Intensity Practices: Sky Hypnosis and Vagal Blitz

Sky Hypnosis and Vagal Blitz are different animals from ordinary regulation practices: high-intensity state-shifters that a stable person may find useful even before doing any of the therapies above, but not prerequisites for healing. I keep them outside the main route.

Sky Hypnosis can make you lightheaded. Vagal Blitz is a lying-down-only practice with cardiovascular and mental-health cautions. I would use the complete guide, start with the least intense version that gives a useful shift, and stop when balance, orientation, or recovery becomes unreliable. I do not chase fainting or loss of consciousness.

I also do not count “wow” as an outcome measure. A huge release, a lot of bliss, or an altered-state shift can feel profound and still leave the actual problem basically untouched. I use the ordinary-life test above: am I more able to handle pain, boundaries, conflict, and ordinary life, or is the practice mainly helping me avoid grief, conflict, practical problems, or deeper relational healing? The extraordinary state does not rescue it.
