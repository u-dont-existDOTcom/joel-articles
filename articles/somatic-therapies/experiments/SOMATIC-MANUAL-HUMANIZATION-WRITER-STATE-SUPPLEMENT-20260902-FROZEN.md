## 1. Correction to frozen-episode Lesson C — the model may rethink an argument, but not silently

The frozen writer state said, in effect, that owner re-authoring may change the thought while a model humanization pass may not make equivalent semantic changes merely because they would improve the writing. Joel corrected the possible overreading of that lesson:

> The model CAN rethink the arguments, but not silently. If it thinks the arguments need re-thinking, it should propose the rethink it did and explain why it thought it was needed.

Durable rule:

- A model is allowed to notice that the inherited argument, premise, architecture, or source packet is weak, incomplete, unsupported, or organized around the wrong thought.
- It may reason beyond the bounded packet using the article's function, current owner material, ordinary-world logic, source recovery, research, and general human knowledge.
- But a conceptual change is **not a hidden humanization edit**. Before substituting it into owner-facing article prose, surface the proposed rethink explicitly: what the current claim/architecture is, what the proposed replacement or expansion is, why the rethink seems necessary, what evidence/experience/logic produced it, and what downstream claims/functions would change.
- Joel then accepts, rejects, modifies, or supplies his own realization. Only owner-accepted conceptual changes become article authority.
- This is not a requirement to ask permission for every sentence-level inference. It applies when the change materially alters argument, causal model, sequence, certainty, source basis, recommendation, or section function.

So the correct distinction is **model conceptual initiative: allowed; silent semantic substitution: forbidden**.

## 2. Follow ordinary curiosity upstream when a central claim feels like invented fluff

Joel described the thought route that produced the successful Introduction rewrite:

- The unsourced central idea felt as if it could sound like invented fluff.
- Natural curiosity asks: **where did this claim come from?**
- The source route led to Levine's animal observations / the Malawi park-biologist anecdote.
- The next ordinary question is: **what happened to the animals that did not shake/release?**
- The answer supplied consequence and stakes rather than merely decorating the claim with a citation.

Durable rule:

When a central claim feels generic, suspiciously self-generated, or under-motivated, do not immediately paraphrase it more elegantly. Follow the most obvious reader/author curiosity chain upstream and downstream:

1. Where did this idea come from?
2. What observation, source, experience, or problem originally made it plausible?
3. What happened in the contrasting case / when the process did not occur?
4. Why would that consequence make the claim matter here?

Use source discovery when it changes the thought, not merely to bolt a citation onto an already finished sentence. Books are legitimate source targets; use available book previews, searchable editions, libraries, excerpts, or other lawful access when available. If the exact source cannot be recovered, say so rather than inventing it.

This does **not** authorize transplanting external prose for style. Source content may change or support the argument; source syntax is not a humanization donor.

## 3. General human knowledge can extend a mechanism, but it must remain provenance-honest

After the animal-shaking source, Joel's next thought was not `therefore humans shake`. It was: shaking is one form of release, but humans are more complicated and may release through other bodily/emotional processes. Joel drew the concrete possibilities from his own human experience.

The Chat could also have generated candidate possibilities from general human knowledge/training data or research. But provenance must remain honest:

- Do not present model-supplied general knowledge as Joel's lived experience.
- A model may propose plausible human extensions, examples, or mechanisms and identify them as model/research-derived proposals.
- Empirical or therapeutic claims still need normal evidence treatment when material.
- If Joel recognizes the proposal as his own view/experience and adopts it, that owner acceptance changes its article authority.

The useful operation is **reality extension from mechanism**, not abstract taxonomy completion.

## 6. Somatic cadence diagnosis — map to the existing rhythm/thought-shape protocol

Joel's additional diagnosis of the failed Chat prose: it had an AI-like **cadence**.

This is not a new theory. It maps directly to the existing `project-sources/RHYTHM-AND-THOUGHT-SHAPE.md` findings on repeated verdict cadence, equalized thought duration, recursive mini-essay closure, and spoken-connective loss.

The failed Somatic attempts provide a local example. They repeatedly used neighboring, similarly weighted declarative beats such as:

- state the condition;
- `You can ...` / `You may ...`;
- `Sometimes ...`;
- another short explanatory verdict;
- reset for the next paragraph;
- conditional close such as `At that point ...` / `When someone ...`.

Even after sentence count and wording changed, the prose retained a metronomic sequence of similarly weighted statements with comparable end-stops. Paragraphs also tended to consume similar amounts of rhetorical time before landing a conclusion. That can sound model-shaped even when no individual sentence is obviously bad.

Admission check for future Somatic prose:

- Read the literal passage aloud or internally for **repeated rhetorical beat**, not just repeated words.
- Flag neighboring sentences that repeatedly perform the same `state -> explain/judge -> stop` job with similar weight or end-loading.
- Flag paragraphs that each occupy a similar conceptual bar-length and land a polished verdict before resetting.
- Check whether a naturally connected thought was broken into separate clean beats where an ordinary speaker would carry it through `and`, `but`, `because`, `so`, a dependent clause, or simply uneven thought duration.
- Do **not** repair by randomizing sentence length, inserting fragments, or making the prose rough. Repair the underlying thought relation and let cadence change as a consequence.

This cadence check is blocking when the writer can credibly hear the same metrical pattern in a quarantined candidate.

## 7. Typos versus idiolect

Joel clarified that ordinary typos do not need preservation. This does not change the frozen raw trajectory, whose job is exact historical evidence, but it changes downstream editorial handling:

- a confirmed typo is not protected language and should be corrected normally;
- unusual language should not be assumed to be a typo when its status is unclear;
- confirmed intentional wording such as `one-one` remains identity-bearing and should be preserved;
- do not manufacture typos or retain known errors as a humanization tactic.
