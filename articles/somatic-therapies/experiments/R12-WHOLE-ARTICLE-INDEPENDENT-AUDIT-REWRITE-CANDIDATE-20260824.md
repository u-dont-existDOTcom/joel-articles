# Introduction

Trauma isn’t only a story about something bad that happened. Your body learned from it too. It learned to brace, freeze, collapse, disconnect, scan for danger, or flood, and it can keep doing those things long after the danger is gone.

Especially with complex or developmental trauma, there is a rough logic to recovery: enough safety and regulation to stay present, some way for accumulated survival activation to move, and deeper processing that does not simply overwhelm you.

So before I go digging for the deepest memory, I want to know something more basic: can I stay here while I’m doing it? Can I stop if I need to? Can I come back to ordinary life afterward? If getting close to the trauma makes you disappear, stay highly activated, or lose the ability to function, going deeper is not automatically progress.

That is why Somatic Experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, EFT, shaking, Brainspotting, EMDR, and cognitive integration all end up in this guide even though they are not the same kind of thing. Some are therapies, some adjuncts, and some experiential practices with much thinner scientific support. They also overlap with deeper [inner-child therapy](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/): sometimes the body needs to become easier to inhabit before you can approach the younger parts of yourself without getting swallowed by them.

I will distinguish published research and common clinical practice from my own experience, community reports, and qigong or energetic explanations when it matters. Something helping does not by itself prove why it helped.

## Your Physical State Can Change What Therapy Does

These therapies do not happen in a physical vacuum. Sleep, substance use, medication effects, pain, nutrition, general physiological stability, fear learning, inflammation, dissociation, glutamate/GABA balance, and autonomic tone can all affect how much therapy you can tolerate and how much benefit you get from it.

I have seen friends get very little from these therapies until they addressed physical neuroinflammation with things like low-dose naltrexone, phosphatidylserine, DMSO, and so on. In my own experience, I cannot do real healing work when I feel like I have toxins in my gut. Ozonated zeolite helps that a lot. Ozonated charcoal water also helps me when I am surrounded by chaotic energy fields, whether EMF or auric.

I think of that as neurological de-armoring: not another trauma technique, but part of the physical ground the techniques are working on. I have separate guides on [sleep](https://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/) and [neurological de-armoring](http://dearmor.u-dont-exist.com/) if that seems relevant to you.

# There Is No Single Right Order

If you are repeatedly overwhelmed, severely dissociative, actively unsafe, unable to settle after activation, or without enough practical support to tolerate trauma work, I would emphasize regulation before going deeply into memories. But I do not think everybody needs to complete Somatic Experiencing, yoga, EFT, and shaking before they are “allowed” to do EMDR. A stable person with one clear event may be ready much earlier, and capacity-building can become its own endless waiting room if it turns into a reason never to treat the trauma.

The real test is less elegant than a stage model: are you sleeping, functioning, relating, and getting freer, or becoming more activated and more obsessed with processing? If sleep itself is the bottleneck, I have a separate [sleep guide](http://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/).

The same method can also be used at very different intensities. Present-focused self-hypnosis is not immersive memory work; gentle shaking is not forced catharsis; resource-oriented Brainspotting is not the same as entering highly charged developmental material.

# How Somatic Work and Inner-Child Reparenting Fit Together

Inner-child reparenting does not have to wait until all the body work is finished. Lighter forms can begin early and run alongside it.

If you are flooded and almost completely identified with the child-state, it may be hard to access the part of you that can comfort or protect that child. In my inner-child work I call those functions the Nurturer and Protector. Sometimes you can only find a neutral witness, or “borrow” the stance of a steady adult and imagine what that person would do. Even one ordinary Protector action can matter. Gentle [self-hypnosis](http://hypnosis.u-dont-exist.com/) can help too when it stays present-focused rather than plunging into memories.

In my experience, EFT, Somatic Experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, gentle shaking or qigong, non-forced longer exhales, and gentle Buteyko-style breathing can relax the body and soften tension around the solar plexus or abdomen so heart chakra love becomes easier to access. Brainspotting can also prepare for inner-child work when it is resource-oriented or carefully titrated.

With diffuse developmental or relational trauma, I am more inclined to use EMDR after there is at least some access to a Nurturer and Protector who can help hold what the memory work opens. A stable person with one discrete event may be ready much earlier.

And once the body softens even slightly, you do not have to use the opening to enter painful memories. Bring to mind a pet, a baby, a friend, a partner, or anyone you naturally love. Feel the real love that is already there, and see whether a little of it can move from the heart toward the younger part around the solar plexus and then back up in a loop.

I would save deep memory work, immersive child dialogue, deep hypnosis, and suggestive exploration for when you can remain present, stop voluntarily, and come back afterward.

# Start With Enough Safety to Stay Present

## Somatic Experiencing

[Somatic Experiencing](http://traumahealing.org/) makes sense near the beginning when the body cannot get close to trauma without being swamped by it. It works with sensation, orientation, impulse, defensive response, and pacing without requiring the whole story first. Titration and pendulation let you touch activation in tolerable amounts and come back toward something steadier.

Some people spend three to six months building those capacities, and severe developmental trauma can take longer. I would treat that as orientation, not a prerequisite or countdown. If you are stable and have a clear target, there may be no reason to wait that long before adding trauma-focused work.

## Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

Yoga can fit early, but I mean the slow, choice-based, non-invasive version: trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, or very gentle yin. No power yoga. No hot yoga. Nobody announcing, “Open your hips and release your trauma.” With yin, I would keep it propped, short, and choice-based.

Good trauma-sensitive instructions sound like:

- “You might try…”
- “You can keep your eyes open.”
- “You can come out of the shape at any time.”
- “Notice whether this feels useful, neutral, or too much.”
- “A posture can have several correct versions; choose one that fits your body.”

I would avoid intense stretching, long passive holds, forced breathwork, breath retention, hands-on adjustments without clear consent, aggressive hip openers, or instructions to “push through sensation.” The point is choice and embodiment, not proving how much sensation you can endure.

## Gentle Shaking / TRE

Gentle shaking or basic [TRE](https://traumaprevention.com/frequently-asked-questions/) can also be useful here, but the dose should stay low enough that you finish more regulated rather than blasted open.

Very mild Trauma Release Exercises, light neurogenic tremoring, gentle standing shaking, or short playful movement can all work. If the shaking becomes compulsive, frightening, dissociative, or hard to stop, it has gone past what I mean by gentle.

# What Helps Between Deeper Sessions

A therapy session can only do so much if the activation simply builds back up for the rest of the week. This is where I care about things that are easy enough to use while ordinary life is actually happening.

## EFT / Tapping

EFT is very nice for that. Part of it is just distraction: when you are triggered, you are giving your hands and attention something else to do instead of only being dragged along by the activation. You can also literally activate different parts of the brain by tapping different parts, so it is interesting to notice how you feel as you move through the tapping points. And it is a little bit of a massage.

Actually, I mentioned earlier that shaving my head and massaging it works really well for me. Sometimes I feel like that works even better than EFT.

The advantage of EFT is that you can use it during conflict, a shame spiral, social fear, abandonment panic, freeze, anger, or an intrusive fragment of memory that suddenly has your whole body involved. It can fit before a difficult conversation, after getting triggered, or around a therapy session when the mind is looping and the body is obviously in the loop too. I just would not confuse taking the pressure down with doing all the deeper trauma work.

## [Shaking Qigong](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) / Shaking Medicine

My friend Louka is why I am linking this particular class. He had tried many different healing and somatic modalities, including basic TRE. TRE itself did nothing for him, and nothing helped him much until he got into this class, which costs $10 per month on Skool.

The class is much more than just TRE.

By “blockage” I mean what energy is stuck in you. I often have the kind of natural shaking that animals have, rather than noticing much benefit from intentional therapeutic techniques. Especially on psychedelics, it is like my body knows exactly which part to shake and how. So it is not just “shaking = mental health.” When the right thing is shaken the right way, things loosen up as they should and the energy flows.

TRE is a system they are trying to teach en masse, so they had to distill the method down to what seems to work most of the time for most people. Obviously, if you could get more angles at it, you would get more chance of success—and less chance of being called an expert. “Just try lots of stuff till it works” is how my expertise runs on this.

I did not try the Shaking Qigong class, but I have done qigong and found it very helpful for moving energy. The class also looks like a middle ground between structured TRE and totally unstructured shaking, with a social element that may help people who do better practicing with others.

This can be more substantial than the little exploratory shakes I was talking about above—maybe 10–45 minutes as a regular practice for chronic freeze, non-verbal emotional processing, stress accumulating between therapy sessions, or leftover activation after deeper work. You still need to be able to stop and settle afterward.

Rhythmic movement may reduce muscular armoring and chronic tension, increase bodily awareness, shift autonomic state, or let interrupted defensive impulses finish. Qigong uses a different language: removing stagnation, increasing qi circulation, dissolving stored emotional charge, reconnecting breath, body, and movement. I do not assume those are identical explanations.

What I would not do is turn shaking into a catharsis contest, treat it as a standalone treatment for severe PTSD, or use it instead of skilled help when somebody is highly dissociative or unstable.

## A Discharge → Settle Sequence

One combination I like is:

1. [**Shaking Qigong**](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) **/ TRE** — let some of the activation move.
2. **Restorative or gentle yin yoga** — soften, settle, and rebalance energy flow.
3. **EFT tapping** — re-establish emotional calm and cognitive orientation if activation is still high.

With yin, I would keep the same trauma-sensitive rules: propped, non-coercive, and not an endurance contest. Long passive holds can get surprisingly evocative; especially early on I would rather use shorter holds than ambitious five-minute shapes. Yoga can also make sense before shaking or after deeper processing.

# When the Trauma Is More Body Than Story

Sometimes there is no clean memory to target. What shows up first may be a sensation, impulse, emotion, image, or fragment that is hard to put into words. This is sometimes described as *subcortical* processing; I mostly mean that the material is arriving through the body before there is a clean verbal story, not that one precise brain region “contains” the trauma.

## Brainspotting

I used my finger to direct my gaze slowly around my field of vision and noticed that different memories felt different, or different memories came up, at different angles. That was basically how I figured out very quickly that I could do a simple version myself.

In my own case, looking up-left often feels more like remembering, while up-right feels more like constructing or thinking something through. That happens to match an old Neuro-Linguistic Programming claim. The general NLP rule has not held up in controlled research, so I would not use somebody’s eye direction as a universal decoder. But gaze and eye movement really do interact with memory retrieval; my own pattern does not have to be a rule for everyone else to be useful to me.

I like Brainspotting because it makes it easy to do something like edging: go toward the uncomfortable spot, feather-touch the boundary, then come back out instead of diving straight into the worst of it. In somatic language that is close to titration and pendulation. It feels more effective to me than just hammering the distress.

Another reason I like it is that it is democratic like EFT. You can experiment with a simple version yourself; a formal session adds therapist attunement, focused attention, and bodily awareness around the gaze position.

# When There Is a Memory You Can Actually Point To

## EMDR

EMDR is more targeted toward specific memories or traumas, with recall procedures and a clearer thing you are working on. Brainspotting you can feel out, and it does not require knowing exactly what you are targeting. That is the main difference in my mind.

A discrete traumatic event, flashback, or specific trigger network is therefore a natural EMDR target: an accident, assault memory, military trauma, distinct flashback, or residual trigger that is still easy to identify. EMDR can be highly effective. A stable person working with one clear event may be ready quite early. I am more cautious about starting there when the trauma is diffuse and developmental, mainly bodily or pre-verbal, relational, or the nervous system is still unstable. That is where Brainspotting may fit better for me.

Afterward I would keep things almost boring: walk, eat healthy food, hydrate, sleep early if you are tired, do restorative yoga, journal only if it grounds you, maybe use a little EFT if activation is still there.

I also would not casually stack Brainspotting, EMDR, intense shaking, and deep yin on the same day unless you already know your system handles that much and you have skilled support. Sometimes the useful thing after processing is simply to stop processing.

# Making Sense of What Changed

## Light CBT / Narrative Integration

Trying to reframe a danger while the body still experiences the threat as present often does not get very far. Once the body is less trapped there, cognitive work has more to work with.

Then I can revisit old beliefs, build a more coherent life story, separate past danger from present reality, clarify values and boundaries, and reclaim agency. That can feed back into [inner-child reparenting](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/), identity and differentiation, and eventually things like forgiveness—when forgiveness is actually relevant—and life direction.

# How I Judge Whether Any of This Is Helping

I care less about whether a session feels dramatic than whether the trigger has actually lost some of its power. By *restimulation* I mean what happens when I remember the event later: does the same charge come back into the body and mind, or is it weaker?

While I was working on this article I was still upset about an argument with my housemate. I had been washing her moldy clothes for her, one of the dresses got discolored, and she yelled at me about it. I was still stewing over how she treated me.

So I did a quick bit of self-Brainspotting. I moved my finger and eyes slowly around while thinking about the event. When my gaze went farther to either side I noticed some *pīti*—in Buddhist language, a kind of tingling bliss—as I moved into more peripheral vision. I do not take that as proof of the simple claim that “peripheral vision activates the parasympathetic nervous system”; the research is more complicated than that.

Subjectively, I could move from the more settled/*pīti* side to the distressed side from moment to moment and kind of move the bliss into the distress while I was thinking about the event. Afterward it felt as though the event held less negative energy, or less blocking energy, in my mind.

The important part is what happens later. If an hour later I am stewing just as hard, then the shift was probably temporary and I may need to work with it again. If I can remember the event and the same charge does not come back as strongly, that is a much more interesting result. One treatment of EMDR is often not enough either; I am not expecting every useful method to erase a trigger in one pass.

For a bigger intervention, I would check again that night, the next morning, and over the next two or three days. What happened to sleep, irritability, dissociation, pain, compulsive processing, relationships, and ordinary functioning? A calm ending to a session can still have been too much.

The amount of research behind a method matters, but it cannot tell me by itself what my own nervous system needs first. When shaking, qigong, breathwork, or somatic communities make huge claims about releasing decades of trauma, awakening kundalini, clearing stored emotion, or rapidly healing the entire nervous system, I treat the claim as a hypothesis and watch what changes in actual life. I pay attention to people whose patterns resemble mine, change one thing at a time when I can, and keep asking: am I actually doing better?

# Optional High-Intensity Practices: Sky Hypnosis and Vagal Blitz

[Sky Hypnosis](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/try-this-10-second-sky-hypnosis-bodymind) and [Vagal Blitz](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/vagal-blitz-breathwork-gives-best) are optional high-intensity state-shift and release practices. A stable person may find them useful before doing any of the therapies above; they are not the “last stage” of anything and they are definitely not prerequisites for healing.

Sky Hypnosis can make you lightheaded. Vagal Blitz is a lying-down-only practice with cardiovascular and mental-health cautions. Use the complete guide, start with the least intense version that gives you a useful shift, and stop when balance, orientation, or recovery becomes unreliable. Do not chase fainting or loss of consciousness.

A huge release, a lot of bliss, or an altered-state shift can feel like healing even when very little has changed afterward. I use the same ordinary test: when I remember the event later, am I actually less restimulated? Am I more able to handle pain, boundaries, conflict, and ordinary life? If the practice is mainly helping me avoid grief, conflict, practical problems, or deeper relational healing, that matters more to me than how extraordinary the state felt.
