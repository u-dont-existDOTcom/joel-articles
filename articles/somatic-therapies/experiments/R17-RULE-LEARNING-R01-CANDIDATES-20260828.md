## Candidate R17-SE-R01 — owner-Human Introduction + Somatic Experiencing

```text
Introduction

Physical trauma reactions can continue after the danger is over, even when you know you're safe.

With complex or developmental trauma, going straight for the deepest memory can be overwhelming. Instead of diving off the deep end heroically, take it easy, one step at a time. As Buddhist nun Pema Chodron says, "feather touch" is all it takes to gently open up.  Peter Levine, the founder of Somatic Experiencing, calls this gentle work around the edges, "pendulation."

In inner-child therapy, you can know exactly what the younger part needs and still get pulled so far into the child-state that there isn't enough adult perspective left to give it.

Somatic Experiencing

Somatic Experiencing is probably the first thing on this list that I would look at when even brushing the edge of the trauma is too much. You don't have to explain the whole story first. You can start with a sensation, look around the room, notice an impulse or defensive response, and see whether the body can stay with a small amount without going over the edge.

That small amount is the point of titration. With pendulation, you also come back toward whatever feels steadier instead of heroically staying in the hardest place. Some people spend three to six months building that much capacity, and severe developmental trauma can easily take longer. But if you're already stable and have a clear target, I can't see a reason to wait the full three months just because somebody turned an average into a rule.
```

## Candidate R17-YOGA-R01 — continuous choice question

```text
Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

The word yoga covers so many different things that it is almost useless here. I am definitely not talking about power yoga, hot yoga, or a teacher announcing, “Open your hips and release your trauma.” I mean the slow choice-based versions: trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, and sometimes very gentle yin. With yin I would use props, keep the hold short, and choose a shape that is easy to leave.

Probably the simplest test is whether the teacher keeps giving you choices. “You might try this” is different from an order. You should be able to keep your eyes open, come out whenever you want, and decide that a sensation is useful, neutral, or too much. There can be several correct versions of the posture because people have different bodies.

If the class turns into intense stretching, forced breathwork, breath retention, aggressive hip opening, or long passive holds that you have to endure, it has missed the point. The same goes for hands-on adjustment without real consent. You are not proving anything by pushing through, and suffering through the shape is not what makes it therapeutic. Choice has to remain available while you are actually doing it.
```

## Candidate R17-BRAINSPOTTING-R01 — discovery remains the governing movement

```text
Brainspotting

Brainspotting is a funny one for me because I basically discovered a crude version by moving my finger around and watching what happened. I directed my gaze slowly across my field of vision, and different memories either felt different or came up at different angles. It did not take long to figure out that I could experiment with that myself.

There is one quirk I keep noticing. Up-left often feels more like remembering to me, while up-right feels more like I am constructing something or thinking it through. NLP made a famous general rule out of something like this, but that rule did not hold up in controlled research, and I would never try to read somebody else's eyes with it. I only use the pattern because my own system keeps doing it. Eye position and movement can interact with memory more broadly, which is a much less tidy claim.

What makes Brainspotting especially useful for me is the edging. I can move toward an uncomfortable spot, feather-touch the boundary, and then come back out. That is close to what somatic people call titration and pendulation, and it works better for me than hammering the distress.

It is also democratic in the same way EFT is. A person can experiment with a simple version, while a formal session adds the therapist's attunement, focused attention, and awareness of what is happening in the body around that gaze position. Being able to experiment does not mean I would use the deepest trauma as a home project, though. If you cannot notice overwhelm, stay present, or stop when you need to, that is the limit.

The cases that make me most curious are diffuse developmental trauma, pre-verbal or body-held material, and trauma that has not responded very well to talking about it. Sometimes the body seems to know more than the story, so I do not necessarily need a perfect target before the session starts moving. A younger part can show up that way before there is a neat memory to narrate, and then the Nurturer or Protector can join the work. D'Antoni et al. (2022) compared Brainspotting, EMDR, and related methods if you want to follow the research side.

Afterward, if my body still wants a gentle discharge, Shaking Qigong can fit. If I feel floaty or far away, I would rather walk around and get oriented to ordinary life.
```

## Candidate R17-EMDR-R01 — one practical distinction, followed through

```text
EMDR

EMDR is the one on this list where I usually think, there it is, that is the memory. The target and the recall procedure are clearer. Brainspotting is easier to feel your way into when you do not know exactly what you are looking for, and that is the main difference in my mind.

An accident, an assault memory, military trauma, one distinct flashback, or a very specific network of triggers are obvious examples of what I mean. If somebody is stable and has one clear event, making them spend months on preliminary body work just because the sequence looks tidy on paper would be kind of silly. EMDR may be the right place to begin.

I get more cautious when the trauma is diffuse or developmental, mostly bodily or pre-verbal, relational, or the person's nervous system is still unstable. I would want at least some access to a Nurturer and Protector before opening something very charged, and Brainspotting might fit earlier.

After EMDR I would make the rest of the day almost boring. Walk, eat, hydrate, and sleep early if you are tired. Restorative yoga can be nice. Journal if it actually grounds you, or use a little EFT if there is still activation. I would not casually pile Brainspotting, EMDR, intense shaking, and deep yin into one day unless I already knew my system could handle that much and had skilled support.

Then I watch the wake. How was sleep that night? Am I more irritable the next day, more dissociated, in more pain, or suddenly compelled to keep processing? Am I treating people better or worse? A calm final ten minutes does not tell me that the dose was right. Sometimes the useful thing is simply to stop processing.
```

- accident, assault, military trauma, distinct flashback, specific-trigger-network use cases: preserved;

## Candidate R17-SKY-VAGAL-R01 — intensity as the live question

```text
Sky Hypnosis and Vagal Blitz

Sky Hypnosis and Vagal Blitz are a little strange to put in the same article as restorative yoga because they are deliberately intense state-shifters. Somebody stable may find either of them useful even before trying the therapies above, but they are not prerequisites for healing.

The question for me is whether I can stay present, stop voluntarily, and come back afterward. If I cannot do those three things, I am not going to use deep memory work, an immersive inner-child dialogue, deep hypnosis, or suggestive exploration to find out what happens.

Sky Hypnosis can make you lightheaded. Vagal Blitz needs to be done lying down and has cardiovascular and mental-health cautions, so I would read the complete guide and begin with the least intense version that still gives a useful shift. If balance, orientation, or recovery becomes unreliable, stop. Fainting and loss of consciousness are not goals.

Neither practice gets points just for making me say “wow.” A huge release, bliss, or an altered state can feel profound while the actual problem stays basically untouched. What happens when I remember the event later? Am I any better at pain, boundaries, conflict, and ordinary life? And if the practice is mostly helping me dodge grief, conflict, practical problems, or deeper relational healing, then the extraordinary state is not doing the job I needed it to do.
```
