## Aquatic Bodywork / Water Therapy

Hale is trained in aquatic bodywork. She says people sometimes get very young in the water, even to the point of feeling as if they're back in the womb. I don't know what exactly is happening there and I wouldn't call it prenatal memory, but as an experience it isn't hard to imagine: you're warm, floating, being held, and gravity has almost disappeared. It also does something very simple physically. The therapist can stretch and move you in directions that would be very hard to reproduce on land.

The thing Hale worries about is that the emotional effect can outrun the therapist. She doesn't like dropping somebody straight into a water session cold. She first wants to know what they're feeling and what they want to work on. Then the water. Afterward, massage, partly to bring them back down. And if something is still open, she doesn't assume the hour ending means the process ended.

I would ask a water therapist about that before I booked: what happens if I come out much more emotionally open than I went in?

There is actual research on WATSU, one branch of aquatic bodywork, although not enough to make the bigger claims above scientific facts. A 2024 randomized study found immediate improvements in body awareness, pain and mood after WATSU, and a 2025 retrospective PTSD study in 111 veterans found substantial symptom improvement after a course of trauma-informed aquatic therapy. The latter had no control group.

Then there's the stupid problem of ears. Some people just get swimmer's ear easily. Earplugs and clean water don't always solve it. I don't think the main article needs a DIY vinegar recipe; a short note to dry the ears and follow medical guidance on preventive drops is enough.
