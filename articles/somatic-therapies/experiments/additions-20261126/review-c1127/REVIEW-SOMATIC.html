Introduction to this Guide

P1While my general approach to healing trauma isreparenting the inner child, I wrote this guide to somatic therapies because sometimes that vulnerable-feeling part of you is more accessible physically than it is mentally, especially at first.

P2The body was involved in surviving what happened to you, and it has its own innate resolution process. The Dominant Trauma-Memory Therapies Are Missing Something

P1Currently, as a PTSD or cPTSD patient, your health insurance (if you’re lucky enough to have it) might cover the most scientifically validated trauma therapies: Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) and trauma-focused Cognitive Behavioral Therapies.

P2All of these focus on the trauma memory itself, reactivating it in order to change the meaning and/or the processing and response to it. Their logic is simple: slowly increase your exposure to painful memories until you can fully re-live the experience without overwhelming your nervous system. The CBT trauma therapies also use regular CBT to re-narrate the meaning of the experiences, so that, for example, being abused as a child no longer creates a story that “I deserve abuse.”

P3Those therapies do show meaningful impact for many people in terms of reducing hyperarousal, and sometimes dissociation. But there are two main problems with them that Peter Levine, et al. have explained. On the one hand, as Levine points out, exposure desensitization therapy can itself be re-traumatizing, which isn’t well-captured in the positive evidence, because reasons for dropout and adverse events were often poorly or inconsistently reported. On the other hand, even when these trauma-memory approaches “work,” they are working against the body’s understanding of things by creating new cognitive-behavioral patterns that suppress the old conditioned fear responses as they come up. That can produce a reduction of symptoms without solving the root of them, in the same way that disciplining a child can force proper behavior without making it natural.

P4Another very recently discovered problem with CBT-style therapy is it assumes that people actually have some kind of inner narrative thinking, but that’s not true for everyone. So a CBT therapist might ask how you felt about a situation, and you put it into words even if it never was in words to begin with (you literally just felt it). Now the therapist asks you to change your narration, without realizing that it was created for them right then.

P5The philosophy behind somatic therapies starts from the other end. It assumes that the body actually knows how to release the tension it has stored. Instead of treating dissociation and hyperarousal as symptom endpoints to reduce, they use them to diagnose the structure of nervous system dysregulation. In Levine’s SIBAM model (In An Unspoken Voice,ch. 7), we can understand how dissociation and hyperarousal actually work by splitting up our ordinary holistic experience into five layers. Dissociation is when these layers are not well-connected. Hyperarousal is when channels are so tightly linked that one rapidly activates the others:

**Sensation**: feelings inside the body, like tension, balance, heat, etc.

P6**Image**: what you see, remember, dream

P7**Behavior**: voluntary, involuntary, and crucially, actions that got aborted mid-stream during the traumatic incident.

P8**Affect**: emotions, like fear, anger, grief, disgust, and joy. Also bodily feelings like vulnerability, hyper-vigilant panic, or profound relief.

P9**Meaning**: the story that explains all of these, containing beliefs and judgements.

P10With this therapeutic model, one can direct their attention toward individual layers of experience that are either flattened/ignored, or hogging the show.

P11Levine and many other somatic theorists believe that once the body can fully feel itself and unleash the energy that got stuck, the meaning layer that CBT addresses often handles itself. EMDR also works at the bodily level and allows the meaning level to handle itself, but it uses the body more as a barometer and metabolizer of distress encoded in memory networks rather than as the primary instrument of finishing the action that got blocked by trauma.

P12However, other somatic therapists and even Levine himself (in academic writing like the article linked), agree that re-evaluating the meanings and narratives around trauma can still be useful for many people, especially if the bottom-up process is honored first or concurrently.

Breathwork

P1There are many types of breathwork. My favorites are explained on my breathwork page, and I explain that more at the end of this article. But for the purpose of gentle grounding, I’d say the most useful one is Buteyko reduced breathing. By simply pursing your lips together on exhale, you can slow down your exhale. Slower exhales, combined with not breathing more on inhales, lead to overall reduction in breathing. That slight oxygen hunger feels a bit anxiogenic for the first 20 seconds or so, but after that, you’ll feel much calmer. This targets one actual cause of anxiety, which is over-breathing. You can use this any time, not just during a therapy session.

Somatic Experiencing

Video — YouTube video
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aDYRkLAAH2U

P1I put Somatic Experiencing up at the beginning because it helps people to reconnect with their body, to begin to feel it again. And as you can see from my intro, it’s founder, Peter Levine, has developed what I consider the most comprehensive framework for understanding trauma and the way the body releases it.

P2Imagine your body is like your child (I keep falling into that analogy 😛). Your child is saying “Mommy, daddy, I need something.” But you’re busy with your work so you don’t hear. After a while, your baby boo is going nuts because you don’t listen, and s/he starts screaming and throwing a fit. Now you yourself are “triggered” or “flooded” and screaming, and you don’t even know why. But if you could just learn to listen to your body before it becomes a crisis, that could solve a lot of problems before they even start!

P3The usual therapy theory here talks about expanding your “window of tolerance,” but I think my analogy is a better representation of what Levine is trying to say. He uses the concepts of titration (going from the shallow end of the pool slowly deeper), pendulation (going in and out of the deep end to learn safety there), and orientation (coming out of the pool, drying off, noticing you’re safe). That’s the beautiful thing about not actually being in the trauma. Since it’s a memory, you can approach it in an optimal and safe way, bit by bit.

P4While Levine doesn’t use self-hypnosis in his SE, he has mentioned it can be a valuable fit if done right, which is why I’ve integrated SE into my self-hypnosis guide and app.

A real world example of SE saving a life!

P1My friend B. is a super intelligent, caring, and hard-working PhD chemist who’s now in 3rd year ivy league med school in Colorado. Ivy league med school work load is absolutely brutal (like some bizarre hazing ritual), and on top of that, various Palestinian family members of his died recently. That’s how he went from dying to living again. He concluded from that experience that his medical school training is irrelevant for many health problems because it doesn’t contain the mind-body framework required for understanding this, and it’s a lot more common than doctors might imagine.

**Ecstatic Dance & Shaking Qigong** / Shaking Medicine

P1Shaking Qigong is a creative fusion of TRE style shaking with qigong. Qigong is an ancient sort of “martial art” designed for health. It looks a bit like Tai Chi, with slow, flowing movements connected to the breath. Qi/Chi means vital energy, and the idea is that health is automatic when your Qi is flowing well. Emotional blocks or physiological blocks can block that Qi flow.

P2The form I am linking to is a class for $10 per month on Skool which my friend Louka took. I am recommending this specific class because Louka tried many different healing and somatic modalities, including basic TRE, but nothing helped him much until he got into this class. I do not recommend taking the super expensive personal coaching from the teacher of the class unless you can easily afford it.

P3We already discussed gentle TRE/shaking in Layer 1. Now we’re getting deeper into it. This is what we talked about also in the introduction, how an animal needs to shake off the activation energy from when they were in flight/flight/freeze before they can heal. If the Somatic Experiencing didn’t get that wiggles out of you completely, you’ve come to the right Layer.

P4Some people think the benefits you get from dance, and especially ecstatic dance, might be connected to the benefits provided by shaking. Both of them contain rhythmic movement, muscular de-armoring, greater mind-body connection, and basically just a release. So if you prefer wild dancing, you can try that. If you prefer to try discharging all this pent up energy in a more organized therapeutic framework, you can try shaking medicine. Either way, it can still get crazy! Personally, I don’t feel super open for dancing unless I’m kinda high, because there’s a social/show aspect to it.

P5With shaking medicine, we might aim for a regular practice of 10-45 minutes, or whatever you feel resonates. The main thing is you want to be able to settle and orient yourself afterwards. With ecstatic dance, people could sometimes go all night.

P6I would not consider shaking or dancing to be a full trauma therapy all by itself. I’d say it’s more of an opening tool. It’s also not appropriate for everyone, as it can cause dissociation when overdone. One physical safety note: forceful, rapid back-and-forth neck movement can injure the neck. Make sure you’re supervised and held if you’re going that deep.

P1One daily sequence I like is simple:

**Shaking Qigong / TRE** to discharge excess activation.

P2**Restorative or gentle yin yoga** to rebalance energy flow and mind-body cooperation.

P3**Massage** to ground back down lovingly.

P4**EFT tapping** if there is still emotional activation or mental looping at any point in the day.

P5I have some stuck energy in my solar plexus because I got beaten up every day in kindergarten, with kids punching me right there. When I wiggle that out, I feel like I can put my hand on there and the other hand on my heart and open up some nice inner-child self-love feedback loop. Everyone will feel something different, but pay attention to what’s new, what’s freeer, what’s nicer than before. Sometimes you can miss it if you’re not paying attention.

P1This is the part of the map I think of as targeted memory work or “reconsolidation,” which refers to the way we untangle the charged memory, reprocess its significance, and put it back together in a healthier way, while still being honest about what happened. It works best when you are having flashbacks to a specific event, rather than something more general that brainspotting or other prior therapies could touch with a broader brush. The goal here is to reduce the “stickiness” of these memories, so that we don’t get stuck every time they pop up.

Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing

P1EMDR is the main modality designed exactly for reducing the charge held by specific traumatic events, like accidents, assaults, fights to the death, and so forth. Although it’s not completely understood how it works, the best theory is that by moving the eyes from side to side (or doing some other type of bilateral stimulation), you can split your attention, which weakens the durability of the memory you’re focusing on. In that more flexible state, you can re-encode a positive image (like, “I can handle this.”) onto the event before memory hardens again.

P1Hypnosis is a therapy as old as time itself. While the modern technique of it was discovered by Franz Anton Mesmer in the 1770s, shamans have been using dancing, music, and drugs to induce hypnosis for therapeutic purposes forever. We enter into trance states every night which resemble hypnosis as we go to sleep and as we wake up as well. Those states can also be used for deep therapy.

P2It’s interesting that EMDR used to be critiqued as a re-branded, minimal distillation of hypnosis. Even when you look at moving the eyes back and forth, that’s almost the classic hypnotic induction. That’s not to say that EMDR is hypnosis or it requires suggestibility, but the idea of splitting your attention is also similar. In hypnosis we use attention splitting to subdue the conscious mind and allow access to the lower-level programming layer in the subconscious.

P3I believe hypnosis can be an even more powerful reprogramming tool in many ways than EMDR, but because it depends so heavily on the technique and skill of the therapist (or you if you’re doing self-hypnosis), it is harder to do randomized trials to measure the effect size, which is why insurance generally doesn’t cover it.

P4One thing that’s exceptionally useful and dangerous with hypnosis is its ability to give us information we normally don’t have access to. You might have tried many things to understand what happened to you in childhood, and yet you still can’t quite remember. Maybe you can’t even remember your childhood at all. In that case, EMDR might be useless. Hypnosis could be uniquely useful there. But we need to be very careful to treat the memories recovered this way as provisional. They can bring up real feelings for us to work on, but the actual event narratives may be confabulated, and require empirical verification. I would treat the memories like dreams to start with.

P5I’m personally not a fan of mainstream command hypnosis, which is why I wrote my communion self-hypnosis guide that also integrates my inner-child reparenting therapy as well as Somatic Experiencing methods.

How I Judge Whether Any of This Is Helping

P1As mentioned in the Intro section, you will be the final judge of what’s helping, obviously. P2It is good to do some research before jumping into a long course of therapy, especially if it will cost a lot in time or money.
