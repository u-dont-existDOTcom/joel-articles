### Can't Stay With a Feeling?

Some people can feel their body just fine, but the moment a real feeling starts coming up in a session, they slide away from it, maybe by explaining it or by agreeing with whatever the therapist says. If that's you, look for a therapist who catches you sliding away and helps you stay with the feeling, which a few short-term psychodynamic therapies (talk therapies that grew out of Freud, only far more active) are built to do.

In [Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy](https://psykologtidsskriftet.no/debatt/2021/09/intensive-short-term-dynamic-psychotherapy-methods-evidence-indications-and), the therapist points out each slide as it happens and helps you turn back toward the feeling, watching your body for signs of anxiety as you go. People who get overwhelmed easily get a slower, more supportive version of it. If it's one feeling in particular that scares you, grief for example, [Affect Phobia Therapy](https://psykologtidsskriftet.no/node/15971/pdf) treats that fear the way a fear of spiders gets treated, bringing you closer to the feeling a little at a time, a bit like the titration in Somatic Experiencing. And for people who can't let someone's care reach them, there's [Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy](https://www.apa.org/pubs/videos/4310759), where the first job is making sure you're not alone with the feeling, and the therapist follows what that feeling does in your body as it moves.

These three overlap a lot, and all of them also work with memories and relationships, so you don't need to finish this layer before trying one. As with water therapy, I'd ask before booking: "What do you do when I start moving away from a feeling, and what do you do if I get overwhelmed?" If their answer leaves no room for you to slow down, or to disagree with them about what you're feeling, keep looking.

### Imagery Rescripting

If one scene from your childhood still leaves you feeling helpless, imagery rescripting is a way to go back into it with help. You picture the scene again, and this time somebody steps in to protect you or give you what you needed: at first the therapist, and later your own adult self. It's close to the way I [re-do trauma memories](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/i/189761959/re-do-trauma-memories) in my inner child guide. It has been tested against EMDR for exactly this kind of trauma: in [a trial with 155 adults](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32892758/) whose PTSD came from childhood, both groups ended up with fewer PTSD symptoms, with no real difference between them, and only about 8% dropped out. Since you make up the new scene on purpose, it can heal without telling you anything about what really happened back then.

### Pain That Won't Quit?

Some people come to all of this because of pain, and if you've had pain for months and a doctor has found no injury or disease that explains it, two newer therapies are worth knowing about. [Pain Reprocessing Therapy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8482298) mixes body awareness with rethinking the pain, so you learn to feel it as a false alarm from an oversensitive brain and start moving toward the sensations and activities you've been avoiding. It also works on the emotions that make the pain worse. It helped most people in its first big trial: of 151 adults with chronic back pain, 66% of those who got four weeks of it were pain-free or nearly so afterward, compared to 20% of those given a placebo shot, and most were still doing well a year later.

If what's tangled up with your pain is mostly feelings and relationships, [Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03918642) goes there directly, with writing about stress, letting out anger or grief you've been holding back, and practice being more open with people. It beat CBT for older veterans in chronic pain: 63% of those who did it had their pain drop by 30% or more (a lower bar than pain-free), compared to 17% with CBT.

Nobody has tested the two against each other yet, so I'd start with the one that sounds more like your pain, and judge it the way I describe [at the end](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/i/201523223/how-i-judge-whether-any-of-this-is-helping).

## Sources checked (2026-11-26)

- ISTDP: Abbass 2021, "Intensive Short-term Dynamic Psychotherapy: Methods, Evidence, Indications and Limitations", Tidsskrift for Norsk Psykologforening: the graded format; in clinics using ISTDP most patients have low anxiety tolerance and get a supportive, graded version.
- Affect Phobia Therapy: Schanche, Hjeltnes, Berggraf, Ulvenes 2013, "Affect Phobia Therapy: Principles of graded affect exposure in psychotherapy" (same journal): phobic reactions toward one's own feelings, "just as one can develop phobias towards external objects"; gradual exposure to avoided feelings. McCullough's manual (Guilford 2003): systematic desensitization within a psychodynamic frame.
- AEDP: APA, Fosha, "Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy": an affect-centered psychodynamic framework; the somatic experience of affect in relationship; undoing aloneness.
- Imagery rescripting: Boterhoven de Haan et al. 2020, British Journal of Psychiatry 217(5):609-615 (PubMed 32892758): 155 adults with PTSD from childhood trauma before age 16; up to 12 sessions of 90 minutes, twice a week, with the therapist intervening in the imagined scene first and the patient's adult self later; both effective; drop-out 7.7%.
- PRT: Ashar et al. 2022, JAMA Psychiatry 79(1):13-23 (PMC8482298): 151 adults with primary chronic back pain; one physician telehealth session and eight sessions over four weeks, "a combination of cognitive, somatic, and exposure-based techniques"; 33 of 50 PRT, 10 of 51 placebo, 5 of 50 usual care pain-free or nearly pain-free after treatment, "with gains largely maintained through 1-year follow-up"; NIH: "The treatment also includes training in managing emotions that may make pain feel worse."
- EAET: Yarns et al. 2024, JAMA Network Open (NCT03918642): 126 veterans aged 60 to 95 with at least three months of musculoskeletal pain; nine sessions; 63% with EAET and 17% with CBT had at least 30% less pain after treatment; techniques "writing about stress, role playing how to handle difficult relationships, recognizing and expressing anger and other feelings, and being more open with others".
- No head-to-head trial of PRT and EAET was found (the research lane's search, 2026-11-26; mine found none either).
