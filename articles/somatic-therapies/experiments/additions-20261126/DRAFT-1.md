### Can't Stay With a Feeling?

Some people can feel their body just fine, but the moment a real feeling starts coming up in a session, they slide away from it: they start explaining, crack a joke, agree with whatever the therapist says, or turn on themselves. If that sounds like you, another body technique probably won't help much, because what needs help is the way you leave the feeling, and for that you want a therapist who catches you leaving and helps you stay.

A few short-term talk therapies from the psychodynamic tradition (the one that grew out of Freud, but far more active) are built around that moment. In [Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy](https://psykologtidsskriftet.no/debatt/2021/09/intensive-short-term-dynamic-psychotherapy-methods-evidence-indications-and) (ISTDP), the therapist points out the slide as it happens and helps you turn back toward the feeling while watching your body for anxiety, and for people who get overwhelmed easily it has a slower, more supportive version. If one feeling in particular scares you, like grief, anger or pride, [Affect Phobia Therapy](https://psykologtidsskriftet.no/node/15971/pdf) treats that fear the way a fear of spiders gets treated, so you come closer to the feeling a little at a time. And if the hard part is letting someone's care actually reach you, [Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy](https://www.apa.org/pubs/videos/4310759) (AEDP) starts by making sure you're not alone with the feeling, and follows what it does in your body as it moves through.

They overlap a lot and they go into memories and relationships too, so don't take their place in this layer too literally. The name on the door also won't tell you how pushy or gentle a particular therapist is, so I'd ask before booking: "What do you do when I start moving away from a feeling, and what do you do if I get overwhelmed?" And whatever they say, you're still allowed to slow down, skip an exercise, or tell them they've got your feelings wrong.

Sources checked: Abbass 2021, "Intensive Short-term Dynamic Psychotherapy: Methods, Evidence, Indications and Limitations" (the graded format; most patients in ISTDP clinics have low anxiety tolerance and get a supportive, graded version); Schanche et al. 2013, "Affect Phobia Therapy: Principles of graded affect exposure in psychotherapy" (phobic reactions toward one's own feelings; gradual exposure to avoided feelings); APA, Fosha, "Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy" (affect-centered psychodynamic framework; the somatic experience of affect in relationship; undoing aloneness).

### Imagery Rescripting

If one scene from your childhood still leaves you feeling helpless, imagery rescripting is a way to go back into it with help. You picture the scene again, and this time somebody steps in to protect you or give you what you needed, first the therapist and later your own adult self, which is close to what I describe as [re-doing trauma memories](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/i/189761959/re-do-trauma-memories) in my inner child guide. In [a trial with 155 adults](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32892758/) whose PTSD came from childhood trauma, both the people who got it and the people who got EMDR improved, without a real difference between them, and few dropped out. The new version is something you imagine on purpose, so it can't tell you what actually happened back then.

Sources checked: Boterhoven de Haan et al. 2020, British Journal of Psychiatry 217(5):609-615 (155 adults with PTSD from childhood trauma before 16; up to 12 sessions of 90 minutes, twice a week; both treatments effective; drop-out 7.7%).

### Pain That Won't Quit?

If you've had pain for months and your doctors haven't found an injury or disease that explains it, two newer therapies are worth knowing about. [Pain Reprocessing Therapy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8482298) (PRT) teaches you to read the pain as a false alarm from an oversensitive brain, and to move toward the sensations and activities you've been avoiding, with some work on the emotions that make pain worse. In a trial with 151 adults with chronic back pain, 66% of those who got four weeks of it were pain-free or nearly pain-free afterward, compared to 20% of those given a placebo shot, and most kept that a year later.

[Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03918642) (EAET) leans harder into the feelings and relationships around the pain: writing about stress, letting out anger or grief you've held back, and practicing being more open with people. With older veterans in chronic pain, 63% of those who did EAET had their pain drop by 30% or more, compared to 17% of those who did CBT.

Both work with fear and with feelings, they just lean different ways, and nobody has tested them against each other yet, so pick the one whose way of working makes more sense to you.

Sources checked: Ashar et al. 2022, JAMA Psychiatry 79(1):13-23 (151 adults with primary chronic back pain; one physician telehealth session and eight sessions over four weeks; 33 of 50 PRT, 10 of 51 placebo, 5 of 50 usual care pain-free or nearly pain-free after treatment; maintained through one year; NIH: "The treatment also includes training in managing emotions that may make pain feel worse"); Yarns et al. 2024, JAMA Network Open (126 veterans aged 60 to 95 with at least three months of musculoskeletal pain; nine sessions; 63% with EAET vs 17% with CBT had at least 30% less pain after treatment; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03918642 describes EAET's techniques).
