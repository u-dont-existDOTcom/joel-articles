## A1, "Can't Stay With a Feeling?" (h2)
[paragraph 1]
A1-S1: If you can feel your body just fine by now but still slip away the moment a feeling comes up in a session (you start explaining it, or agree with whatever the therapist says just to get past it), there's Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy.
A1-S2: The therapist catches you at it each time and asks what you're feeling right then.
[paragraph 2]
A1-S3: It's usually anxiety about the feeling that makes you slip away, so the therapist also watches for its signs, like tense muscles, sighing, or your thinking going foggy, to tell when it's getting to be too much for you.
A1-S4: If you can only take a little of that anxiety, like most people who try this therapy, you'd get a slower version of it, where the therapist stops to help you settle whenever the anxiety climbs.
[paragraph 3]
A1-S5: If it's one feeling in particular that scares you, like grief, Affect Phobia Therapy treats that fear a lot like a phobia, getting you used to the feeling bit by bit, at a pace your anxiety can handle.
[paragraph 4]
A1-S6: And if what you can't let in is kindness, when someone cares about you and it just bounces off, there's Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy.
A1-S7: The therapist works to keep you from feeling alone with whatever comes up, and when some of their care does get in, they ask what that's like and where you feel it in your body.
[paragraph 5]
A1-S8: All three keep steering you back toward the feelings you'd rather slip away from, which can get to be too much.
A1-S9: So before booking, I'd ask the kind of thing I'd ask a water therapist: "What happens if I get overwhelmed, or if you say I'm dodging a feeling and I don't think I am?"
A1-S10: I'd want them to say they'd slow down, and that they'd listen.

## A3, "Pain That Won't Quit?" (h1)
[paragraph 1]
A3-S1: If your pain has lasted months, and a doctor has checked and found no injury or disease to explain it, there are two therapies I'd look into.
A3-S2: In the first, Pain Reprocessing Therapy, you learn to treat the pain as a false alarm: your brain is making real pain, signaling danger where the doctors haven't found any injury.
A3-S3: Part of how you learn it's a false alarm is by paying close attention to how the pain feels in your body, without being scared of it.
A3-S4: You also slowly go back to things you've been avoiding because of the pain, like going for walks.
[paragraph 2]
A3-S5: Pain Reprocessing Therapy's first big trial was only in mild to moderate chronic back pain.
A3-S6: There, 66% of the people who got four weeks of the therapy rated their pain 0 or 1 out of 10 afterward, compared to 20% of those given a placebo shot, and the gains mostly held a year later.
[paragraph 3]
A3-S7: The second therapy is for pain that seems bound up with stress and feelings about the people in your life.
A3-S8: Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy works on the feelings directly.
A3-S9: Much of the therapy is letting out anger and other feelings you've held back, and rehearsing in role-play what you'd say to someone you've never stood up to.
[paragraph 4]
A3-S10: In a 2024 trial of veterans aged 60 and up, this therapy beat CBT: 63% had their pain drop by at least 30% afterward, compared to 17% with CBT.
A3-S11: A 30% drop is a lower bar than getting your pain down to 0 or 1 out of 10, so the two trials' numbers can't be compared.
[paragraph 5]
A3-S12: I haven't found a trial comparing the two therapies.
A3-S13: So if your pain seems tied to stress or to people in your life, I'd start with the second, and otherwise with the first.
A3-S14: I'd also ask a therapist where they trained in that therapy, and keep track of how it's going the way I describe in the last section, How I Judge Whether Any of This Is Helping.
