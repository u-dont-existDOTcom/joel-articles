A1 (heading: Can't Stay With a Feeling?; goes after "Further reading: D’Antoni et al., 2022 — Comparative study o..." and before "Layer 4: Unstick the Memories You Can Actually Point To")
  paragraph 1:
  A1-S1: Some people can feel their body just fine, but the moment a real feeling starts coming up in a session, they slide away from it, maybe by explaining it or by agreeing with whatever the therapist says.
  A1-S2: If that's you, look for a therapist who catches you sliding away and helps you stay with the feeling, which a few short-term talk therapies are built to do.
  paragraph 2:
  A1-S3: In Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy, the therapist points out each slide as it happens and helps you turn back toward the feeling, watching your body for signs of anxiety as you go.
  A1-S4: People who get overwhelmed easily get a slower, more supportive version of it.
  A1-S5: If one feeling in particular scares you, grief for example, Affect Phobia Therapy brings you closer to it a little at a time, the way a fear of spiders gets treated.
  A1-S6: And for people who can't let someone's care reach them, Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy first makes sure you're not alone with the feeling, then follows what that feeling does in your body.
  paragraph 3:
  A1-S7: These overlap a lot, and they also work with memories and relationships, so you don't need to finish this layer first.
  A1-S8: As with water therapy, I'd ask before booking: "What do you do when I start moving away from a feeling, and what if I get overwhelmed?"
  A1-S9: If the answer leaves no room for you to slow down, or to disagree with them about what you're feeling, keep looking.

A2 (heading: Imagery Rescripting; goes after "While EMDR is often treated as a therapy for anyone with tra..." and before "Hypnosis, the ancestral therapy")
  paragraph 1:
  A2-S1: If one scene from your childhood still leaves you feeling helpless, imagery rescripting is a way to go back into it with help.
  A2-S2: You picture the scene again, and this time somebody steps in to protect you or give you what you needed: at first the therapist, later your own adult self, close to the way I re-do trauma memories in my inner child guide.
  A2-S3: In a trial that tested it against EMDR with 155 adults whose PTSD came from childhood, both groups ended up with fewer symptoms, and neither came out clearly ahead.
  A2-S4: Since you make up the new scene on purpose, it can heal without telling you what really happened back then.

A3 (heading: Pain That Won't Quit?; goes after "Inner-child reparenting is another form of integration thera..." and before "Optional High-Intensity State-Shift and Release Practices")
  paragraph 1:
  A3-S1: If you came to all of this because of pain that's lasted months, and a doctor has found no injury or disease that explains it, two newer therapies are worth knowing about.
  A3-S2: Pain Reprocessing Therapy mixes body awareness with rethinking the pain, so you learn to feel it as a false alarm from an oversensitive brain and start doing the things you've been avoiding.
  A3-S3: It helped most people in its first big trial: of 151 adults with chronic back pain, 66% of those who got four weeks of it were pain-free or nearly so afterward, compared to 20% given a placebo shot, and most were still doing well a year later.
  paragraph 2:
  A3-S4: If what's tangled up with your pain is mostly feelings and relationships, Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy goes there directly, with writing about stress, letting out anger or grief you've held back, and practice being more open with people.
  A3-S5: It beat CBT for older veterans in chronic pain: 63% had their pain drop by 30% or more, a lower bar than pain-free, compared to 17% with CBT.
  paragraph 3:
  A3-S6: Nobody has tested the two against each other yet, so I'd start with the one that sounds more like your pain, and judge it the way I describe at the end.
