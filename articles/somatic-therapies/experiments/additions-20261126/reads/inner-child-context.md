# Two sections of the inner child guide as live on 2026-11-25 (context for IC1 and IC2)

### Re-do Trauma Memories

Once you’ve got your cast of perfected future characters (from Borrow Hindsight ) in your mind, you can also go back to earlier traumatic memories and relive them in a better way.

For example, I’ll take one instance from my own childhood. I was only spanked one time as a child. I hung up the (expensive) phone call my dad was on with a very important official in the Soviet Union, because I wanted some attention. I don’t think he was exactly furious at me, but he decided this deserved a spanking, even if he didn’t agree with spanking in general. Since he wasn’t furious, the spanking didn’t really hurt, but it hurt me emotionally quite a lot, because I was so extremely shocked that my parents would use physical violence on me. Like I lost my sense of safety with them at that moment. And I may have learned that I was undeserving of attention, that I behaved in ways that made me deserve physical violence, that I was bad, etc.

Many people experienced something like this where the consequences for their little mind were much more severe than anyone would imagine, and in almost every case, the adult themself does not currently understand the extent of the damage until the little one feels safe to tell them. I can’t tell you how many people I’ve worked with have laughed at how brutally they were beaten as kids. Just laughed it off like it was nothing, because “you either laugh or you cry.”

Whatever the situation was, we will contrast it with the corrected version. For my friend J., whose dad savagely beat him every time he smiled, I’d have the corrected version be, he smiles, and his dad smiles, happy to see him happy. Ok, that one might seem too easy, although for J, it might take years to imagine that correction.

### Physical Pain?

Physical pain can bring on the urge to escape, and it can tie into emotional issues. Say an attack has you curled up on the bathroom floor, and whatever knocked the pain out last time (say, fent, or ketamine) starts looking real good, though you know it’ll leave you worse if you keep resorting to that quick fix.

By now, you may have already been through a million doctors who shrugged their shoulders, misdiagnosed or mistreated you, or you tried alternative treatments that were too costly or hard to grade. Sometimes it really is so simple that the best doctors and scanners can’t figure it out, like your house is moldy. It is worth going through all the basic checks first that you can do yourself, and trying the things that make most sense. (For more on that, check my detox guide .)

As you may have realized at this point, you need to be your own advocate and researcher when interacting with the medical system. If you don’t feel up to it, bring someone you trust to speak up for you (like, not that relative who never respected your wishes), and ask what a treatment’s for.

Be your own medical advocate!

And when the symptoms come and go randomly, the best time to get diagnostics done could be right at the actual time of the crisis, rather than waiting until you feel well enough to make an appointment, and they may be able to treat it while it’s happening. But what if the attacks come almost every day? You can’t move into the ER. And normal test results don’t prove the pain is imaginary, just as a treatment that helps doesn’t explain what’s causing it.

I’d ask for a chronic pain specialist and a written plan for the flare-ups you can see coming, including the new or severe signs that mean you go to the ER right away, but don’t let amelioration of symptoms be the end of the investigation.
