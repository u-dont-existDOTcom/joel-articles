## A1, "Can't Stay With a Feeling?" (h2)
[paragraph 1]
A1-S1: Water therapy and Brainspotting let a feeling come up on its own.
A1-S2: But some people can feel their body just fine and still slip away from a feeling the moment it shows up in a session, explaining it instead of feeling it, or agreeing with whatever the therapist says.
A1-S3: If that's you, look into talk therapies like Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy, where the therapist points out each time you slip away and helps you turn back toward the feeling.
[paragraph 2]
A1-S4: The therapist also watches for signs of the anxiety that's making you slip away, like tense muscles, sighing, or your thinking going foggy.
A1-S5: Most people who try this therapy can't take much of that anxiety, so they get a slower version, where the therapist stops to help them settle whenever it climbs.
[paragraph 3]
A1-S6: If it's one feeling in particular that scares you, like grief, Affect Phobia Therapy treats that fear a lot like a phobia, getting you used to the feeling bit by bit, at a pace your anxiety can handle.
[paragraph 4]
A1-S7: And if what you can't let in is kindness, when someone cares about you and it just bounces off, there's Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy.
A1-S8: The therapist makes sure you don't feel alone with whatever comes up, and when some of their care does get in, they ask what that's like and where you feel it in your body.
[paragraph 5]
A1-S9: As with water therapy, I'd ask before booking: "What do you do when I start slipping away from a feeling, or get overwhelmed?
A1-S10: And what if I think you've read me wrong?"
A1-S11: If the answer leaves no room for you to slow down or to disagree, keep looking.

## A3, "Pain That Won't Quit?" (h1)
[paragraph 1]
A3-S1: Maybe pain is what brought you to this guide.
A3-S2: If yours has lasted months, and a doctor has checked and found no injury or disease to explain it, two therapies are worth knowing about.
[paragraph 2]
A3-S3: In Pain Reprocessing Therapy, you learn that the pain, though real, is a false alarm, your brain signaling danger where there isn't any, partly by paying close attention to how it feels in your body.
A3-S4: Then, a little at a time, you go back to things you've been avoiding because of it, like going for walks.
[paragraph 3]
A3-S5: Pain Reprocessing Therapy's first big trial was only in mild to moderate chronic back pain.
A3-S6: There, 66% of the people who got four weeks of the therapy rated their pain 0 or 1 out of 10 afterward, compared to 20% of those given a placebo shot, and the gains mostly held a year later.
[paragraph 4]
A3-S7: The second therapy is for pain that seems bound up with stress and feelings about the people in your life.
A3-S8: Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy works on the feelings directly.
A3-S9: Much of the therapy is letting out anger and other feelings you've held back, and rehearsing in role-play what you'd say to someone you've never stood up to.
[paragraph 5]
A3-S10: In a 2024 trial of veterans aged 60 and up, this therapy beat CBT: 63% had their pain drop by at least 30% afterward, compared to 17% with CBT.
A3-S11: A 30% drop is a lower bar than 0 or 1 out of 10, so the two trials' numbers can't be compared.
[paragraph 6]
A3-S12: I haven't found a trial comparing the two.
A3-S13: So if your pain seems tied to stress or to people in your life, I'd start with the second, and otherwise with the first.
A3-S14: I'd also ask a therapist where they trained in that therapy, and keep track of how it's going the way I describe in the last section, How I Judge Whether Any of This Is Helping.
