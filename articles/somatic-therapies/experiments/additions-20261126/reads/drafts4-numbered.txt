## A1 — Can't Stay With a Feeling? (h2, goes after: Further reading: D’Antoni et al., 2022 — Comparative study of EMDR, Brainspottin… / before: Layer 4: Unstick the Memories You Can Actually Point To)
A1-S1: Some people can feel their body just fine, but the moment a real feeling starts coming up in a session, they slide away from it, maybe by explaining it or by agreeing with whatever the therapist says.
A1-S2: If that's you, look for a therapist who catches you sliding away and helps you stay with the feeling, which a few short-term talk therapies are built to do.
A1-S3: In Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy, the therapist points out each slide as it happens and helps you turn back toward the feeling, watching your body for signs of anxiety as you go.
A1-S4: Most people who come to it get overwhelmed easily, so they usually get a version that goes a step at a time, with the therapist helping them settle whenever the anxiety climbs too high.
A1-S5: If one feeling in particular scares you, grief for example, Affect Phobia Therapy gets you used to that feeling bit by bit, at a pace your anxiety can handle.
A1-S6: Some people's trouble is with care itself: when someone is kind to them, they can't let it in.
A1-S7: Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy starts there, first making sure you're not alone with the feeling, then following what that feeling does in your body.
A1-S8: All three also go into memories and relationships, so you can keep working with the same therapist through the next two layers.
A1-S9: As with water therapy, I'd ask before booking: "What do you do when I start moving away from a feeling, and what if I get overwhelmed?"
A1-S10: If the answer leaves no room for you to slow down, or to disagree with them about what you're feeling, keep looking.

## A2 — Imagery Rescripting (h2, goes after: While EMDR is often treated as a therapy for anyone with trauma… / before: Hypnosis, the ancestral therapy)
A2-S1: If one scene from your childhood still leaves you feeling helpless, imagery rescripting is a way to go back into it with help.
A2-S2: You picture the scene again, and this time somebody steps in to protect you or give you what you needed.
A2-S3: At first that's the therapist, and later it's your own adult self, close to the way I re-do trauma memories in my inner child guide.
A2-S4: The best evidence is for adults whose PTSD started in childhood.
A2-S5: In a trial of 155 of them, imagery rescripting and EMDR both brought symptoms down, and neither came out clearly ahead.
A2-S6: The new scene is one you make up on purpose, so it changes how the memory feels without changing, or proving, what happened back then.
A2-S7: Like EMDR, it takes you back into the memory, so I'd go slowly and do some grounding afterward.

## A3 — Pain That Won't Quit? (h1, goes after: Inner-child reparenting is another form of integration therapy… / before: Optional High-Intensity State-Shift and Release Practices)
A3-S1: The rethinking in Layer 5 has a use for pain, too.
A3-S2: If you came to all of this because of pain that's lasted months, and a doctor has found no injury or disease that explains it, two newer therapies are worth knowing about.
A3-S3: Pain Reprocessing Therapy mixes body awareness with rethinking the pain.
A3-S4: You learn to feel it as a false alarm from your brain rather than a sign of damage, and then you start doing the things you've been avoiding.
A3-S5: Its first big trial was only in chronic back pain, and there 66% were pain-free or nearly so after four weeks of it, against 20% given a placebo shot, with the gains mostly holding a year later.
A3-S6: If what's tangled up with your pain is mostly feelings and relationships, Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy goes there directly.
A3-S7: Much of it is letting out anger and other feelings you've held back, and rehearsing in role-play what you'd say to someone you've never stood up to.
A3-S8: Its main trial so far was in veterans aged 60 and up, where it beat CBT: 63% had their pain drop by at least 30%, a lower bar than pain-free, against 17% with CBT.
A3-S9: Nobody has tested the two against each other yet, so I'd start with the one that sounds more like your pain.
A3-S10: I'd also ask the therapist where they trained in it, and judge it the way I describe at the end.
