## A1 — Can't Stay With a Feeling? (h2, goes after: Further reading: D’Antoni et al., 2022 — Comparative study of EMDR, Brainspottin… / before: Layer 4: Unstick the Memories You Can Actually Point To)
A1-S1: By now you might feel your body just fine, and still, the moment a real feeling comes up in a session, you slide away from it.
A1-S2: Maybe you start explaining it, or you agree with whatever the therapist says so you don't have to stay in it.
A1-S3: (Some people could give a whole lecture on their feelings without feeling one of them.)
A1-S4: What helps then is a therapist who catches you sliding away and brings you back to the feeling, and a few talk therapies are built around doing exactly that.
A1-S5: In Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy, the therapist calls out each slide right as it happens and keeps turning you back toward the feeling, while watching your body for anxiety (tense muscles, sighing, a foggy head).
A1-S6: That sounds intense, and for most people who come to it, it would be, so most of them get a slower version, where the therapist helps them settle whenever the anxiety climbs too high.
A1-S7: If it's one feeling in particular that scares you, like grief, Affect Phobia Therapy treats that fear a lot like a phobia, getting you used to the feeling bit by bit, at a pace your anxiety can handle.
A1-S8: And if your trouble is letting kindness in (someone cares about you and it just bounces off), Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy first makes sure you don't feel alone with whatever comes up, then follows how it feels in your body.
A1-S9: As with water therapy, I'd ask before booking: "What do you do when I start moving away from a feeling, and what if I get overwhelmed?"
A1-S10: If the answer leaves no room for you to slow down, or to disagree with them about what you're feeling, keep looking.

## A2 — Imagery Rescripting (h2, goes after: While EMDR is often treated as a therapy for anyone with trauma… / before: Hypnosis, the ancestral therapy)
A2-S1: If one scene from your childhood still leaves you feeling small and helpless when it comes back, imagery rescripting lets you walk back into it, this time with backup.
A2-S2: You picture the scene again, and somebody steps in to protect you or to give you what you needed back then.
A2-S3: At first that's the therapist, and later it's you as an adult.
A2-S4: It's a lot like the way I re-do trauma memories in my inner child guide.
A2-S5: Does it work?
A2-S6: For adults whose PTSD started in childhood, about as well as EMDR: in a trial with 155 of them, both helped, and neither clearly beat the other.
A2-S7: Since you know the new version is imagined, it changes how the memory feels, but it doesn't change what you remember happening.
A2-S8: It does take you back into the memory, though, same as EMDR, so I'd stop whenever it gets to be too much, and do some grounding afterward.

## A3 — Pain That Won't Quit? (h1, goes after: Inner-child reparenting is another form of integration therapy… / before: Optional High-Intensity State-Shift and Release Practices)
A3-S1: Maybe pain is what brought you to this guide.
A3-S2: If yours has lasted months, and a doctor has checked and found no injury or disease to explain it, two therapies are worth knowing about.
A3-S3: In Pain Reprocessing Therapy, you learn that the pain is a false alarm, your brain signaling danger where there isn't any, partly by paying close attention to how it feels in your body.
A3-S4: Then, a little at a time, you go back to things you've been avoiding because of the pain, like going for walks.
A3-S5: Pain Reprocessing Therapy's first big trial was only in mild to moderate chronic back pain.
A3-S6: There, 66% of the people who got four weeks of the therapy rated their pain 0 or 1 out of 10 afterward, compared to 20% of those given a placebo shot, and the gains mostly held a year later.
A3-S7: The second therapy is for pain that seems bound up with stress and feelings about the people in your life.
A3-S8: Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy goes straight at those feelings.
A3-S9: Much of the therapy is letting out anger and other feelings you've held back, and rehearsing in role-play what you'd say to someone you've never stood up to.
A3-S10: In a 2024 trial of veterans aged 60 and up, this therapy beat CBT: 63% had their pain drop by at least 30% afterward, compared to 17% with CBT.
A3-S11: A 30% drop is a lower bar than 0 or 1 out of 10, so don't compare the two trials' numbers.
A3-S12: I haven't found a trial comparing the two.
A3-S13: So if your pain seems tied to stress or to people in your life, I'd start with the second, and otherwise with the first.
A3-S14: I'd also ask a therapist where they trained in that therapy, and keep track of how it's going the way I describe in the last section, How I Judge Whether Any of This Is Helping.
