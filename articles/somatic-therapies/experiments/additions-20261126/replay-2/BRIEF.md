## The way (from his fixes of 27 November)

- Clear up, as early as you can, every word a reader could take two ways: say whose ("the therapist" when two therapists are in play: which one), what an "it" or "at it" stands for (say the act), how much and how often a degree word means ("too much": too much of what, and always or sometimes?), and which kind ("feelings about someone": which feelings?).
- Don't join two clauses with "and" when the second isn't simply added to the first: say how they relate ("by", "so", "because"), or let each be its own sentence.
- Say how a thing helps (what it changes, and how), not the stance it takes. "It believes X" doesn't tell the reader how it helps.
- Don't open a paragraph on a study ("In a 2018 study of ...") with nothing tying it to what came before: open on what we learned, tied to the paragraph before, then give the numbers.
- When a paragraph ends on what you'd want to hear from someone, make it the concrete things they'd do, not a neat pair of reassurances.
- Where a long name will be told apart from another later, give its short form in brackets where it's named, and use the short forms where the reader has to tell the two apart.
- Give the pace and the order of steps the reader takes ("as you're able", "then").
- Plain words; keep every fact, number, link and limit; no new claims; straight quotes; no dashes as punctuation.

### Somatic guide, the section "Can't Stay With a Feeling?" (after Brainspotting, which ends on "I would ask a water therapist about that before I booked")

REWRITE A1-P1: If you can feel your body just fine by now but still slip away the moment a feeling comes up in a session (you start explaining it, or agree with whatever the therapist says just to get past it), there's [Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy](https://psykologtidsskriftet.no/debatt/2021/09/intensive-short-term-dynamic-psychotherapy-methods-evidence-indications-and). The therapist catches you at it each time and asks what you're feeling right then.

(stays) It's usually anxiety about the feeling that makes you slip away, so the therapist also watches for its signs, like tense muscles, sighing, or your thinking going foggy, to tell when it's getting to be too much for you. If you can only take a little of that anxiety, like most people who try this therapy, you'd get a slower version of it, where the therapist stops to help you settle whenever the anxiety climbs.

(stays) If it's one feeling in particular that scares you, like grief, [Affect Phobia Therapy](https://psykologtidsskriftet.no/node/15971/pdf) treats that fear a lot like a phobia, getting you used to the feeling bit by bit, at a pace your anxiety can handle.

(stays) And if what you can't let in is kindness, when someone cares about you and it just bounces off, there's [Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy](https://www.apa.org/pubs/videos/4310759). The therapist works to keep you from feeling alone with whatever comes up, and when some of their care does get in, they ask what that's like and where you feel it in your body.

REWRITE A1-P5: All three keep steering you back toward your feelings, which can get to be too much. So before booking, I'd ask the kind of thing I'd ask a water therapist: "What happens if I get overwhelmed, or if you say I'm dodging a feeling and I don't think I am?" I'd want them to say they'd slow down, and that they'd listen.

### Somatic guide, the section "Pain That Won't Quit?" (after Layer 5)

REWRITE A3-P1: If your pain has lasted months, and a doctor has checked and found no injury or disease to explain it, there are two therapies I'd look into. In the first, [Pain Reprocessing Therapy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8482298), you learn to treat the pain as a false alarm: your brain is making real pain, signaling danger where the doctors haven't found any injury. Part of how you learn it's a false alarm is by paying close attention to how the pain feels in your body, without being scared of it. You also slowly go back to things you've been avoiding because of it, like going for walks.

(stays) Pain Reprocessing Therapy's first big trial was only in mild to moderate chronic back pain. There, 66% of the people who got four weeks of the therapy rated their pain 0 or 1 out of 10 afterward, compared to 20% of those given a placebo shot, and the gains mostly held a year later.

REWRITE A3-P3: The second therapy is for pain that seems bound up with stress and feelings about the people in your life. [Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03918642) works on the feelings directly. Much of the therapy is letting out anger and other feelings you've held back, and rehearsing in role-play what you'd say to someone you've never stood up to.

REWRITE A3-P4: In a 2024 trial of veterans aged 60 and up, this therapy beat CBT: 63% had their pain drop by at least 30% afterward, compared to 17% with CBT. A 30% drop is a lower bar than getting your pain down to 0 or 1 out of 10, so the two trials' numbers can't be compared.

(stays) I haven't found a trial comparing the two therapies. So if your pain seems tied to stress or to people in your life, I'd start with the second, and otherwise with the first. I'd also ask a therapist where they trained in that therapy, and keep track of how it's going the way I describe in the last section, [How I Judge Whether Any of This Is Helping](#§how-i-judge-whether-any-of-this-is-helping).

### Inner child guide, the section "Physical Pain?" (his own paragraphs, then the one to rewrite)

### Physical Pain?

Physical pain can bring on the urge to escape, and it can tie into emotional issues. Say an attack has you curled up on the bathroom floor, and whatever knocked the pain out last time (say, fent, or ketamine) starts looking real good, though you know it’ll leave you worse if you keep resorting to that quick fix.

By now, you may have already been through a million doctors who shrugged their shoulders, misdiagnosed or mistreated you, or you tried alternative treatments that were too costly or hard to grade. Sometimes it really is so simple that the best doctors and scanners can’t figure it out, like your house is moldy. It is worth going through all the basic checks first that you can do yourself, and trying the things that make most sense. (For more on that, check my [detox guide](http://detox.u-dont-exist.com).)

As you may have realized at this point, you need to be your own advocate and researcher when interacting with the medical system. If you don’t feel up to it, bring someone you trust to speak up for you (like, not that relative who never respected your wishes), and ask what a treatment’s for.

And when the symptoms come and go randomly, the best time to get diagnostics done could be right at the actual time of the crisis, rather than waiting until you feel well enough to make an appointment, and they may be able to treat it while it’s happening. But what if the attacks come almost every day? You can’t move into the ER. And normal test results don’t prove the pain is imaginary, just as a treatment that helps doesn’t explain what’s causing it.

I’d ask for a chronic pain specialist and a written plan for the flare-ups you can see coming, including the new or severe signs that mean you go to the ER right away, but don’t let amelioration of symptoms be the end of the investigation.

REWRITE IC2: If the tests keep coming back normal while that investigation goes on, I'd also try one of the two talk therapies I go into in [my somatic guide](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/i/201523223/pain-that-wont-quit), Pain Reprocessing Therapy or Emotional Awareness and Expression Therapy. They both treat the pain as real, and they've helped people with long-lasting back, muscle and joint pain.
