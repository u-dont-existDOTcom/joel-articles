## The way (from his fixes of 28 November)

- Say what a thing does in words a reader can picture as this one's, not an act any of its kind does. When a reader is
  choosing between several options, a sentence that would fit any of them tells them nothing. Vague: "The tutor helps you
  through whatever you get stuck on." (any tutor does that). Pictured: "This tutor makes you explain the step you're stuck
  on before showing you anything."
- When the text reports a question someone asks, say it as the question it is, so the reader knows what is being asked
  about and whose. Vague: "Afterward, the teacher asks what that's like." Said as asked: "Afterward, the teacher asks
  which line of the poem you found hardest to read aloud."
- A paragraph that turns from what came before to what to do about it doesn't open on a pointer the reader has to
  resolve ("All four of these ...", "Both of them ..."): its first sentence connects what came before to what comes
  next, and what it points back to is named where the reader needs it. Vague: "All four take a lot of evenings. So
  before signing up, I'd ask ..." Connected: "If your evenings are already full, a weekly class can quietly stall. So
  before signing up, I'd ask these teachers ..."
- Plain words; keep every fact, link and question; no new claims; straight quotes; no dashes as punctuation. Change what
  the way above asks for and leave the rest of each paragraph as it is.

## Can't Stay With a Feeling?

(stays) If you can feel your body just fine by now but you still slip away the moment a feeling comes up in a session (you start explaining it, or agree with whatever the therapist says just to get past it), there's [Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy](https://psykologtidsskriftet.no/debatt/2021/09/intensive-short-term-dynamic-psychotherapy-methods-evidence-indications-and). The ISTDP therapist catches you each time they notice you slipping, and asks what you're feeling right then.

(stays) It's usually anxiety about the feeling that makes you slip away, so the therapist also watches for its signs, like tense muscles, sighing, or your thinking going foggy, to tell when it's getting to be too much for you. If you can only take a little of that anxiety, like most people who try this therapy, you'd get a slower version of it, where the therapist stops to help you settle whenever the anxiety climbs.

(stays) If it's one feeling in particular that scares you, like grief, [Affect Phobia Therapy](https://psykologtidsskriftet.no/node/15971/pdf) treats that fear a lot like a phobia, getting you used to the feeling bit by bit, at a pace your anxiety can handle.

REWRITE A1-P4: And if what you can't let in is kindness, when someone cares about you and it just bounces off, there's [Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy](https://www.apa.org/pubs/videos/4310759). The AEDP therapist stays with you through whatever comes up. When some of their kindness does get in, they ask what that's like and where you feel it in your body.

REWRITE A1-P5: All three keep steering you back toward your feelings, which can get to be too intense sometimes. So before booking, I'd ask the kind of thing I'd ask a water therapist: "What happens if I get overwhelmed, or if you say I'm dodging a feeling and I don't think I am?" I'd want to hear that they would change the approach, listen to me and be gentler.
