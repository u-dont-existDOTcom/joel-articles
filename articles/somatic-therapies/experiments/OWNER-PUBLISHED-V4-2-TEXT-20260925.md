Introduction to this Guide
While my general approach to healing trauma is reparenting the inner child, I
wrote this guide to somatic therapies because sometimes that vulnerablefeeling part of you is more accessible physically than it is mentally, especially
at first.
The body was involved in surviving what happened to you, and it has its own
innate resolution process. Peter Levine, the founder of Somatic Experiencing
therapy, in his book, In An Unspoken Voice, explains that his accidental
realization about the need to complete the body’s trauma response was
corroborated by a park biologist in Malawi. He told Levine that captured
animals who hadn’t trembled and breathed spontaneously before being
released were unlikely to survive in the wild.

But discovering the body’s programming isn’t simply something you can read
a book for. Some bodies are frozen and need to shake. Some are dissociated
and need to feel, cry, laugh, self-massage, breathe more, less, in new ways,
etc. Learning to feel the body, connect with it, and let the energy flow the way
it needs to, is like learning to ride a bike. You can read about it and follow the
practices, and even pay lots of money to a Bike Expert, but ultimately, you
have to feel it out and see what works.

and combinations of the ones already invented. It becomes a bewildering

The Dominant Trauma-Memory
Therapies Are Missing Something
Currently, as a PTSD or cPTSD patient, your health insurance (if you’re lucky
enough to have it) might cover the most scientifically validated trauma
therapies: Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) and
trauma-focused Cognitive Behavioral Therapies.

All of these focus on the trauma memory itself, reactivating it in order to
change the meaning and/or the processing and response to it. Their logic is
simple: slowly increase your exposure to painful memories until you can fully
re-live the experience without overwhelming your nervous system. The CBT
trauma therapies also use regular CBT to re-narrate the meaning of the
experiences, so that, for example, being abused as a child no longer creates
a story that “I deserve abuse.”
Those therapies do show meaningful impact for many people in terms of
reducing hyperarousal, and sometimes dissociation. But there are two main
problems with them that Peter Levine, et al. have explained. On the one
hand, as Levine points out, exposure desensitization therapy can itself be retraumatizing, which isn’t well-captured in the positive evidence, because
reasons for dropout and adverse events were often poorly or inconsistently
reported. On the other hand, even when these trauma-memory approaches
“work,” they are working against the body’s understanding of things by
creating new cognitive-behavioral patterns that suppress the old conditioned
fear responses as they come up. That can produce a reduction of symptoms
without solving the root of them, in the same way that disciplining a child can
force proper behavior without making it natural.
Another very recently discovered problem with CBT-style therapy is it
assumes that people actually have some kind of inner narrative thinking, but
that’s not true for everyone. So a CBT therapist might ask how you felt about
a situation, and you put it into words even if it never was in words to begin
with (you literally just felt it). Now the therapist asks you to change your
narration, without realizing that it was created for them right then.
The philosophy behind somatic therapies starts from the other end. It
assumes that the body actually knows how to release the tension it has
stored. Instead of treating dissociation and hyperarousal as symptom
endpoints to reduce, they use them to diagnose the structure of nervous
system dysregulation. In Levine’s SIBAM model (In An Unspoken Voice, ch.
7), we can understand how dissociation and hyperarousal actually work by
splitting up our ordinary holistic experience into five layers. Dissociation is
when these layers are not well-connected. Hyperarousal is when channels
are so tightly linked that one rapidly activates the others:

Sensation: feelings inside the body, like tension, balance, heat, etc.
Image: what you see, remember, dream
Behavior: voluntary, involuntary, and crucially, actions that got aborted
mid-stream during the traumatic incident.
Affect: emotions, like fear, anger, grief, disgust, and joy. Also bodily
feelings like vulnerability, hyper-vigilant panic, or profound relief.
Meaning: the story that explains all of these, containing beliefs and
judgements.

With this therapeutic model, one can direct their attention toward individual
layers of experience that are either flattened/ignored, or hogging the show.

Levine and many other somatic theorists believe that once the body can fully
feel itself and unleash the energy that got stuck, the meaning layer that CBT
addresses often handles itself. EMDR also works at the bodily level and
allows the meaning level to handle itself, but it uses the body more as a
barometer and metabolizer of distress encoded in memory networks rather
than as the primary instrument of finishing the action that got blocked by
trauma.
However, other somatic therapists and even Levine himself (in academic
writing like the article linked), agree that re-evaluating the meanings and
narratives around trauma can still be useful for many people, especially if the
bottom-up process is honored first or concurrently.

Layer 1: Gently Feel Inward and
Loosen Up

Breathwork
There are many types of breathwork. My favorites are explained on my
breathwork page, and I explain that more at the end of this article. But for the
purpose of gentle grounding, I’d say the most useful one is Buteyko reduced
breathing. By simply pursing your lips together on exhale, you can slow down
your exhale. Slower exhales, combined with not breathing more on inhales,
lead to overall reduction in breathing. That slight oxygen hunger feels a bit
anxiogenic for the first 20 seconds or so, but after that, you’ll feel much
calmer. This targets one actual cause of anxiety, which is over-breathing. You
can use this any time, not just during a therapy session.

Somatic Experiencing

I put Somatic Experiencing up at the beginning because it helps people to
reconnect with their body, to begin to feel it again. And as you can see from
my intro, it’s founder, Peter Levine, has developed what I consider the most
comprehensive framework for understanding trauma and the way the body
releases it.
Imagine your body is like your child (I keep falling into that analogy

😛). Your

child is saying “Mommy, daddy, I need something.” But you’re busy with your
work so you don’t hear. After a while, your baby boo is going nuts because
you don’t listen, and s/he starts screaming and throwing a fit. Now you

yourself are “triggered” or “flooded” and screaming, and you don’t even know
why. But if you could just learn to listen to your body before it becomes a
crisis, that could solve a lot of problems before they even start!
The usual therapy theory here talks about expanding your “window of
tolerance,” but I think my analogy is a better representation of what Levine is
trying to say. He uses the concepts of titration (going from the shallow end of
the pool slowly deeper), pendulation (going in and out of the deep end to
learn safety there), and orientation (coming out of the pool, drying off, noticing
you’re safe). That’s the beautiful thing about not actually being in the trauma.
Since it’s a memory, you can approach it in an optimal and safe way, bit by
bit.

While Levine doesn’t use self-hypnosis in his SE, he has mentioned it can be
a valuable fit if done right, which is why I’ve integrated SE into my selfhypnosis guide and app.

Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga
After calming down with the breathwork and learning to listen to your body
with SE, yoga can help the body-mind connection become more of a two-way
conversation, which is more useful in the real world also. After all, we need to

EFT / Tapping
The Emotional Freedom Technique (EFT) is a very simple anytime anywhere
practice that only takes a few seconds or minutes of just lightly tapping on
different parts of you head (NOT with a hammer!). It helps with all kinds of
situations. Your pet monitor lizard peed on you. Your inner child ran away

Maybe you think you’re the weirdo and nobody would want to be with you,

Massage and Energy Transmission
Massage is one of the most obvious, and yet, one of the most oft-overlooked

Layer 4: Unstick the Memories You
Can Actually Point To

This is the part of the map I think of as targeted memory work or
“reconsolidation,” which refers to the way we untangle the charged memory,
reprocess its significance, and put it back together in a healthier way, while
still being honest about what happened. It works best when you are having
flashbacks to a specific event, rather than something more general that
brainspotting or other prior therapies could touch with a broader brush. The
goal here is to reduce the “stickiness” of these memories, so that we don’t get
stuck every time they pop up.

Eye Movement Desensitization and
Reprocessing
EMDR is the main modality designed exactly for reducing the charge held by
specific traumatic events, like accidents, assaults, fights to the death, and so
forth. Although it’s not completely understood how it works, the best theory is
that by moving the eyes from side to side (or doing some other type of
bilateral stimulation), you can split your attention, which weakens the
durability of the memory you’re focusing on. In that more flexible state, you
can re-encode a positive image (like, “I can handle this.”) onto the event
before memory hardens again.

While EMDR is often treated as a therapy for anyone with trauma, the truth is
that going directly into the trauma memory can sometimes be re-traumatizing.
Not everyone handles EMDR well, which is one reason I have all these other
layers here. After an EMDR session, it’s a good idea to do some grounding
activities.

Hypnosis is a therapy as old as time itself. While the modern technique of it
was discovered by Franz Anton Mesmer in the 1770s, shamans have been
using dancing, music, and drugs to induce hypnosis for therapeutic purposes
forever. We enter into trance states every night which resemble hypnosis as
we go to sleep and as we wake up as well. Those states can also be used for
deep therapy.
It’s interesting that EMDR used to be critiqued as a re-branded, minimal
distillation of hypnosis. Even when you look at moving the eyes back and
forth, that’s almost the classic hypnotic induction. That’s not to say that
EMDR is hypnosis or it requires suggestibility, but the idea of splitting your
attention is also similar. In hypnosis we use attention splitting to subdue the
conscious mind and allow access to the lower-level programming layer in the
subconscious.

I believe hypnosis can be an even more powerful reprogramming tool in
many ways than EMDR, but because it depends so heavily on the technique
and skill of the therapist (or you if you’re doing self-hypnosis), it is harder to
do randomized trials to measure the effect size, which is why insurance
generally doesn’t cover it.
One thing that’s exceptionally useful and dangerous with hypnosis is its ability
to give us information we normally don’t have access to. You might have tried
many things to understand what happened to you in childhood, and yet you
still can’t quite remember. Maybe you can’t even remember your childhood at
all. In that case, EMDR might be useless. Hypnosis could be uniquely useful
there. But we need to be very careful to treat the memories recovered this
way as provisional. They can bring up real feelings for us to work on, but the
actual event narratives may be confabulated, and require empirical
verification. I would treat the memories like dreams to start with.
I’m personally not a fan of mainstream command hypnosis, which is why I
wrote my communion self-hypnosis guide that also integrates my inner-child
reparenting therapy as well as Somatic Experiencing methods.

Layer 5: Integrate, Differentiate,
and Build a Life of Meaning

Narrative and Cognitive Integration
Once we’ve tuned into our body, our subconscious, and moved the energy in
the ways it needed to move, there’s still the cognitive integration layer,
because we’re actually humans, not just animals. This is the part that
insurance often does pay for to start with (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy,
etc.), although this guide is predicated on the idea that it’s not always the best
place to start.
When I say cognitive integration, I’m talking about revisiting old beliefs,
identities, and stories, now that their foundations might have crumbled a bit.
We need to rebuild all that so it makes sense now that we’re seeing clearer.
When we re-evaluate and start to see that maybe we are actually safe now,
we can also take back our power. And at the same time, we might even

realize we need to apologize to some folks for painting a picture that we
wouldn’t paint about them anymore. We may begin to forgive, and ask for
forgiveness. Conversely, we might realize we need to put up better
boundaries with people that we let in too easily.
Inner-child reparenting is another form of integration therapy, although it’s
more all-encompassing and less purely cognitive, so it can fit in this layer as
well as beyond it.

Optional High-Intensity State-Shift
and Release Practices

If you feel like somatic therapies are not really cracking you open even after
neurological de-armoring, or if they have already started working but you feel
you’re ready to dive deeper, here are a few suggestions that I’ve benefited
from.
Sky Hypnosis is a very quick 10-second technique I modified from the even
quicker 2-second technique that went viral on YouTube a while back. It’s
exhilarating, but also brings an immense calm which lasts quite a while after,
especially if you sit down and meditate at the end.

Vagal Blitz breathwork is a more powerful technique in the same vein. I
developed this by modifying Wim Hof’s breathwork to get the benefits that I
sometimes got using his approach to come out more quickly & reliably.

Safety note: both of these methods are designed to produce
lightheadedness, and both of them may be risky if you have heart disease.
Practice gently at first to see how safe and manageable it is for you. Use the
complete guide for each, and don’t feel like you need to do these if they seem
dangerous.

Psychedelics
The idea of radically changing your state of mind and body can’t be
discussed without mentioning psychedelics. With a high enough dose of
these, you won’t even know whether you have a mind and a body or what the
difference is!
I’ve gotten tremendous benefits from using psychedelics, but not just any of
them. Especially loveyhuasca, cactus, and iboga. These natural plant allies
also have immense health benefits. It seems like every month some new
study comes out showing how different psychedelics can treat a vast range of
physical illnesses. That’s partly due to actual physiological mechanisms, and
partly through the mind-body interaction.
You can use psychedelics on their own, although they work much better for
trauma when carefully integrated into therapy. Go low and go slow, though,
because these tools are infinitely powerful, and you can easily end up worse
than you went in if you aren’t careful.

Advanced practices can be a bypass
Aside from the intensity risk of these optional practices, one must also
consider the risk of too much good feelings from them (or from anything else).
We can use good feelings as a distraction from actual healing. That’s called
bypassing, and it’s often discussed in the context of spiritual bypassing.
where people use spiritual practices to escape the problems of their personal
life.

How I Judge Whether Any of This
Is Helping
As mentioned in the Intro section, you will be the final judge of what’s helping,
obviously. I say obviously, but I’m surprised by how many people try a
particular practice or therapy even for many years just because they trust
their therapist, with nothing to show for it at the end. Of course, that’s part of
being disconnected from yourself. But we need to consciously take agency
back here, not just be passive doers or receivers of whatever therapy we’ve
paid for or heard about.
It is good to do some research before jumping into a long course of therapy,
especially if it will cost a lot in time or money. But even if you have a zillion
studies saying this therapy has a large effect size on the thing you’re treating,
it’s still interesting to talk to people who’ve done these therapies and see how
they were before vs after. It’s still important to see if they make some kind of
sense, and finally, you still have to dip your feet in and see if it’s a fit for you.
While we’re in the therapy, as well as after, apart from tracking obvious things
like how well we’re sleeping or how well we can focus, we should look at
whether we have more capacity to meet pain, more honesty, virtue, proper
boundaries, and healthier social interactions. Or are we still avoiding grief,
conflict, practical difficulties, and relational healing?

And as for selecting a therapist, word of mouth is one of the best ways.
Interview them and see how they respond. Are they able to show you a bit of
what they can do and what their philosophy is before charging you? Do they
actually seem like they care, or they just want to get a quick $250? If they are
soliciting clients, chances are they aren’t very good. Good therapists usually
already have too many clients. And that actually pushes them to be less open
for extending free sessions, so it’s a bit of a catch-22.
