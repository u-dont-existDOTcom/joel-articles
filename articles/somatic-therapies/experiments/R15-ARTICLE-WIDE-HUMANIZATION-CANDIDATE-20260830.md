# Introduction

Trauma can be finished in real life while your body is still doing it. You know you are safe; meanwhile you are bracing, looking for danger, going numb, freezing, collapsing, or suddenly flooded.

That leftover bodily reality is what I mean by somatic here.

With complex or developmental trauma, going straight for the deepest memory is not my first concern. First: can I stay here when something comes up? Can I stop? Will I more or less be myself afterward? If the answer is no, making the experience overwhelming does not turn it into deep processing.

What comes next depends on what is actually happening. I might need regulation. I might need to let some old survival activation move instead of trying to reason with a body that still thinks the danger is here. Or I might be stable enough to go directly to EMDR or another trauma-focused method. Those are different needs, not steps in a program.

This matters to me for inner-child work in particular. I can know exactly what a younger part needs and still be unable to reach it because everything in me is clenched around the trauma. Sometimes my body has to become a slightly easier place to live before [inner-child therapy](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/) stops feeling like I am being swallowed by the child-state.

## Your Physical State Can Change What Therapy Does

I don't put “therapy” on one side and the physical state of the nervous system doing the therapy on the other. The same session can go very differently depending on sleep, pain, substances or medication, nutrition, fear learning, inflammation, dissociation, glutamate/GABA balance, autonomic tone, or just how physiologically stable I am that day.

I have watched friends get almost nothing from these therapies until they addressed what I understood as physical neuroinflammation, using things such as low-dose naltrexone, phosphatidylserine, DMSO, and so on. My own version is that I can't do any real healing work when I feel like I have toxins in my gut. Ozonated zeolite helps me a lot with that. So does ozonated charcoal water when I feel surrounded by chaotic energy fields, whether I interpret them as EMF or auric.

This is important to me, and it connects with my other articles. I call it neurological de-armoring—not another trauma exercise, but the ground I am asking the exercise to work on. I wrote separate guides on [sleep](https://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/) and [neurological de-armoring](http://dearmor.u-dont-exist.com/) for anyone who suspects the ground is the problem.

# Where I Would Start

There is no ladder I always follow. If one event is obviously the problem and I can touch it without losing myself, I may go straight to EMDR. If touching trauma makes me vanish, flood, or spend the rest of the day wrecked, I am going to start more gently.

But “building capacity” can become its own avoidance project. I don't want to regulate forever because nobody wants to treat the thing that hurts. The question is what happens away from the therapy: am I sleeping, functioning, relating, and getting freer? Sometimes sleep is the whole bottleneck; that is why I have a separate [sleep guide](http://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/).

The name of a method tells me less than the intensity and the way I use it. Present-focused self-hypnosis is not immersive memory work. A small amount of shaking is not forced catharsis. Brainspotting can help me orient and resource, or it can take me into much hotter material. Same label, very different experience.

# Somatic Work and Inner-Child Reparenting

The gentler body work matters to me because it can make reparenting possible. Regulation itself is not the grand finale.

When I am completely flooded, I am basically the child-state. There may not be enough adult perspective online to comfort the child or keep it safe. In my inner-child work, those adult functions are the Nurturer and Protector. Sometimes I cannot find either one yet. I can still try being a neutral witness, or borrow adulthood for a moment: take the position of a steady adult even if it does not feel fully mine. One ordinary protective action is already something. Gentle [self-hypnosis](http://hypnosis.u-dont-exist.com/) can help as long as it stays in the present instead of pulling me deep into memory or child dialogue.

EFT, Somatic Experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, gentle shaking or qigong, a longer exhale that is not forced, gentle Buteyko-style breathing—I have found all of these useful for softening the body around the abdomen or solar plexus. When that area unclenches, heart-chakra love is easier for me to reach. The body work can come before inner-child work or keep going beside it.

Brainspotting can be part of the reparenting too. Using it for a resource, or going in very carefully, is nothing like using it to enter highly charged developmental material. With diffuse developmental or relational trauma I lean toward waiting on EMDR until there is at least some Nurturer and Protector available to hold what gets opened. I would not turn that into a universal rule. Someone stable who has one discrete event may be ready much sooner.

And if my body softens, I don't have to spend the opening hunting for pain. I can think of a pet, a baby, a friend, a partner—someone I genuinely love. Feel the love that is already there. Then I see if a little of it can travel from my heart toward the younger part I feel around the solar plexus, and back up again, almost like a loop.

Deep memory work, immersive child dialogue, deep hypnosis, suggestive exploration: I save those for when staying present, stopping voluntarily, and coming back are things I can actually do.

# Building Enough Safety to Stay Present

## Somatic Experiencing

[Somatic Experiencing](http://traumahealing.org/) is one of the methods I reach for when merely approaching the material sends my body over the edge. It gives me sensation, orientation, impulse and defensive response to work with, slowly, without demanding the whole story first.

Titration and pendulation are useful partly because there is no prize for beating the trauma in a fight. I touch as much activation as I can honestly tolerate, notice what happens, and come back toward something steadier. Some people spend three to six months building that capacity, and severe developmental trauma can take longer. I treat that as orientation, not a countdown. If I am already stable and the target is clear, I see no reason to wait out an arbitrary number of months before adding focused trauma work.

## Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

By yoga, I do not mean power yoga, hot yoga, or somebody declaring, “Open your hips and release your trauma.” I mean slow and choice-based: trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, perhaps very gentle yin. For yin, I want props, short holds, and an easy exit.

The instructions should sound more like this:

- “You might try…”
- “You can keep your eyes open.”
- “Come out of the shape whenever you want.”
- “Is this useful, neutral, or too much?”
- “There can be several right versions. Pick the one that fits your body.”

Intense stretching, breath retention, forced breathwork, aggressive hip opening, long passive holds, uninvited hands-on adjustments, pushing through because suffering is supposedly therapeutic—that is not what I am recommending. Once I can't choose, it isn't the practice I mean anymore.

## Gentle Shaking / TRE

Here I mean a little shaking, not an attempt to blast trauma out of my body. Basic [TRE](https://traumaprevention.com/frequently-asked-questions/), very light neurogenic tremoring, gentle shaking while standing, even a short bit of playful movement may be plenty. Once it feels compulsive, frightening, dissociative, or difficult to stop, it is no longer what I mean by gentle.

# What I Use Between Deeper Sessions

A wonderful therapy session is not worth much if I spend the following week rebuilding all the pressure. I need at least one thing that still works while ordinary life is happening.

## EFT / Tapping

EFT is very nice for this. It regulates me, distracts me, and gives my hands something to do other than ride the reaction all the way down.

I think of the different tapping points as activating different parts of the brain. That is my thought, not a neuroscience result I am proving here. I move through the points and see how I feel at each one. It is also a little massage. Actually, I feel like shaving my head and massaging it works really well for me—maybe even better than EFT.

The big advantage is that EFT travels. I can tap before a difficult conversation, immediately after somebody triggers me, or when a thought loop has recruited my whole body. It can take the pressure down. I don't confuse that with finishing the deeper trauma work.

## [Shaking Qigong](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) / Shaking Medicine

My friend Louka is why I am linking this particular class. He had tried many different healing and somatic modalities, including basic TRE. TRE itself did nothing for him, and nothing helped him much until he got into this class, which costs $10 per month on Skool.

The class is much more than just TRE.

By “blockage” I mean what energy is stuck in you. I often have the kind of natural shaking that animals have, rather than noticing much benefit from intentional therapeutic techniques. Especially on psychedelics, it is like my body knows exactly which part to shake and how. So it is not just “shaking = mental health.” When the right thing is shaken the right way, things loosen up as they should and the energy flows.

TRE is a system they are trying to teach en masse, so I think they had to distill the method down to what seems to work most of the time for most people. If that is right, more angles should give more chances of success—and less chance of being called an expert. “Just try lots of stuff till it works” is how my expertise runs on this.

I haven't tried the linked class myself. What interests me is the extra movements and positions. My guess is that some people need the right position or movement together with the tremor before they find the blockage. I have done qigong, though, and it helped me move energy. I can see why guided shaking plus qigong might sit between a completely predictable TRE routine and unstructured shaking. There is a social side too. For someone who needs guidance, gets nothing from standard TRE, or benefits from seeing other people get results, that middle ground could matter.

The short exploratory shakes earlier are a different dose. For a regular practice, I can imagine anything from 10 to 45 minutes—my estimate, not a duration I learned from or tested in Louka's class. I am thinking of chronic freeze, emotional material with no words, stress that piles up between therapy sessions, or activation still hanging around after deeper work. Whatever the duration, I need to be able to stop and settle afterward.

Why might shaking help? I don't know exactly. Muscles or defensive impulses may get to complete something. Movement and close bodily attention may change arousal. In qigong language, stuck qi starts moving. I am fine leaving those as different possible accounts. I don't need one to certify the others.

I stop taking the experiment lightly when shaking becomes a catharsis competition or gets sold as a complete treatment for severe PTSD. If somebody is highly dissociative or unstable, experimentation is not a substitute for skilled help.

## A Discharge → Settle Sequence

Sometimes I want the shaking to land somewhere rather than leave my system revving. A combination I like is:

1. [**Shaking Qigong**](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) **/ TRE** — let some activation move.
2. **Restorative or gentle yin yoga** — soften and let the energy rebalance.
3. **EFT tapping** — if I am still activated, come back toward emotional calm and cognitive orientation.

The same yin rules still apply: props, no coercion, no endurance contest. Long passive holds can become surprisingly evocative, so I would rather start short than prove I can survive an ambitious five-minute shape. Yoga can come before shaking, too, or after deeper processing. This sequence is something I like, not a law.

# When the Body Knows More Than the Story

Sometimes there is no tidy memory. I have a body reaction, an image, an impulse, an emotion, a fragment—and no coherent explanation attached to it. This is sometimes called *subcortical* processing. I use the word in a limited way: the material is arriving before its story. I am not saying one exact brain region contains the trauma.

## Brainspotting

I used my finger to guide my gaze slowly around my field of vision and watched what happened. Would the same memory feel different at another angle? Would a different memory come up? It was intuitive; I more or less figured out immediately how to experiment on myself.

There is one pattern I notice in me: up-left often feels more like remembering, while up-right feels more like constructing or thinking something through. NLP made a famous general rule out of something similar, and controlled research has not supported that rule. So I do not look at somebody else's eyes and decide what their brain is doing. I only use the pattern because my own system repeats it. More generally, gaze and eye movement do interact with memory retrieval.

The part I like most is what I call edging, although I don't know if that is the right word. I move toward the uncomfortable spot, feather-touch its edge, and come out again instead of jumping straight into the deep end. In somatic language this is close to titration and pendulation. It feels more useful to me than hammering away at the distress.

Brainspotting also gets so many points from me for being democratic like EFT. I don't need an expensive expert for a simple experiment, though I could use one: a formal session adds therapist attunement, focused attention, and awareness of what my body is doing around the gaze position.

Being able to do something myself does not make the deepest trauma a good solo experiment. If I cannot tell when I am overwhelmed, stay present, or stop, that is the boundary.

Brainspotting makes particular sense to me when the trauma is diffuse, developmental, pre-verbal, held mainly in the body, or simply has not responded to thought-based approaches. I do not have to know the exact target before something starts moving. That also lets it meet inner-child reparenting in a natural way: a younger part can show up in my body before I have a neat memory to tell, and the Nurturer or Protector can be there when it does. # When I Can Point to the Memory

## EMDR

EMDR feels more like this to me: there it is. That is the memory. The target and the recall procedure are clearer. Brainspotting is easier to feel my way into when I don't yet know what I am looking for. That is the distinction I actually use.

An accident, an assault memory, military trauma, a distinct flashback, a specific trigger network—those are the kinds of targets that make me think of EMDR. It can be highly effective. If somebody is stable and one event is clear, I wouldn't make them complete months of body work just to satisfy a tidy sequence.

I am more cautious when the trauma is diffuse or developmental, mainly bodily or pre-verbal, relational, or when the nervous system is still unstable. There I want at least a little access to the Nurturer and Protector before opening very charged material. Brainspotting may make more sense earlier.

After EMDR I want an almost comically boring day. Walk. Eat something. Drink water. Sleep early if I am tired. Restorative yoga, if it genuinely feels good. I journal only if the journaling grounds me, and I might use a little EFT if activation is still hanging around. What I don't casually do is stack Brainspotting, EMDR, intense shaking, and deep yin on the same day. Not unless I already know my system can handle that dose and skilled support is there.

Sometimes the useful thing after processing is to stop processing.

# Making Sense of What Changed

## Light CBT / Narrative Integration

When my body stops insisting that the old thing is happening right now, I can finally talk to the belief. Before that, I may win the argument in my head and still lose it everywhere else.

This is where I revisit old beliefs and make a life story that is more coherent. I can separate past danger from present reality; look again at values, boundaries, agency, and where my life is going; even think about forgiveness when forgiveness is actually relevant. It circles back to [inner-child reparenting](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/). The adult is more available, and I can tell the child-state from the whole of me.

# How I Know Whether It Actually Helped

My outcome measure is boring: does the thing still hit as hard later? By *restimulation* I mean what happens when I remember the event again. Does the same charge come back into the body and mind, or not?

While I was working on this article I was still upset about an argument with my housemate. I had been washing her moldy clothes for her, one of the dresses got discolored, and she yelled at me about it. I was still stewing over how she treated me.

So I did a quick bit of self-Brainspotting. I moved my finger and eyes slowly around while thinking about the event. When my gaze went farther to either side I noticed blissful tingles as I moved into more peripheral vision. I do not take that as proof of the simple claim that “peripheral vision activates the parasympathetic nervous system”; the research is more complicated than that.

Subjectively, I could move from the more settled side with the blissful tingles to the distressed side from moment to moment and kind of move the bliss into the distress while I was thinking about the event. Afterward it felt as though the event held less negative energy, or less blocking energy, in my mind.

What I care about is the version of me an hour later, not the one enjoying the last minute of the exercise. Am I still stewing just as hard? Then the change was probably temporary, and I may need to try again. Can I remember what happened without getting grabbed in the same way? That is interesting. I don't expect every useful method to wipe out a trigger in one pass. EMDR often takes more than one session as well.

A bigger intervention leaves a wake, and sometimes I only notice it the next day: sleep is off, I am irritable or more dissociated, pain is worse, or I have this weird compulsion to keep processing. How I treat people belongs in the wake too. The session might have ended with ten calm minutes and still have been far too much.

Research matters to me. I just don't use the size of a literature as a ranking algorithm for my nervous system. A shaking, qigong, breathwork, or somatic community may say that a practice releases decades of trauma, awakens kundalini, clears stored emotion, or rapidly heals the whole nervous system. My own result cannot prove all of those explanations. What I can test is whether the practice does anything useful in my life. I look at people whose patterns resemble mine, change one thing at a time when I can, and return to the least glamorous question in the article: am I actually doing better?

# Optional High-Intensity Practices: Sky Hypnosis and Vagal Blitz

[Sky Hypnosis](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/try-this-10-second-sky-hypnosis-bodymind) and [Vagal Blitz](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/vagal-blitz-breathwork-gives-best) are different animals. They shift state hard. I have put them here, outside the main route, because they are not prerequisites for healing—although a stable person might still find them useful much earlier.

Sky Hypnosis can make me lightheaded. Vagal Blitz is for lying down only and comes with cardiovascular and mental-health cautions. I use the complete guide, begin with the least intense version that produces a useful change, and stop if balance, orientation, or recovery starts becoming unreliable. Fainting and loss of consciousness are not goals.

I don't count “wow” as a result either. A huge release, bliss, an altered state—it can all feel profound while the actual problem sits there untouched. Later, can I handle pain, boundaries, conflict, and ordinary life any better? If the extraordinary practice is mainly helping me dodge grief, conflict, practical problems, or deeper relational healing, its intensity does not rescue it.
