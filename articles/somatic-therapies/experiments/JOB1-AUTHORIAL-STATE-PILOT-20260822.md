## Control C — registered r01 realization

### Job 1: Stay Present and Keep Choice

#### Somatic Experiencing

Somatic Experiencing makes sense to me as an early option when the main problem is that activation runs away with you. Instead of forcing a detailed trauma narrative, it pays attention to sensation, orientation, impulse, defensive response, and pacing. The basic skill is noticing activation without immediately drowning in it.

This is where the usual language about expanding your “window of tolerance” becomes practical. Titration, pendulation, and orientation are all ways of asking: can you touch something difficult in a small enough dose, notice what happens, and come back? Some people may spend three to six months mainly building those capacities, and severe developmental trauma can take longer. I would treat that as orientation, not a countdown. If you are stable and have one clear target, you may be ready for focused trauma work much sooner.

#### Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

Yoga can fit early too, but I mean a very particular kind of yoga. No power yoga. No hot yoga. Nobody announcing, “Open your hips and release your trauma.” The useful version is slow, choice-based, and non-invasive, with body awareness, flexibility, enjoyment, and agency as the point.

Good options include trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, and very gentle yin when it is propped, short, and easy to leave. The language matters too. “You might try…” is very different from “stay there.” “You can keep your eyes open.” “You can come out whenever you want.” “Notice whether this feels useful, neutral, or too much.” A posture can have several valid versions; choose one that actually fits your body.

I would avoid intense stretching, long passive holds, forced breathwork, breath retention, hands-on adjustments without clear consent, aggressive hip openers, or anybody telling you to push through sensation. If the point is to rebuild choice, the practice should contain actual choices.

## Test D — authorial-state realization

### Job 1: Stay Present and Keep Choice

#### Somatic Experiencing

If activation runs away with you, I do not see much point in forcing a detailed trauma story just because trauma work is supposed to go “deep.” The part of Somatic Experiencing that makes sense to me early is much more basic: sensation, orientation, impulse, defensive response, pacing—can you notice what starts happening without immediately drowning in it?

That is where “window of tolerance” stops being jargon for me. Titration, pendulation, orientation: touch something difficult in a small enough dose that you can still notice it, and then come back. Some people may spend three to six months mostly building that capacity, and severe developmental trauma can take longer, but I would not turn the number into a waiting period. It is orientation, not a countdown. If you are stable and there is one clear target, focused trauma work may make sense much sooner.

#### Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

Yoga can help with the same job, and it can also defeat the point if the practice takes choice away. I am not talking about power yoga or hot yoga, and definitely not somebody announcing, “Open your hips and release your trauma.” I mean slow, choice-based, non-invasive yoga where body awareness, flexibility, enjoyment, and agency are actually the point.

Trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, or very gentle yin can all fit. With yin especially: prop it, keep it short, and make it easy to leave. “You might try…” is different from “stay there.” You can keep your eyes open. You can come out whenever you want. You can notice whether something feels useful, neutral, or too much. There can be several valid versions of a posture; use the one that fits your body.

I would avoid intense stretching, long passive holds, forced breathwork, breath retention, hands-on adjustments without clear consent, aggressive hip openers, and instructions to push through sensation. If the point is rebuilding choice, the practice needs to contain actual choices.
