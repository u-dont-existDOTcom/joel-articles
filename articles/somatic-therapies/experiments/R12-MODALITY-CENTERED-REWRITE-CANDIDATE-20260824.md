# Introduction

Trauma isn’t only a story about something bad that happened. Your body learned from it too. It learned to brace, freeze, collapse, disconnect, scan for danger, or flood, and it can keep doing those things long after the danger is gone.

For many people, especially people with complex or developmental trauma, there is still a rough order that makes sense. You need enough safety and nervous-system regulation to stay present; some of the accumulated stress and survival response may need somewhere to go; and then deeper trauma processing becomes much more workable.

So before I go digging for the deepest memory, I want to know something more basic: can I stay here while I’m doing it? Can I stop if it is too much? Can I come back to ordinary life afterward? If getting close to the trauma makes you disappear, stay highly activated, or lose the ability to function, going deeper is not automatically progress.

That does not mean everybody needs to “complete” Somatic Experiencing, yoga, EFT, or shaking before doing Brainspotting or EMDR. These are different modalities and practices. Some are formal therapies; some are adjuncts or experiential practices with much thinner scientific support. A stable person with one clear traumatic memory may be ready for EMDR quite early. Somebody with diffuse developmental trauma who becomes overwhelmed whenever they get close to it may need more time working with regulation, body awareness, or less direct forms of processing first.

The name of the modality is not enough to tell you how intense the work will be. Present-focused self-hypnosis is different from immersive memory work; resource-oriented Brainspotting is different from entering highly charged developmental material; gentle shaking is different from cathartic release. Several of these can overlap, and you can use more than one without pretending you are moving through a fixed protocol.

I also do not want preparation to become an endless waiting room. At some point the trauma itself has to change. I care about sleep, relationships, functioning, freedom, and how much power the triggers still have—not whether I have completed the “right” sequence of therapies or had an impressively dramatic session. If sleep itself is the bottleneck, I have a separate [sleep guide](http://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/).

## Neurological De-Armoring and Physical State

These therapies do not happen in a physical vacuum. Sleep, substance use, medication effects, pain, nutrition, general physiological stability, fear learning, inflammation, dissociation, glutamate/GABA balance, and autonomic tone can all affect how much therapy you can tolerate and how much benefit you get from it.

I have seen friends get very little from these therapies until they addressed physical neuroinflammation with things like low-dose naltrexone, phosphatidylserine, DMSO, and so on. In my own experience, I cannot do real healing work when I feel like I have toxins in my gut. Ozonated zeolite helps that a lot. Ozonated charcoal water also helps me when I am surrounded by chaotic energy fields, whether EMF or auric.

I have separate guides on [sleep](https://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/) and [neurological de-armoring](http://dearmor.u-dont-exist.com/) for that side of it.

# How Somatic Work and Inner-Child Reparenting Fit Together

Somatic work and [inner-child therapy](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/) overlap a lot. Reparenting is not something that only happens after somatic work; lighter forms can run alongside it. Sometimes the body needs to become a little easier to inhabit before you can approach the younger parts of yourself without simply getting swallowed by them.

In the inner-child framework I use, I call the caring adult capacity the Nurturer and the protective adult capacity the Protector. If somebody is flooded and almost completely identified with the child-state, they may not be able to access either one yet. Then the first useful move may be simple orientation, outside safety, a neutral witness, borrowing some adult steadiness from another person, or one ordinary Protector action. Gentle [self-hypnosis](http://hypnosis.u-dont-exist.com/) can also help when it stays present-focused instead of going deeply into memories or child dialogue.

In my experience, EFT, Somatic Experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, gentle shaking or qigong, non-forced longer exhales, and gentle Buteyko-style breathing can help the body relax and soften tension around the solar plexus or abdomen so heart chakra love becomes easier to access.

Once the body softens even slightly, you do not have to use the opening to enter painful memories. Bring to mind a pet, a baby, a friend, a partner, or anyone you naturally love. Feel the real love that is already there, and see whether a little of it can move from the heart toward the younger part around the solar plexus and then back up in a loop.

Brainspotting can also prepare for inner-child work or run alongside it. A resource-oriented or carefully titrated session is very different from using it to enter highly charged developmental material. For diffuse developmental or relational trauma, I am more inclined to do EMDR after there is at least some access to a Nurturer and Protector that can help hold what the memory work opens. That is not a universal rule; a stable person working with one discrete traumatic event may be ready for EMDR much earlier.

Deep memory work, immersive child dialogue, deep hypnosis, and suggestive exploration are different again. If I cannot remain present, stop voluntarily, and return to ordinary life afterward, I would not take those as the next step yet.

# Somatic Experiencing

[Somatic Experiencing](http://traumahealing.org/) makes sense to me near the beginning when somebody is easily overwhelmed because it can work with sensation, orientation, impulse, defensive response, and nervous-system pacing without forcing the person to narrate the trauma before the body can tolerate it.

The point is not the deepest session possible. It is being able to notice activation without immediately getting swallowed by it. Titration, pendulation, orientation, and learning the difference between activation and overwhelm are useful for exactly that.

Some people spend three to six months building these capacities, and severe developmental trauma can take longer. I would take that as orientation, not a prerequisite or countdown. A stable person with a clear target may move into trauma-focused therapy much sooner.

# Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

Yoga can fit early too, but I mean the slow, choice-based, non-invasive version: trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, or very gentle yin. No power yoga. No hot yoga. Nobody announcing, “Open your hips and release your trauma.” With yin, I would keep it propped, short, and choice-based.

Good trauma-sensitive instructions sound like:

- “You might try…”
- “You can keep your eyes open.”
- “You can come out of the shape at any time.”
- “Notice whether this feels useful, neutral, or too much.”
- “A posture can have several correct versions; choose one that fits your body.”

I would avoid intense stretching, long passive holds, forced breathwork, breath retention, hands-on adjustments without clear consent, aggressive hip openers, or instructions to “push through sensation.” The objective is choice, safety, and embodiment—not dramatic release.

Yoga also does not have one fixed place in trauma work. It can help with body awareness and agency before deeper therapy, help somebody settle after shaking or processing, or simply feel good later because stretching and reconnection are what the body wants.

# EFT / Tapping

EFT is very nice for keeping activation from building up between deeper sessions. Part of it is just distraction: when you are triggered, you are giving your hands and attention something else to do instead of only getting dragged along by the activation. You can also literally activate different parts of the brain by tapping different parts, so it is interesting to notice how you feel as you move through the tapping points. And it is a little bit of a massage.

I mentioned earlier that shaving my head and massaging it works really well for me. Sometimes I feel like that works even better than EFT.

The practical advantage is that EFT is quick enough to use while life is happening: conflict, a shame spiral, social fear, abandonment panic, freeze, anger, or an intrusive fragment of memory that suddenly has your whole body involved. You can use it before a difficult conversation, after getting triggered, or around a therapy session when the mind is looping and the body is obviously in the loop too.

That is also why I like it as a bridge between body-based and cognitive work. I just would not confuse lowering the pressure with doing all the deeper work, especially with severe trauma.

# [Shaking Qigong](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) / Shaking Medicine

My friend Louka is why I am linking this particular class. He had tried many different healing and somatic modalities, including basic TRE. TRE itself did nothing for him, and nothing helped him much until he got into this class, which costs $10 per month on Skool.

The class is much more than just TRE.

By “blockage” I mean what energy is stuck in you. I often have the kind of natural shaking that animals have, rather than noticing much benefit from intentional therapeutic techniques. Especially on psychedelics, it is like my body knows exactly which part to shake and how. So it is not just “shaking = mental health.” When the right thing is shaken the right way, things loosen up as they should and the energy flows.

TRE is a system they are trying to teach en masse, so they had to distill the method down to what seems to work most of the time for most people. Obviously, if you could get more angles at it, you would get more chance of success—and less chance of being called an expert. “Just try lots of stuff till it works” is how my expertise runs on this.

I did not try the Shaking Qigong class, but I have done qigong and it was very helpful in moving energy, so I can understand how the two would naturally go together. I can also see how it might be more of a middle ground between fully predictable TRE structure and totally unstructured shaking. That probably suits more people who feel like they need some guidance but are not able to benefit from standard TRE. It is also a social thing, so for many people it works better than individual practice when they see others getting results.

There are at least two very different ways I am talking about shaking in this article. One is mild and exploratory: a few minutes of shaking where the aim is simply to loosen up and stay regulated. The other is something you might use as a regular discharge practice, maybe 10–45 minutes, for chronic freeze, non-verbal emotional processing, stress accumulating between therapy sessions, or leftover activation after deeper work. With the more sustained version, I still want to know that I can stop, orient, and settle afterward.

Why shaking helps is less certain. Rhythmic movement could reduce muscular armoring and chronic tension, increase awareness of bodily sensation, shift the system out of fight-or-flight, or let interrupted defensive impulses finish. Qigong describes what is happening in different language: removing stagnation, increasing qi circulation, dissolving stored emotional charge, and reconnecting breath, body, and movement. Those explanations are not the same kind of evidence, even when they point toward a similar experience.

I would not turn shaking into a catharsis contest, use it as a standalone treatment for severe PTSD, make it the primary trauma-processing modality for somebody who is highly dissociative or unstable, or use it instead of skilled help in that situation.

## A Discharge → Settle Sequence

One sequence I like is:

1. [**Shaking Qigong**](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) **/ TRE** — discharge excess activation.
2. **Restorative or gentle yin yoga** — let the system soften, settle, and rebalance energy flow.
3. **EFT tapping** — re-establish emotional calm and cognitive orientation.

With yin, I would still keep it gentle, propped, and non-coercive. Long passive holds can get too evocative in trauma work, and especially early on I would rather use shorter holds than ambitious five-minute shapes. Nobody should be encouraging you to endure sensation for the sake of “release.”

# Brainspotting

Sometimes there is no clean memory to target. What shows up first may be a sensation, impulse, emotion, image, or fragments that are hard to put into words. Brainspotting interests me especially for that kind of material.

I used my finger to direct my gaze slowly around the field of vision to see how different memories felt, or what memories came up at different angles. That was basically how I figured out very quickly that I could do a simple version myself.

In my own case, looking up-left often feels more like remembering, while up-right feels more like constructing or thinking something through. That happens to match an old Neuro-Linguistic Programming claim. The general eye-direction rule has not held up in controlled research, so I would not use it as a universal decoder. The more interesting point for me is simply that changing gaze can change what comes up and how a memory feels.

I like Brainspotting because it makes it easy to do something like edging: going in and out of the uncomfortable spot and feather-touching the boundary instead of jumping straight into the deep end. In somatic language, this is close to titration and pendulation. It feels more effective to me. Graduated exposure can reduce peak distress too, so I do not take my preference as a general rule.

I also give Brainspotting extra points because it is democratic like EFT. You do not necessarily need an expensive expert every time you want to experiment with a simple version, although formal Brainspotting adds therapist attunement to the gaze position, focused attention, and bodily awareness.

Being able to experiment with it yourself does not mean diving into the deepest trauma alone. If I cannot stay present, tell when I am getting overwhelmed, and stop and settle, that is the limit. Once enough ordinary-trigger regulation and body awareness are there, Brainspotting can go deeper without overwhelming you.

That is why it interests me for diffuse developmental trauma, pre-verbal or body-held material, trauma that has not responded well to purely cognitive approaches, and those emotional knots where the body seems to know more than the story. You do not necessarily need to know exactly what you are targeting before the session starts moving. Afterward, I would not automatically turn the rest of the day into another processing marathon. If spontaneous shaking comes, let it stay gentle; if the body still wants some discharge, gentle [Shaking Qigong](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) is one option. If you feel floaty or far away, walking meditation may be better. Restorative, gentle yin, or somatic yoga can help when what you want is rest, stretching, toning, or reconnection, and EFT can help if emotional activation is still high.

# EMDR

EMDR makes more sense to me when there is a specific memory, trauma, flashback, or trigger network you can actually point to. It has a clearer target and more structured recall procedures. Brainspotting can be much more exploratory.

Accidents, assault memories, military trauma, distinct flashbacks, or residual triggers left after deeper somatic work are natural examples. EMDR can be highly effective. A stable person working with one clear event may be ready quite early; I am more cautious about starting there when the trauma is diffuse and developmental, mainly bodily or pre-verbal, relational, or the nervous system is still unstable. That is where Brainspotting may fit better for me.

Afterward I would keep things almost boring: walk, eat healthy food, hydrate, sleep early if you are tired, do restorative yoga, journal only if it grounds you, maybe use a little EFT if activation is still there.

I also would not casually stack Brainspotting, EMDR, intense shaking, and deep yin on the same day unless you already know your system handles that much and you have skilled support. More processing can simply be more processing; sometimes the missing piece is integration.

# Light CBT / Narrative Integration

Trying to reframe a danger while the body still experiences the threat as present often does not get very far. Once the body is less trapped there, cognitive work has something to work with.

Then I can revisit old beliefs, build a more coherent life story, separate past danger from present reality, clarify values and boundaries, and reclaim agency. That can feed back into [inner-child reparenting](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/), identity and differentiation, and eventually things like forgiveness—when forgiveness is actually relevant—and life direction.

# How I Judge Whether Any of This Is Helping

I care less about whether a session feels dramatic than whether the trigger has actually lost some of its power. By “restimulation” I mean what happens when I remember the event later: does the same charge come back into the body and mind, or is it weaker?

While I was working on this article I was still upset about an argument with my housemate. I had been washing her moldy clothes for her, one of the dresses got discolored, and she yelled at me about it. I was still stewing over how she treated me.

So I did a quick bit of self-Brainspotting. I moved my finger and eyes slowly around while thinking about the event. When my gaze went farther to either side I noticed some *pīti*—in Buddhist language, a kind of tingling bliss—as I moved into more peripheral vision. I would not turn that into the simple claim that peripheral vision activates the parasympathetic nervous system; the research is more complicated than that.

Subjectively, I could go from the more settled/*pīti* side to the distressed side from moment to moment and kind of move the bliss into the distress while I was thinking about the event. Afterward I felt like the event held less negative energy, or less blocking energy, in my mind.

The useful test was not whether that moment felt interesting. If an hour later I was still stewing over how she treated me, then whatever shifted was temporary. If I could remember the event later and still be less restimulated by it, that was much more convincing. A single EMDR session is generally not enough either; the point is still to keep checking what survives beyond the session. The same principle applies to bigger interventions: check again that night, the next morning, and over the next two or three days. Look at sleep, irritability, dissociation, pain, compulsive processing, relationships, and ordinary functioning. A calm ending can still have been too much.

I do not think the size of the research literature settles which modality is right for me either. A thin literature does not prove a method useless, and a large literature does not tell me which therapy my own nervous system needs first. Published research, common clinical use, my own experience, community reports, traditional or energetic explanations, and proposed mechanisms are all information, but they are not interchangeable. A method helping does not by itself prove the explanation for why it helped.

When shaking, qigong, breathwork, or somatic communities make huge claims about releasing decades of trauma, awakening kundalini, clearing stored emotion, or rapidly healing the entire nervous system, I treat those as hypotheses and community signal and watch what changes in actual life. I start from first principles, pay attention to people with patterns similar to mine, change one thing at a time when possible, and keep asking the unglamorous question: am I actually doing better? I go slowly enough to see what the practice actually did; intensity by itself does not answer that.

# Optional High-Intensity Practices: Sky Hypnosis and Vagal Blitz

[Sky Hypnosis](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/try-this-10-second-sky-hypnosis-bodymind) and [Vagal Blitz](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/vagal-blitz-breathwork-gives-best) are optional high-intensity state-shift and release practices. Stable people may find them useful before doing any of the other therapies; they are not prerequisites for healing.

Sky Hypnosis can make you lightheaded. Vagal Blitz is a lying-down-only practice with cardiovascular and mental-health cautions. Use the complete guide, start with the least intense version that gives you a useful shift, and stop when balance, orientation, or recovery becomes unreliable. Do not chase fainting or loss of consciousness.

A huge release, a lot of bliss, or an altered-state shift can feel like healing. I use the same test as above: when I remember the event later, am I actually less restimulated—and am I more able to handle pain, boundaries, conflict, and ordinary life? If the practice is mainly helping me avoid grief, conflict, practical problems, or deeper relational healing, that tells me something too. Even a genuinely useful release does not automatically mean I am ready for deep memory work. The linked guides mix my experience with community reports; any proposed physiological mechanism is a separate question.
