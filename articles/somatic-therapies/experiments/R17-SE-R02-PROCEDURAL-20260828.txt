Introduction

Physical trauma reactions can continue after the danger is over, even when you know you're safe.

With complex or developmental trauma, going straight for the deepest memory can be overwhelming. Instead of diving off the deep end heroically, take it easy, one step at a time. As Buddhist nun Pema Chodron says, "feather touch" is all it takes to gently open up.  Peter Levine, the founder of Somatic Experiencing, calls this gentle work around the edges, "pendulation."

In inner-child therapy, you can know exactly what the younger part needs and still get pulled so far into the child-state that there isn't enough adult perspective left to give it.

Somatic Experiencing

Somatic Experiencing is probably the first thing on this list that I would look at when even brushing the edge of the trauma is too much. You don't have to explain the whole story first.

Say the first thing you notice is a tight place in your stomach. You look around the room, notice what changes, and see if your body wants to pull away, push, brace, or do nothing at all. That is already something to work with. If the activation gets too big, back off; if it does not, give it a little time and see what happens next.

The small-dose part is titration. Moving between the difficult sensation and something steadier is pendulation. Some people spend three to six months building this capacity, and severe developmental trauma may take longer. Someone who is already stable and has one clear target may be ready for trauma-focused work much sooner.
