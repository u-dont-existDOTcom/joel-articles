# Job 2: Keep the Pressure Low Between Deeper Sessions

A good therapy session can only do so much if the activation just builds back up during the rest of the week. So Job 2 is the ordinary-life question: what can I do between deeper sessions so I am not accumulating pressure faster than I can process it?

## EFT / Tapping

EFT is very nice for this kind of regulation. Part of it is just distraction: when you are triggered, you are giving your hands and attention something else to do instead of only getting dragged along by the activation. You can also literally activate different parts of the brain by tapping different parts, so it is interesting to notice how you feel as you move through the tapping points. And it is a little bit of a massage.

Actually, I mentioned earlier that shaving my head and massaging it works really well for me. Sometimes I feel like that works even better than EFT.

The practical advantage is that EFT is quick enough to use while life is happening: conflict, a shame spiral, social fear, abandonment panic, freeze, anger, or an intrusive fragment of memory that suddenly has your whole body involved. It can fit before a difficult conversation, after getting triggered, or before or after a therapy session when the mind is looping and the body is obviously in the loop too. That is also why it can bridge body-based and cognitive work. I just would not confuse lowering the pressure with doing all the deeper work, especially with severe trauma.

## [Shaking Qigong](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) / Shaking Medicine

My friend Louka is why I am linking this particular class. He had tried many different healing and somatic modalities, including basic TRE. TRE itself did nothing for him, and nothing helped him much until he got into this class, which costs $10 per month on Skool.

The class is much more than just TRE.

By `blockage` I mean what energy is stuck in you. I often have the kind of natural shaking that animals have, rather than noticing much benefit from intentional therapeutic techniques. Especially on psychedelics, it is like my body knows exactly which part to shake and how. So it is not just `shaking = mental health`. When the right thing is shaken the right way, things loosen up as they should and the energy flows.

TRE is a system they are trying to teach en masse, so they had to distill the method down to what seems to work most of the time for most people. Obviously, if you could get more angles at it, you would get more chance of success—and less chance of being called an expert. `Just try lots of stuff till it works` is how my expertise runs on this.

I did not try the Shaking Qigong class, but I have done qigong and it was very helpful in moving energy, so I can understand how the two would naturally go together. I can also see how it might be more of a middle ground between fully predictable TRE structure and totally unstructured shaking. That probably suits more people who feel like they need some guidance but are not able to benefit from standard TRE. It is also a social thing, so for many people it works better than individual practice when they see others getting results.

For Job 1 I mean mild, exploratory shaking. Here I mean something you might actually use as a regular discharge practice, maybe 10–45 minutes, for chronic freeze, non-verbal emotional processing, stress accumulating between therapy sessions, or leftover activation after deeper work. The limit is still the same: you need to be able to stop, orient, and settle afterward.

Why it helps is a separate question. Rhythmic movement could reduce muscular armoring and chronic tension, increase awareness of bodily sensation, possibly shift the system out of fight-or-flight, and perhaps let interrupted defensive impulses finish. Qigong describes what is happening in different language—removing stagnation, increasing qi circulation, dissolving stored emotional charge, and reconnecting breath, body, and movement. I would not treat one explanation as proof of the other.

What I would not do is turn shaking into a catharsis contest, treat it as a standalone treatment for severe PTSD or the primary trauma-processing modality, or use it instead of skilled help when somebody is highly dissociative or unstable.

## The Discharge → Settle Stack

One useful sequence is:

1. [**Shaking Qigong**](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) **/ TRE** — discharge excess activation.
2. **Restorative or gentle yin yoga** — let the system soften, settle, and rebalance energy flow.
3. **EFT tapping** — re-establish emotional calm and cognitive orientation.

Yin can help with the settling part, but keep it gentle, propped, and non-coercive. Long passive holds can get too evocative in trauma work, and especially early on I would rather use shorter holds than ambitious five-minute shapes. Nobody should be encouraging you to endure sensation for the sake of “release.”

Yoga is not really confined to this point on the map anyway. It can show up early for body awareness and agency, after shaking because you need to settle, or after deeper processing because stretching and reconnection feel good.

# Job 3: When the Trauma Is More Body Than Story

Sometimes there is no clean memory to target. What shows up first may be a sensation, impulse, emotion, image, or fragments that are hard to put into words. That is what I mean here by *subcortical* processing—not that one precise brain region contains the trauma, but that the material is showing up through the body before there is a clean verbal story.

## Brainspotting

I used my finger to direct my gaze slowly around the field of vision to see how different memories felt, or what memories came up at different angles. That was basically how I figured out very quickly that I could do a simple version myself.

I had heard the old claim that looking up-left means remembering and up-right means thinking or constructing something. I checked it, and that fixed map does not hold up well. But there is a real connection between eye position, eye movement, memory, and processing; it is just not a simple lookup table.

I like Brainspotting because it makes it easy to do something like edging. I did not know if that was the right word. What I mean is going in and out of the uncomfortable spot and kind of feather-touching the boundary of it rather than jumping straight into the deep end. `Titration` and `pendulation` are the established somatic terms that are closest to what I mean. It feels more effective to me. I also thought I had seen studies showing that gradual work was more effective generally; the check I did here was narrower—graduated exposure reduced peak distress, which is not the same as proving it is always more effective.

And I give Brainspotting so many more points because it is democratic like EFT. You do not need any expensive expert to do it for you, although you can get one too, and it is available at any moment. Formal Brainspotting adds therapist attunement to the gaze position, focused attention, and bodily awareness.

Being able to experiment with a simple version yourself does not mean diving into the deepest trauma alone. The limiting question is still whether you can stay present, regulate ordinary triggers, notice when the dose is becoming too much, stop, and settle afterward. You do not need to build that capacity through any particular checklist of therapies; once enough of it is there, Brainspotting can go deeper without overwhelming you.

That is also why Brainspotting interests me for diffuse developmental trauma, pre-verbal or body-held material, trauma that has not responded well to purely cognitive approaches, and those emotional knots where the body seems to know more than the story. You do not necessarily need to know exactly what you are targeting before the session starts moving. For further reading, see [D’Antoni et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8835026/), a comparative study involving EMDR, Brainspotting, and related methods.

I would not turn the rest of the day into another processing marathon. If spontaneous shaking comes, let it happen gently; if the body still wants discharge, use gentle [**Shaking Qigong**](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/). If you feel floaty or far away, walking meditation may be better. Restorative, gentle yin, or somatic yoga can help if what you want is rest, stretching, toning, or reconnection, and EFT can help if emotional activation is still high.

# Job 4: When There Is a Memory You Can Actually Point To

## EMDR

EMDR is more targeted toward specific memories or traumas, with recall procedures and a clearer thing you are working on. Brainspotting you can feel out, and it does not require knowing exactly what you are targeting. That is the main difference in my mind.

That makes a discrete traumatic event, flashback, or specific trigger network a natural EMDR target: accidents, assault memories, military trauma, distinct flashbacks, or residual triggers left after deeper somatic work. EMDR can be highly effective. A stable person working with one clear event may be ready quite early; I am more cautious about starting there when the trauma is diffuse and developmental, mainly bodily or pre-verbal, relational, or the nervous system is still unstable. That is where Brainspotting may fit better.

Afterward I would keep things almost boring: walk, eat healthy food, hydrate, sleep early if you are tired, do restorative yoga, journal only if it grounds you, maybe use a little EFT if activation is still there.

I also would not casually stack Brainspotting, EMDR, intense shaking, and deep yin on the same day unless you already know your system handles that much and you have skilled support. More processing can simply be more processing; sometimes the missing piece is integration.

## Neurological De-Armoring Support

This is important to me because it connects with my other articles. I have seen in friends that these therapies often did not do anything until their physical neuroinflammation was addressed, with things like low-dose naltrexone, phosphatidylserine, DMSO, and so on.

Also in my own experience, I cannot do any real healing work when I feel like I have toxins in my gut. Ozonated zeolite helps that a lot. Ozonated charcoal water helps a lot when I am surrounded by chaotic energy fields, whether EMF or auric.

That is why I keep the physical de-armoring material next to memory work rather than treating it as a separate wellness topic. Sleep, substance use, medication effects, pain, nutrition, general physiological stability, fear learning, inflammation, dissociation, glutamate/GABA balance, and autonomic tone can all affect how much therapy you can tolerate and what reconsolidation feels like. I have separate guides on [sleep](https://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/) and [neurological de-armoring](http://dearmor.u-dont-exist.com/) for that side of it.

# Job 5: Make Sense of What Changed

## Light CBT / Narrative Integration

Trying to reframe a danger while the body still experiences the threat as present often does not get very far. Once the body is less trapped there, cognitive work has something to work with.

Then I can revisit old beliefs, build a more coherent life story, separate past danger from present reality, clarify values and boundaries, and reclaim agency. That can feed back into [inner-child reparenting](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/), identity and differentiation, and eventually things like forgiveness—when forgiveness is actually relevant—and life direction.

# How I Judge Whether Any of This Is Helping

Here is a very ordinary example of what I mean by `restimulation`. While I was working on this article I was still upset about an argument with my housemate. I had been washing her moldy clothes for her, one of the dresses got discolored, and she yelled at me about it. I was still stewing over how she treated me.

So I did a quick bit of self-Brainspotting. I moved my finger and eyes slowly around while thinking about the event. When my gaze went farther to either side I noticed some *pīti*—in Buddhist language, a kind of tingling bliss—as I moved into more peripheral vision. I had assumed this was simply `peripheral vision activates the parasympathetic nervous system`, but the research is not that clean.

Subjectively, I could go from the more settled/*pīti* side to the distressed side from moment to moment and kind of move the bliss into the distress while I was thinking about the event. Afterward I felt like the event held less negative energy, or less blocking energy, in my mind. If one hour later I am still stewing over how she treated me, then perhaps the Brainspotting was more temporary, but I should try again because in general one treatment of EMDR is also not enough. If I can remember the event and still be less restimulated by it, then it worked more durably.

For a bigger intervention, do not judge the dose only by how you feel at the end of the session. Check again that night, the next morning, and over the next two or three days: sleep, irritability, dissociation, pain, compulsive processing, relationships, and ordinary functioning. A calm ending can still have been too much.

I do not think the size of the research literature settles this for me. A thin literature does not prove a method useless, and a large one does not tell me which therapy my own nervous system needs first. What matters is keeping the kinds of evidence straight: published research, common clinical use, my own experience, community reports, and qigong or energetic explanations are not interchangeable. When shaking, qigong, breathwork, or somatic communities make huge claims about releasing decades of trauma, awakening kundalini, clearing stored emotion, or rapidly healing the entire nervous system, I treat those as hypotheses and community signal and watch what changes in actual life. I start from first principles, pay attention to people with patterns similar to mine, change one thing at a time when possible, and keep asking the unglamorous question: am I actually doing better? Go slowly enough to see what the practice actually did. Intensity is not the same thing as healing.

# Optional High-Intensity Practices: Sky Hypnosis and Vagal Blitz

[Sky Hypnosis](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/try-this-10-second-sky-hypnosis-bodymind) and [Vagal Blitz](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/vagal-blitz-breathwork-gives-best) do not actually have to wait until the end of the map; stable people may find them useful before doing any of the other therapies. I put them here because they are optional high-intensity state-shift and release practices, not ordinary regulation exercises and definitely not prerequisites for healing.

Sky Hypnosis can make you lightheaded. Vagal Blitz is a lying-down-only practice with cardiovascular and mental-health cautions. Use the complete guide, start with the least intense version that gives you a useful shift, and stop when balance, orientation, or recovery becomes unreliable. Do not chase fainting or loss of consciousness.

The psychological trap is that a huge release, a lot of bliss, or an altered-state shift can feel like healing while the same trauma is still running your relationships and behavior. My simplest test is still restimulation: what happens when I remember the event later? Am I actually less activated by it? I also care whether I can meet pain, set boundaries, deal with conflict, and participate in life rather than using the practice to avoid grief, conflict, practical problems, or deeper relational healing. And even a huge release does not automatically mean you are ready for deep memory work; I would still check orientation, ability to stop, sleep, physical recovery, and whether I am actually more willing to approach the real issue. The benefits I describe in the linked guides include my experience and community signal; the proposed physiological mechanisms are a separate question.
