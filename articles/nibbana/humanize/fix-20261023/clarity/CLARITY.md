# Nibbāna: what passes Pangram but is hard to understand (2026-10-23)

Joel's question, 14:14 UTC: "is there any other stuff in the nibbana guide that's hard to understand but still passing pangram?"

Yes: **29 passages**, 12 of them hard enough that most readers wouldn't get them on a first read. They cluster in two kinds of place: the sutta-heavy paragraphs, where several citations and a conclusion share one sentence, and the theory sections (light flows, "established" and "unestablished", the physics parallels), where a coined term carries the step that the reader needs spelled out.

How they were found: three fresh readers, each given a third of the working copy (your editor HTML of 22 October with every fix so far, your 14:14 fix included) and your two own examples of what counts (SN 22.22 before and after your rewrite, "hard to understand due to the compression", and the percentage paragraph you rewrote today). Each flagged what a smart general reader would have to reread. I then checked every flag against the whole article, since each reader saw only its third: 7 flags were things explained in another part, and are listed at the end as not counted or as small snags.

Each passage has a plainer version that keeps every claim, name, number and citation. These are suggestions; nothing is changed in the working copy. Where a version spells out a step of your reasoning that the passage leaves out, it says "check", because that step is a guess at what you meant. The question of who rewrites them is on [your questions page](https://github.com/u-dont-existDOTcom/joel-articles/blob/claude/community-joel-1018/articles/nibbana/humanize/OWNER-QUESTIONS.md) (N3).

## The hardest: most readers wouldn't get these

### 1. Then I Found The Map Past Oneness

> MN 137 tells practitioners to use unification to relinquish diversity, then non-identification to relinquish unity. In MN 121, at every depth of unification the meditator audits the remaining disturbance and finds “just this much – that which depends on oneness.” Unity itself is the last stress.

**What's hard:** "relinquish diversity", "non-identification" and "audits the remaining disturbance" are abstract and unexplained, "unification", "unity" and "oneness" look like three different things, and two sutta claims plus his conclusion are crammed together with no step showing why unity is "the last stress".

**Plainer (the reader's):** MN 137 gives a two-step recipe. First you use unification (gathering the mind into one) to let go of diversity (the mind spread across lots of different things). Then you use non-identification (not taking anything to be “me”) to let go of unity too. In MN 121, at every depth of unification, the meditator checks what disturbance is still left, and what they find is “just this much – that which depends on oneness.” Put together, that says unity itself is the last stress.

### 2. What “Already Perfect” Means

> Because we don’t now “exist” as the natural laws themselves, but rather as a fabricated abstraction from them, pain, unsatisfactoriness, and injustice still hurt and still call for a response. But once we can experientially touch ultimate reality and realize the path out of the illusory cycle, those problems don’t hold the same ultimate psychological power over us.

**What's hard:** “Exist as the natural laws” versus “a fabricated abstraction from them” is abstract with no example, the “because” link to pain still hurting isn’t spelled out, “ultimate reality” and “illusory cycle” are undefined, and the next line (“Plato’s cave, but more imminent”) adds another unexplained comparison.

**Plainer (the reader's):** Everything is happening according to natural laws, but we don’t live as those laws themselves. We live as a made-up abstraction built out of them, the unified sense of self. Because that’s what we are right now, pain, unsatisfactoriness, and injustice still hurt and still call for a response. But once you can touch ultimate reality (that already-perfect nature) for yourself, and see the path out of the illusory cycle of birth, death, joy, and suffering called saṃsāra, those problems stop having the same ultimate psychological power over you.

**Check:** the reader glossed "ultimate reality" and "the illusory cycle" from elsewhere in the article; check them

### 3. Free Will Vs Conditions

> Every time someone claims to experience pure awareness (sat chit ananda – being, consciousness, bliss), they can still describe how it feels. So that non-duality is false.

**What's hard:** The jump from “they can still describe how it feels” to “so that non-duality is false” is missing its middle step (if it can be described as felt, there are still two things, not one), and the sentence before it (“awares apart from what it awares”) is hard to parse.

**Plainer (the reader's):** Every time someone claims to experience pure awareness (sat chit ananda: being, consciousness, bliss), they can still tell you what it feels like. But if it can be described as something felt, then there’s still a feeler and a felt, so it isn’t really one undivided thing. That’s why I say that kind of non-duality is false.

**Check:** the middle step ("still a feeler and a felt") is the reader's guess at your reasoning; check it

### 4. My Nibbāna Taxonomy

> It also shows the limit: a living arahant still has preferences and experiences pleasure and pain, but won’t be reborn again. Thus, in this article, saṃsāric means this constructed, dukkha-(suffering/unsatisfactoriness)-capable functioning itself, not only the craving that produces another birth.

**What's hard:** “It” and “the limit” point back to Iti 44 without saying the limit of what, and “Thus” jumps to a new definition of saṃsāric with the reasoning (an arahant is done with rebirth but still has the functioning) left out.

**Plainer (the reader's):** Iti 44 also shows the limit of nibbāna while you’re alive: a living arahant still has preferences and still feels pleasure and pain, but won’t be reborn again. So there’s no more rebirth ahead, yet the constructed functioning that can feel pleasure and pain is still running. That’s why, in this article, “saṃsāric” means that constructed functioning itself, the part that’s capable of dukkha (suffering or unsatisfactoriness), and not only the craving that produces another birth.

### 5. Awareness es

> Without the plural, Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu can begin correctly in The Mind Like Fire Unbound and The Wings to Awakening by saying the mind fabricates the experienced cosmos, including time and the present, then leave behind one copy-pasted singular line: “consciousness without feature, without end, luminous all around.” He can’t imagine parallel flows as nibbāna because the suttas say nibbāna lacks “differentiation.”

**What's hard:** One long sentence stacks a plural fix, two book titles, a claim about what he says and a “copy-pasted” singular line, and never says why the singular is the problem (it reads as one consciousness, against his many flows).

**Plainer (the reader's):** That missing plural matters. Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu opens correctly in The Mind Like Fire Unbound and The Wings to Awakening, saying the mind fabricates the experienced cosmos, including time and the present. But then he still leaves in one copy-pasted line in the singular, “consciousness without feature, without end, luminous all around,” which makes it sound like one single consciousness. He can’t imagine parallel flows as nibbāna, because the suttas say nibbāna lacks “differentiation.”

### 6. The Theory of Light Flows As Nibbāna

> It’s saṃsāra when the uncompounded light flows are established as one temporal, material body-world. It’s nibbāna when that establishment fully unweaves.

**What's hard:** "saṃsāra" is never defined here; "uncompounded light flows", "established" and "unweaves" are coined terms stacked in two sentences (and "unweaving" and "light flows" are not defined anywhere in this part); "Exactly" before it does not show how Nāgārjuna's line leads here.

**Plainer (the reader's):** Exactly: saṃsāra (the round of ordinary, rebirth-bound existence) and nibbāna are the same light flows, which aren't built from parts, seen two ways. It's saṃsāra when those flows get set up as one world with a body in it that lasts through time and is made of matter. It's nibbāna when that setting-up comes completely undone (what I call unweaving).

**Check:** "the same light flows ... seen two ways" is the reader's summary of Nāgārjuna's line; check it

### 7. Light Language Like Chantress Seba Never Seen Before!

> Nothing finds footing because no compounded body-world has been established—the same structural reason there is nowhere for suffering to land. Light can be perceived as the material world, but it isn’t an object in the world.

**What's hard:** "Nothing finds footing", "the same structural reason" and "nowhere for suffering to land" lean on a "no footing" image the reader was never shown in this part; "compounded body-world" is jargon; the last sentence is a bold claim with no example.

**Plainer (mine (the reader's, changed)):** Nothing finds footing, because no assembled body-and-world has been set up for anything to stand on, and that’s the same reason suffering has nowhere to land. Light can be perceived as the material world, but it isn’t one of the objects in that world.

### 8. But isn’t nibbāna asaṅkhata , or “unconditioned”?

> Across AN 3.47, where saṅkhata things arise, disappear, and decay while remaining; SN 22.79, on the manufacturing of experience; and the decay and assembly metaphors in DN 16 and SN 5.10, saṅkhata means “compounded,” “assembled,” or “fabricated,” not abstractly “conditioned.”

**What's hard:** The actual claim (saṅkhata means "put together", not "conditioned") sits at the end of a sentence that opens with four citations; "while remaining", "manufacturing of experience" and "abstractly conditioned" are unexplained; the reader is never told why the translation matters.

**Plainer (mine (the reader's, changed)):** The word saṅkhata means “compounded,” “assembled,” or “fabricated,” not “conditioned” in some abstract sense, and you can see that in how the suttas use it: in AN 3.47, saṅkhata things arise, disappear, and decay while they’re still around; SN 22.79 is about how experience gets manufactured; and DN 16 and SN 5.10 use images of decay and assembly.

### 9. Light is unestablished, with no time, no reference point, like nibbāna

> Quantum physics likewise says a photon’s state isn’t established as one definite outcome until measured. Saṃsāra is the unestablished becoming established as a definite relational world.

**What's hard:** "established" and "unestablished" carry three meanings in a row (the suttas' unestablished consciousness, a photon's undecided state, the body-world), and the second sentence uses "the unestablished" as a noun the reader cannot picture; "relational world" is unexplained.

**Plainer (the reader's):** Quantum physics has a similar idea: until a photon is measured, its state isn't pinned down to one definite outcome. Saṃsāra is like that pinning-down: something with no fixed state (what I'm calling unestablished) becomes fixed as one definite world, where everything exists only in relation to everything else.

**Check:** "where everything exists only in relation to everything else" is the reader's gloss of "relational world"; check it

### 10. Light is unestablished, with no time, no reference point, like nibbāna

> Physics can’t demonstrate from outside what meditation can disclose within: light sees itself. Give light a mirror through which to see its own beauty, and reflective consciousness begins establishing a mind, which leads us to the Greek myth of Narcissus, who died from starvation due to unceasingly staring at his beautiful reflection in a pond.

**What's hard:** "light sees itself" jumps from physics to awareness with no bridge; "a mirror" and "reflective consciousness begins establishing a mind" are abstract and unexplained; the Narcissus link (why a mind forming is like staring at a reflection) is never stated.

**Plainer (the reader's):** Physics can't show this from the outside, but meditation can show it from the inside: light sees itself. Suppose light gets something like a mirror, a way of looking at its own beauty. Then consciousness starts turning back on itself, and that turning-back is what begins to set up a mind (a "me" doing the watching). It's a bit like the Greek myth of Narcissus, who stared at his own beautiful reflection in a pond so constantly that he starved to death.

**Check:** "(a me doing the watching)" is the reader's gloss of "establishing a mind"; check it

### 11. Come and See for Yourself!

> If so, untangled reality isn’t a faraway Garden of Eden requiring an arm & a leg’s worth of past-life hypnotherapy. As Roaring Silence says, it appears in blessed moments as “the sparkling-through of our beginningless enlightenment.”

**What's hard:** "If so" rests on two suttas about the mind changing fast, but the step from "the mind changes fast" to "untangled reality is close" is never spelled out; "it" could be the mind or untangled reality; "Roaring Silence" is not introduced.

**Plainer (the reader's):** If the mind really is changing that fast, then it never stays fixed in place, so untangled reality isn't some faraway Garden of Eden that costs an arm and a leg's worth of past-life hypnotherapy to reach. ### 12. Come and See for Yourself!

**What's hard:** "the same shape" has no referent; "permanence is a pattern made from impermanence" is abstract with no example; "flows tangling, getting heavy, and releasing" calls back to ideas this part never mentions.

**Plainer (the reader's):** Physics has the same pattern: things that look permanent are patterns built out of nature's constant change, like a mind that arises as one thing and ceases as another.
