## 1
QUOTE: The DN 11/MN 49 passage is also the closest suttic description I know of nibbāna as light flows, provided you read its “awareness” in the plural.
HARD: "The DN 11/MN 49 passage" points to a passage that is never quoted or described, and "read its 'awareness' in the plural" is not explained (what changes if it is plural?); "suttic" and "light flows" are also undefined in this part.
SCORE: 3
PLAIN: There's also a passage that shows up in both DN 11 and MN 49, and it's the closest description in the suttas that I know of to nibbāna as light flows. It only works that way if you read the word "awareness" in it as plural, meaning many separate flows of awareness rather than one single awareness.

## 2
QUOTE: Nothing finds footing because no compounded body-world has been established—the same structural reason there is nowhere for suffering to land. Light can be perceived as the material world, but it isn’t an object in the world.
HARD: "Nothing finds footing", "the same structural reason" and "nowhere for suffering to land" lean on a "no footing" image the reader was never shown in this part; "compounded body-world" is jargon; the last sentence is a bold claim with no example.
SCORE: 3
PLAIN: Nothing finds footing, because there is no assembled body-and-world for anything to stand on. That is also why suffering has nowhere to land: with no assembled body-world, there is nothing for it to attach to. Light can be perceived as the material world, but it isn't one of the objects inside that world; it's what the world is made of.

## 3
QUOTE: It’s saṃsāra when the uncompounded light flows are established as one temporal, material body-world. It’s nibbāna when that establishment fully unweaves.
HARD: "saṃsāra" is never defined here; "uncompounded light flows", "established" and "unweaves" are coined terms stacked in two sentences (and "unweaving" and "light flows" are not defined anywhere in this part); "Exactly" before it does not show how Nāgārjuna's line leads here.
SCORE: 3
PLAIN: Exactly: saṃsāra (the round of ordinary, rebirth-bound existence) and nibbāna are the same light flows, which aren't built from parts, seen two ways. It's saṃsāra when those flows get set up as one world with a body in it that lasts through time and is made of matter. It's nibbāna when that setting-up comes completely undone (what I call unweaving).

## 4
QUOTE: Physics can’t demonstrate from outside what meditation can disclose within: light sees itself. Give light a mirror through which to see its own beauty, and reflective consciousness begins establishing a mind, which leads us to the Greek myth of Narcissus, who died from starvation due to unceasingly staring at his beautiful reflection in a pond.
HARD: "light sees itself" jumps from physics to awareness with no bridge; "a mirror" and "reflective consciousness begins establishing a mind" are abstract and unexplained; the Narcissus link (why a mind forming is like staring at a reflection) is never stated.
SCORE: 3
PLAIN: Physics can't show this from the outside, but meditation can show it from the inside: light sees itself. Suppose light gets something like a mirror, a way of looking at its own beauty. Then consciousness starts turning back on itself, and that turning-back is what begins to set up a mind (a "me" doing the watching). It's a bit like the Greek myth of Narcissus, who stared at his own beautiful reflection in a pond so constantly that he starved to death.

## 5
QUOTE: Quantum physics likewise says a photon’s state isn’t established as one definite outcome until measured. Saṃsāra is the unestablished becoming established as a definite relational world.
HARD: "established" and "unestablished" carry three meanings in a row (the suttas' unestablished consciousness, a photon's undecided state, the body-world), and the second sentence uses "the unestablished" as a noun the reader cannot picture; "relational world" is unexplained.
SCORE: 3
PLAIN: Quantum physics has a similar idea: until a photon is measured, its state isn't pinned down to one definite outcome. Saṃsāra is like that pinning-down: something with no fixed state (what I'm calling unestablished) becomes fixed as one definite world, where everything exists only in relation to everything else.

## 6
QUOTE: Across AN 3.47, where saṅkhata things arise, disappear, and decay while remaining; SN 22.79, on the manufacturing of experience; and the decay and assembly metaphors in DN 16 and SN 5.10, saṅkhata means “compounded,” “assembled,” or “fabricated,” not abstractly “conditioned.”
HARD: The actual claim (saṅkhata means "put together", not "conditioned") sits at the end of a sentence that opens with four citations; "while remaining", "manufacturing of experience" and "abstractly conditioned" are unexplained; the reader is never told why the translation matters.
SCORE: 3
PLAIN: The word saṅkhata means "put together" (compounded, assembled, fabricated), not "conditioned" in some abstract sense, and you can see that in how the suttas use it. AN 3.47 says saṅkhata things arise, disappear, and decay, all while they are still lasting. SN 22.79 is about how experience gets manufactured. DN 16 and SN 5.10 use images of things decaying and being assembled. That is why I think translating asaṅkhata as "unconditioned" is a mistranslation.

## 7
QUOTE: If so, untangled reality isn’t a faraway Garden of Eden requiring an arm & a leg’s worth of past-life hypnotherapy. As Roaring Silence says, it appears in blessed moments as “the sparkling-through of our beginningless enlightenment.”
HARD: "If so" rests on two suttas about the mind changing fast, but the step from "the mind changes fast" to "untangled reality is close" is never spelled out; "it" could be the mind or untangled reality; "Roaring Silence" is not introduced.
SCORE: 3
PLAIN: If the mind really is changing that fast, then it never stays fixed in place, so untangled reality isn't some faraway Garden of Eden that costs an arm and a leg's worth of past-life hypnotherapy to reach. As Roaring Silence says, untangled reality shows up in blessed moments as "the sparkling-through of our beginningless enlightenment."

## 8
QUOTE: Physics shows the same shape: permanence is a pattern made from nature’s impermanence, reminiscent of a mind arising as one thing and ceasing as another, and of flows tangling, getting heavy, and releasing.
HARD: "the same shape" has no referent; "permanence is a pattern made from impermanence" is abstract with no example; "flows tangling, getting heavy, and releasing" calls back to ideas this part never mentions.
SCORE: 3
PLAIN: Physics has the same pattern: things that look permanent are patterns built out of nature's constant change, like a mind that arises as one thing and ceases as another. It also fits the picture of flows tangling together, getting heavy (binding is literally heavy, as I said earlier in the essay), and then releasing.

## 9
QUOTE: Light is energy in the electromagnetic field spectrum, and energy can’t be created or destroyed. Individual photons are emitted and absorbed, but a photon has no parts to decay or disassemble. So light isn’t fabricated, even if a factory made your light bulbs.
HARD: It reads as a contradiction on a first pass (energy "can't be created" yet photons are "emitted and absorbed"); "fabricated" means "put together from parts" in the previous section, but the "So" does not restate that; the factory joke makes the logic harder to follow.
SCORE: 2
PLAIN: Light is energy in the electromagnetic field spectrum, and energy can't be created or destroyed. Individual photons do get emitted and absorbed, but a photon isn't made of smaller parts, so it has nothing that could fall apart or be taken apart. So light isn't fabricated (put together from parts), even if a factory made your light bulbs.

## 10
QUOTE: Based on the suttas, it is clear that one fruition event is what establishes stream entry. If a later event is needed before that realization happens, the earlier event was, by definition, not stream-entry fruition.
HARD: "that realization" and "the earlier event" refer to events not named in this part, so the reader cannot tell which two events are being compared; "by definition" makes the argument feel circular; the point of the argument is not stated.
SCORE: 2
PLAIN: Based on the suttas, a single event, the nibbāna fruition, is what establishes stream entry, and the realization it brings (identity view seen through, the Dhamma understood, doubt gone) comes with it. So if someone still needed a second, later event before that realization arrived, then the first event wasn't stream-entry fruition, by the very meaning of the term.

## 11
QUOTE: Soh’s false 24/7-bliss claim isn’t the standard: AN 11.12 says a noble disciple can reach ease through recollection, while in MN 75 the Buddha says he felt pain at what ordinary people perceived as sensual pleasure. Enlightenment isn’t a simple bliss switch.
HARD: "Soh's false 24/7-bliss claim" refers to a claim never stated; two suttas are packed into one sentence and nothing says how "ease through recollection" and "pain at sensual pleasure" show that enlightenment is not constant bliss.
SCORE: 2
PLAIN: The standard isn't the non-stop, 24/7 bliss that Soh claimed, which is false. In AN 11.12, a noble disciple can reach ease through recollection, so ease is something a disciple reaches through practice, not a permanent state they're stuck in. In MN 75, the Buddha says that what ordinary people perceive as sensual pleasure, he felt as pain. So enlightenment isn't a simple bliss switch.

## 12
QUOTE: But without the ammunition of desire, prior practice, and readiness to end rebirth and attachment to self-view, pulling the trigger (experiencing Nibbana) only makes a cool sound.
HARD: The gun metaphor starts two sentences earlier and "the trigger" is not named as nibbāna until this parenthesis; "ammunition of desire" is odd, since desire is usually the problem; "the old view" in the next sentence is never specified.
SCORE: 2
PLAIN: But if you don't have the preparation (wanting to be done with rebirth and with clinging to a self-view, real practice, and readiness), experiencing nibbāna does nothing lasting. It's like pulling a trigger with no bullets in the gun: you get a click, a cool experience that gets filed away and doesn't permanently overwrite your old view.
