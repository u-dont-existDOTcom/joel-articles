## What “Nibbāna” Is Actually Saying
The word nibbāna originally described a fire going out. My old nibbāna book wrongly treated “unweaving” as its direct etymology. “Extinguishment” is more common, though it sounds like the nihilism pole the Buddha avoided in his soteriology.
In the fifth-century Visuddhimagga, Buddhaghosa instead derives nibbāna from vāna, “binding” or “fastening”: craving laces one becoming to the next. Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu considers that less academically strict but more illuminating and thus translates nibbāna as “unbinding.” A fire depends on fuel (upādāna, also clinging or sustenance) and is freed when its binding to fuel ends. Like them, I find the interpretive meaning more useful here than strict etymology.
[an image]
The suttas already speak in tangles, knots, and nets. SN 1.23 describes a “tangle within” and “tangle without,” then says the arahants’ (fully enlightened ones’) tangle is disentangled. Dhammapada 90 says the liberated person abandoned every gantha (knot, tie, or bond), while Dhammapada 180 calls craving jālinī (net-like, ensnaring). Unbinding and disentangling are native ways to describe liberation from the binding of the sense streams.
What came apart in my room experience was the fastening together of my sensory flows. Normally that fastening makes them into one settled consciousness, one self, one world, and the kind of existence that gets renewed in another birth. In the room it looked like everything was being unwoven. And since the suttas keep talking in tangles, knots, and nets, I don’t think that weaving picture is an accident.
## Nibbāna Has Volume & Tuning Knobs
In my experience, nibbānic unweaving varies by degree and scope.
This distinction isn’t laid out explicitly in the suttas. AN 11.12 says a noble disciple can reach ease and joy through recollecting the Buddha, Dhamma, & Saṅgha – the community of realized disciples – or recollecting ethical conduct, generosity, or qualities shared with heavenly beings. On top of the recollection route to ease and joy in AN 11.12, I find there can also be a physical loosening of unification, meaning the binding that normally holds all my senses together as one solid experience does so a bit less. I'll use percentages as a playful way of talking about how much of that binding has loosened up. Around 1%, a person can still do everything fine. Around 5%, they can hardly do anything. (And partial untying goes beyond that range too.)
There’s also a strange middle ground where I can have several experiences going on in parallel, like being two different solid birds flying at once. Each of those is still bound up inside itself, so it isn’t nibbāna (nibbāna means unbinding), but it hints at it. Ud 5.10 has something similar: monastics split into a thousand bodies to clean rooms faster, until someone touches one of them and they merge back into one.
The percentage language applies only to partial unweavings. The fingers and room events were both full (100%) unweavings. The difference between those two was one was limited to the body, one was limited to the room, but neither one had anything that could identify or suffer, and both were the full infinite bliss and release (unlike the 2 birds version which was lacking there).
But even with partial unweaving, the bliss and peace can be extremely profound compared to ordinary experience. At something I would jokingly call 5% nibbāna, I become so peaceful and blissful that doing anything feels ridiculous. Once, at a Workaway farm in Malaysia, I remained around that level for four days. I had no interest in tasks, plans, or becoming an impressive person on a farm. Talking and interacting with people gradually brought the ordinary unification back toward 0-1%, just like in Ud 5.10.
[an image]
Most of the time, I function through a mostly unified body, one present scene, and some degree of witnessing – just like other people, mostly. Independently perfect finger-flows and a cloud of independent colors aren’t so useful for using a shovel. But what’s always different from before is I no longer take this unified sense of self to be more than a bad approximation of reality. Nature assembled this unified self for survival purposes, so life could reproduce, and so forth. I get it now.
You can get a small taste of the loosening through the guided meditation later in this article.
## My Nibbāna Taxonomy
My old “hard nibbāna / soft nibbāna” language was also my taxonomy. But here’s a better breakdown of how I’d explain it now:
- Partial nibbāna / partial untying: some unification loosens while enough of the body-world remains for recognition, desire, memory, meaning… basically, life in the normal sense is still possible, but not as tight.
- Full unweaving nibbāna: the unification that creates one functioning and suffering-capable body-world disappears completely.
- Body-limited nibbāna: a full unweaving limited by what the living body has made available, as in the fingers experience.
- Body-uncontained nibbāna: a full unweaving no longer organized around the body’s viewpoint or sensory aperture, as in the room experience.
- Reassembly: the ordinary body, scene, memory, and ability to function return.
- Parinibbāna: the same full unweaving without reassembly, i.e. without rebirth, because the body is dead. The living breakthrough to nibbāna is what makes the final direction knowable. Iti 44 agrees on the difference between nibbāna & parinibbāna.
Iti 44 also shows the limit of nibbāna while you're alive: a living arahant still has preferences and still feels pleasure and pain, although they won't be reborn again, since there's no more craving for existence driving that. So there's no more rebirth ahead, that's a relief. Yet except while in the nibbāna meditation itself, their constructed functioning that can feel pleasure and pain is still running. So in this article, "saṃsāric" refers to that constructed functioning itself, since it is what's capable of dukkha (suffering or unsatisfactoriness).
## Nibbāna is a meditative state and attainment
Many Buddhists love to tell me nibbāna isn’t an experience, just an achievement or attainment – the loss of the fetters (Buddhist deadly sins). The suttas disagree, tho.
AN 9.47 is literally titled the Sandiṭṭhikanibbāna Sutta, “Directly Visible Nibbāna.” It answers “How is nibbāna directly visible?” with a step-by-step meditative progression: when perception and feeling cease and the taints are destroyed by wisdom, that is directly visible nibbāna “in the definitive sense.”
Arahants also described nibbāna samādhis. In AN 10.7, Sāriputta perceives no earth, water, or normal object – only “the cessation of becoming is nibbāna.” AN 10.6 describes the same state as perceiving “this is peaceful, this is sublime… nibbāna” and nothing else, with parallels in AN 11.7 and AN 3.32. (Bhikkhu Brahmāli reads these as an idea about nibbāna; his paper is here, and I address it in the research report.)
Contact language reinforces that these are experiences. In AN 6.46, monastics touch the “deathless element” with the body. AN 9.43-45 has the “body-witness” touch formless liberations and defines one “liberated both ways” by cessation-contact plus destroyed taints. In AN 9.34, Sāriputta treats the absence of feeling in cessation as pleasure. Ud 8.1 calls nibbāna tadāyatanaṃ (“that sphere”), using the same word as the meditative spheres. You don’t “touch” and “enter” a sphere that’s merely a philosophical abstraction.
## How Contact Builds a World
My model of a coherent “world” starts with the finger and room experiences. Blissful light flows were present on their own, with no witness behind them. These appearances or awareness flows meet locally like a MeetUp group – although MeetUp banned me for trying to organize a study group about this, which may be why I’m a Substack author instead of the next Jim Jones.
[an image]
I call those MeetUps "initial contact." That's my own practical term, and it's a bit broader than the Pāli word phassa, which just means contact. These local meetings can help organize a sense door (like the eye which lets light in) and its sense-consciousness (the eye-consciousness, meaning the seeing of the light that comes in).
SN 12.44 gives the canonical sequence: eye and forms condition eye-consciousness; their meeting is contact. Contact begets feeling, then craving and clinging; clinging to experiences becomes clinging to life, leading to becoming and birth. There is “the whole mass of suffering,” which the sutta calls “the origination of the world” – sutta-speak for saṃsāra.
## Awarenesses
I use awareness to mean bare, non-reflective appearance: blue is just blue. Consciousness is awareness reflecting back on itself: “I’m blue!” Pāli doesn’t give me separate nouns for this distinction, but DN 11 and MN 49 describe viññāṇaṃ anidassanaṃ anantaṃ sabbato pabhaṃ – non-manifesting awareness, without end and luminous all around – where the elements and mind-and-body (nāmarūpa, often mistranslated as name-and-form) find no footing. This is distinguished from ordinary sense-consciousness (also viññāṇaṃ) which is established based on perception of something.
I think this non-manifesting awareness should have been “nonmanifesting awarenesses,” strange as that sounds. That missing plural matters. Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu opens correctly in The Mind Like Fire Unbound and The Wings to Awakening, saying the mind fabricates the experienced cosmos, including time and the present. But then he still leaves in one copy-pasted line in the singular, “consciousness without feature, without end, luminous all around,” which makes it sound like one single consciousness. He can’t imagine parallel flows as nibbāna, because the suttas say nibbāna lacks “differentiation.”
I agree that nobody separates or counts anything in nibbāna. But the light flows weren’t one featureless consciousness either; they were nonidentical by nature, not because a mind was there distinguishing them. The fuller comparison is in the research report. This one-versus-many question ultimately separates Buddhism from prior teachings and new-age spirituality, and it matters because unification gives suffering somewhere to land.

## Ignorance is the root
So why would already-perfect flows ever stay tied up in an ever-expanding ball of suffering yarn? I used to think multisensory binding was the root of saṃsāra, based on the Amaro translation of the Bāhiya Sutta, even though that wasn’t my actual experiential insight. Now I realize there’s nothing wrong with flows tangling up and untangling again, which happens naturally and randomly. The trouble is only when they keep tangling. “Wise” (or lucky) flows tangle and release freely, a bit like how fields, or the energy strings in String Theory, bind into quantum particles, which then annihilate each other back into stringy energy flows.
[an image]
And the level of suffering seems to run on the same dial as the tangling. My first-jhāna atoms were a lot more blissful than ordinary life, so calling first jhāna blissful isn’t wrong. But compared with nibbāna, it was euphoria: energized, ecstatic happiness from unification, not the peaceful bliss of release. People usually use bliss for both, and English sutta translations often do too. The Pāli is more precise: the first two jhānas have both pīti—rapture—and sukha—bliss, pleasure, or ease; in the third, the rapture fades while the quieter pleasure remains; in the fourth, even pleasure and pain are gone, leaving equanimity.
If flows forget the beauty of peaceful release, they keep tangling for more euphoric binding. DN 27 says that at some point we emerge as rapture-fed, self-luminous beings, but craving for more leads us to start munching on that yummy dark earth and become solid ourselves. Then we start hoarding food and having sex and eventually develop existentialist philosophy, and now you have anti-natalists blaming their parents for bringing them into the world!
The Buddha names ignorance (avijjā) as the root of the nonstop tangling, the first link of dependent origination. SN 12.2 and MN 9 define it as not knowing dukkha, its origin in craving, its ending, or the path to its ending. My own plain-language addition: ignorance is not knowing that the ecstatic euphoria you get from merging multiple experiences into one doesn't count as real happiness when compared with the bliss and peace of release.
I’m not gonna make the free-love analogy you might expect, because that gets messy. 😘
Money works the same way. Children are happy with none, then get a little power in their pocket and like it. When it runs out, they don’t return to happiness; they want more. They become adults working like hell for more money while growing less happy. The happiness never came from money, but they didn’t know its source, so they chase the wrong condition harder. Nobody chooses that ignorance; we all logically start from it.
[an image]
My dad once told me he was hitchhiking, and had only a nickel left in his pocket, which was causing him terrible anxiety. Someone asked for money and he gave away his last nickel, and then he suddenly felt immensely free!
The Buddha said sensual pleasure itself works this way. In MN 75 he compares it to a leper finding relief by scorching his itchy lesions over hot coals, and feeling as pleasure what a healed man would feel as burning.
## Why the Tangle Is Heavy
In SN 22.22 the five aggregates that make up the unified body-mind are the burden of suffering, and craving is the process that keeps picking that burden up, so that you walk around unaware of the boulder on your back even as you keep making the payments to the Boulder Bank to keep it there (and thank God you can afford to!?). The tangle of the aggregates is what makes them heavy, which sounds counterintuitive since we don't imagine the whole can be more than the sum of its parts in that way. But "binding" is literally heavy in physics too: quarks supply less than 1% of a proton’s mass, roughly 99% comes from gluon-binding energy. Matter weighs what its binding weighs. Light weighs nothing.

When we assemble the parts, we call them a “chariot.” In the same way, where nature heaps together the aggregates, we call them “a being.” The sense of oneness is experientially real because you really do experience one world, but it’s the oneness of an assembly. The “me” it seems to happen to is just an overpaid and underly humble assembly spokesperson.
## The Capacity for Suffering
The main question for me and for the Buddha is whether the capacity for suffering (dukkha) remains. Taking a look at studies on automatic action and split-brain patients, it’s clear that people can function without feeling an inner controller, yet they still feel pain. Anguish requires more reflective consciousness than pain. SN 36.6 describes two darts, where the ordinary person struck by pain also sorrows, grieves, etc, while the fully liberated person (arahant) feels the bodily dart alone when not in nibbāna.
But don’t get it twisted: zombie-like functioning on autopilot, split-brain dissociation, and even a true no-self realization can all occur without making anyone an arahant. Pain without anguish is a symptom of enlightenment, not the definition. An arahant is defined by the complete release of even the subtlest unconscious belief in or craving for unified existence – the full loss of the fetters – which is what ends rebirth.
[an image]
You don’t need several sense streams working together to suffer; one stream is enough. Say all that was left was a single channel of pleasure and pain. Pain would still be in there, because pain is like a plane: it can only land if there’s somewhere solid to land, and even one channel gives it that. During full unweaving, no assembled system remains that could suffer, lack, desire, gain, lose, recognize, or do anything. Supreme bliss is what seems to be left when the capacity for suffering is released (even though you might assume neutrality would be left): as Sāriputta put it in AN 9.34, “nibbāna is pleasant precisely where nothing is felt.”
