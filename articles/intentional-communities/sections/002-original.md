# The New Age May Dawn Suddenly

I recently put my father’s 1988 film, [*Where’s Utopia?*](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/wheres-utopia-click-read-more-my), online for the first time. He made it in 1988 after spending years visiting communal experiments around the United States and asking a question that never really left him: how could human beings live together without reproducing the same loneliness, domination, and stupidity we were supposedly escaping?

He had already spent 17 years in the [Bruderhof](https://www.bruderhof.com/), a Christian community that doesn’t use money internally. He had no personal money there: income went into the common purse and the community provided what members needed. So when I talk later about moving beyond internal money, I’m not proposing something nobody has ever lived. My father spent most of two decades doing it. They also accepted him as a Jewish atheist, making him a remarkably long-running doctrinal exception. In meditation there, he came to his own understanding that “God is the Universe, and the Universe is Love.”

He might have stayed, but the U.S. antiwar movement during Vietnam needed somebody who knew how to set up printing presses. He eventually founded the Aquarian Research Foundation—not a commune, but a nonprofit devoted to researching how the planet could have a positive future and publishing what it found. His conclusion was simple and enormous: human beings need small tribal communities again, because [Civilization Is A Hoax,](https://government-scam.blogspot.com/2020/06/everything-you-were-taught-is-lie.html) as I blogged about at the beginning of the Covid plandemic.

That’s where the ***escuelita*** in my title comes in. *Escuelita* means “little school.” I’m borrowing the word from the Zapatistas, whose [*Escuelita*](https://schoolsforchiapas.org/advances/schools/la-escuelita/) brought outsiders into their communities to learn autonomy by living it. The healing communities I’m proposing should do the same: people live there, heal, learn the relational and practical methods, and some eventually leave to start the next one. Otherwise, we’ve built a lovely refuge for whoever got there first.

That’s how I spent much of my childhood being carried through [Twin Oaks](https://twinoaks.org/), [Acorn](https://www.ic.org/directory/acorn-community-farm/), [East Wind](http://eastwind.org/), [Padanaram](https://www.facebook.com/p/Padanaram-100064853281734/), [Zendik](https://highlandscurrent.org/2017/05/09/leaving-zendik-farm/), and many other experiments. I was usually the visitor’s kid, close enough to see how people lived but never quite part of the tribe. At the time this mostly meant boredom. In retrospect, it gave me a strangely useful comparison sample.

From 1999 to 2001, my family actually lived with the [Love Israel family](https://uwapress.uw.edu/book/9780295988856/the-love-israel-family/) in Washington while escaping [Y2K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_2000_problem), that brief historical period when otherwise reasonable adults prepared for civilization to be defeated by a two-digit date. We lived in a yurt. There were wild berries of every kind, and I loved being in nature. I was also lonely, bored, irritated by the cult programming, and missing my friends in Philadelphia.

I wanted to befriend the children there, but they treated me as an outsider. My mom never liked the place. The leader was also named Love Israel, and to me he seemed like the fakest person I’d ever met, complete with the expected God complex.

We spent a lot of time on our knees in the community garden growing food they could sell in town. The money was opaque and seemed to travel mostly upward toward Love. Meanwhile everybody sang, “Here in the garden, there’s nothing left to do but eat sleep and keep clean.” Again and again. 🌱

My father eventually gave the community his airplane. They sold it instead of using it. He felt tricked, began exposing what was happening, and then we were asked to leave.

[East Wind](http://eastwind.org/) felt almost like the opposite social experience. It had roughly eighty people when I visited: large enough to have real variety, small enough that friendship still felt possible. I was happy there. I also loved the mixed-sex communal showers, and I still wonder whether those survived. These are the questions history books neglect.

East Wind still had a quieter power problem. The woman directing the nut-butter factory brought in much of the community’s income, which seemed to make her unusually difficult to question. The group had a warm social life, distributed labor, and no obvious guru, but the factory’s importance still affected who could be challenged.

[Acorn](https://www.ic.org/directory/acorn-community-farm/) had something like eight people when I visited. There were no children there. I couldn’t tell whether it was a commune I might join or a group of friends who happened to own beautiful land and build lovely things together. Joining an established group of eight friends felt less like applying to a community than asking whether their group chat had an opening.

[Twin Oaks](https://twinoaks.org/) was much larger—perhaps two or three hundred people at the time—and felt almost institutional. It was friendly and interesting, but I didn’t feel a tribe. It felt more like a tiny hippie town with an unusually complicated labor-credit system.

Across nearly all of these places, the same absence kept appearing: no communal therapy practice. Human pain still entered the group, of course. It simply got translated into politics.

I remember weekly meetings run under Robert’s Rules of Order, with enormous agendas, little room for what anyone actually needed to discuss, and the persistent thought, “I’d rather be sleeping.” Sometimes people solved that conflict by snoring through the meeting.

[Zendik](https://highlandscurrent.org/2017/05/09/leaving-zendik-farm/) went in the other direction. They held a brutal community meeting that I’m still recovering from 35 years later. 🤣 Five-year-old me was put in the center and told I was bad because I hadn’t volunteered to help adults with whatever jobs they were doing. Apparently kindergarten had already failed to teach me labor consciousness.

The secular communities often buried hurt under procedure. The spiritual communities often put one person in charge of everybody’s inner life. Most of the rest of this article is my attempt to describe the missing third option.
