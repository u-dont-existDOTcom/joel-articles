# Jealousy Can Capture Monogamy or Free Love

Any community that lasts will eventually be reorganized by attraction and pair-bonding. When two exes live in the same city, they can move to different sides of the city. When they're part of a thirty-person commune, they may still be in the same kitchen the next morning, working in the same team, attending the same meetings, sharing childcare and the same friends. A couple's jealousy or breakup becomes a problem for the entire community, because it will change the loyalties, the sleeping arrangements, the labor, the kids, the alliances, and sometimes who's leaving.

The discussion generally goes straight to "should people be monogamous or free-love?" but as I discuss in my Romance Guide, this is one level too late in the conversation. The community problem is that, in close quarters, jealousy gets magnified, and jealousy can capture either system.

Rigid monogamy can turn fear and possession into a moral law. If people are doing free love, but aren't doing the inner pl/ork, they can just end up with a larger spreadsheet to distribute their fear over. It's not like calling something by a certain name dissolves the childhood panic, the comparison, the terror of abandonment, or the desire to control another person.

I don't think it should be up to the community to decide for everyone what form relationships will take. People can make different agreements with each other, in pairs, threes, fours, whatever. They can change those agreements as they wish. And they can ask for support when those agreements bring up old material.

Whatever forms people choose, I do think that housing, security for kids, membership and access to common necessities should not be contingent on a person staying in some agreement with the people they're romantically involved with.

ZEGG and Tamera have spent decades experimenting with relationships inside community. Their answers are not a set of commandments that everyone must follow. But they are an example of how a community can acknowledge the erotic aspects of life. It is not necessary for 20 adults to pretend that they've become neutral colleagues just because the by-laws say so.

The non-negotiable for me is that jealousy not be allowed to become the hidden governor. This doesn't mean it can't come up. It needs to be able to be talked about and felt in the body. It needs to be explored without humiliation. And it needs to be let go of gradually, and not allowed to turn into punishment, or into community rules.

There are some ways the group can help, and basic boundaries the group can enforce, without deciding what type of relationships people have. Even so, there will still be disagreements about this. That's more realistic than saying it's all been resolved.

(heading) Children Need Freedom Inside Real Protection

[29] [the image's caption:] African kids enjoying their childhood!

[30] Kids also need their own territory, places where they can create their own society, invent games and work out ordinary disputes, without always being under the management of adults.

[31] I got a taste of that at a Unitarian youth camp, and it's still one of the freest memories I have. [32] ZEGG's *Kinderhaus* works from a similar intuition. [33] Adults take care of safety on the outside, but kids still get a world of their own.

[34] This could be badly misunderstood, so the safeguards need to be explicit. [35] The adults still have to pay attention to what goes on in a children's territory. [36] Older kids can't be allowed to quietly boss the younger ones around there, and secrecy can't be treated as independence.

[37] I also reject the suburban panic that treats every warm relationship between a child and an unrelated adult as suspicious. [38] It's good for kids to have a whole bunch of grownups who know them and enjoy them, who would notice when something changes, and who would act when something's wrong. [39] Fear like that can destroy that gift and still not prevent abuse.

[40] There are several parts to this protection:

[41] Give children real agency. [42] When kids say no, it counts. [43] No grownup has a right to their affection, physical contact, obedience outside of legitimate safety needs, or private access to them.

[44] Create a no-secrets rule. [45] No grownup has a right to ask a kid to keep a secret from their mom or another trusted adult, whether it's about a gift, a game, a touch, a conversation or a relationship. [46] A surprise isn't a secret, because at the end of a certain date it has to be told. [47] But secrets meant to isolate a kid aren't allowed.

[48] Teach truthfulness before there's a crisis. [49] Make sure the kids understand the importance of telling the truth, the power of their words, and how what they say will be taken seriously. [50] Don't let the teaching turn into interrogation, punishment when they're not sure, or a reason to brush off what they report. [51] A scared or confused kid may get mixed up telling it and still need protection.

[52] Hear the child even when the accused is someone people love. [53] Communities fail here, because the adult may be charismatic, useful, rich, spiritually important, or everybody's friend. [54] Decide on the response process ahead of time, before anyone's face is attached to it. [55] Keep everyone safe first, right away, then make sure the accusation is investigated competently and fairly. [56] Nobody's status buys silence.

[57] Make sure kids have relationships and a reporting route outside the community. [58] Grandparents, cousins, local friends, teachers and other trusted adults give them perspective on their life, and someone to talk to. [59] Make sure there's at least one trusted adult, advocate or reporting route they can reach without going through the community's leadership. [60] In 1984, Vermont authorities seized 112 kids in the Island Pond raid, but a court rejected the state's blanket request and returned them. [61] State intervention can fail spectacularly, and enclosing kids completely in a community can fail too. [62] Kids need more than one world.

[63] I also looked through the research corpus for a good, complete record of an actual case, a child in an intentional community who remained seriously dangerous to other people: the conduct, the assessment, the intervention, the review, and what happened later. [64] I couldn't find one. [65] It's possible that records are private, or that cases were referred elsewhere. [66] Whatever the reason, I can't claim communities already know how to handle the hardest cases on their own. [67] In those cases, the child, the family, and the people at risk all need access to competent help and review, outside the control of the home community.

(heading) The Door Must Open Outward at Adulthood

[68] Amish young people decide as adults whether to join the church, and roughly 85 percent or more of them end up joining. [69] Pop culture makes *Rumspringa* seem like a wilder, more universal teenage free-for-all than it is, but it still matters that they're baptized as adults, because then belonging is a commitment instead of a fact imposed at birth.

[70] If a community's kids never leave, it has no way of knowing whether they'd come back freely. [71] So let them study, travel and make friends, encounter other ways of living, and disagree with the founders without being treated as traitors.

[72] Every childhood narrows the future. [73] Most ordinary parents aren't going to give their kid a bunch of money at 18 and say, "Go see the world." [74] They'll try to get them ready for some next step that can actually work. [75] A poor or money-free community may have even less cash to offer, and food, medical care and basic survival may rightly come first.

[76] The realistic standard isn't unlimited freedom. [77] It's not making it artificially impossible for people to leave. [78] Kids who grow up without using money especially need to learn enough about how the money world outside operates that they aren't helpless in it: jobs, rent, contracts, banks, scams, ID, getting around, and how to ask for help.

[79] Kids also need transferable skills and education, relationships with people outside the community, access to their own records and documents, and at least one plausible next step outside the community (school, an apprenticeship, work, family, another community). [80] It would be great if the community could also provide resources to smooth the transition, like temporary housing, help with travel, tools or a small stash, but even if it can't, it can at least not make things worse by punishing people for leaving, or by holding back knowledge or records a young adult would need to function outside the community. [81] No upbringing can provide perfect freedom, and the point isn't to erase path dependence. [82] It's to not turn belonging into a trap.

[83] It's possible that some of them will leave permanently, and others will start their own communities with different values rather than wait decades to inherit the original one. [84] And parents may experience that as a loss, even when it's the movement evolving.

(heading) Discuss Child-Rearing Values Before Children Are Caught Between Them

[85] Couples who live together, share the same culture and even the same bed still disagree about screens, vaccinations, schooling, religion, food, discipline and medical care. [86] And that's just two people. [87] Now think of twenty adults, all with different childhoods.

[88] Once a kid is already living inside the disagreement, goodwill doesn't answer those questions. [89] The community has to share enough principles about raising kids to offer them coherence, and leave parents enough room to remain parents.
