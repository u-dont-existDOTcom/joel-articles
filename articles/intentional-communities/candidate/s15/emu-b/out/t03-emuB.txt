Lessons from the Black Tax

Hi again, well let's take a look at something which sometimes (maybe often) complicates things in some commies and may be new for westerners, it's a legacy of commies, let's call it the "Black Tax", it's the responsibility to provide for one's parents, siblings and other family members, paying school fees, medical fees and funerals etc. This is the logical result of being fed, raised, housed, cared for and protected by them. Why not share in return, it is the most natural thing to do and the best way to keep the organism viable.

The problem is that it can be soul destroying in today's world where most people are not sharing their lives, land and work etc. so it prevents people from working to create their own stable lives and independence and so where is the growth? How to move forward? Well this is a problem, it traps the givers and the receivers in a vicious circle. But it is the most ancestral thing in the world so it is deeply rooted in people, and it serves a vital function of keeping people alive. It's the same ethic in a different context of an individualistic money-based world.

It's one of the problems you need to deal with in any money-free commune because you'll always need a bit of money to deal with taxes, hospitals, visas, school fees, black tax, buying tools etc from the outside. Not in the sense of reintroducing individual salaries of course, but in terms of how to deal with it, how much you need in the common purse etc and also how to prevent its introduction by individuals from creating a hierarchy of status etc.

Another related problem is how to deal with people working outside the commune. The money part is only one aspect of it, another is that it's a different way of life, social, time, status, consumption etc. so it's something to deal with. In the first stage people work outside but must give a certain part to the commune but cannot buy their way out of doing their share of the work or use it to get more power in the political system etc and it's important that it doesn't take away all the best people and skills who should be working on the commune's own sources of livelihood instead, thus preventing the creation of a common purse.

In the Zapatista areas some communities used to try to prevent people from migrating to work elsewhere, now they instead discuss the conditions with those who want to leave, they can go but must meet certain conditions in relation to the community. Good move.

Another difference between westerners and africans is that most westerners need to create a tribe and communal life, they start off isolated, while many africans already have all that but see it disappearing, so it's often the reverse situation. And often the more urbanised africans who may be interested in intentional community are those who feel alienated and disconnected from both the urban and traditional cultures.

So as you can see it's a global issue and far from a "western lifestyle thing". And by the way, if you go and visit some of these places, it's good to remember that they are people not museums, they are living and working there, they are not there to entertain you, would you like people coming to your house and staring at you and your family? So be respectful, go with the desire to contribute and learn some of the language and don't expect anyone to provide you with spiritual services!

And lastly, it didn't cost a lot to get my residency papers here, french and wolof are more important than english, people are generally very nice, it is the land of "Teranga" which means hospitality, I'm even staying in a friend's family's house and they don't want rent! It's a nice place but also a watchful and socially conservative place, and some complications with bureaucracy etc.
