Lessons from the Black Tax

You remind me of the so-called 'Black Tax' that is a legacy of the old communal system- if you've got any money at all, you are expected to share it with your parents, siblings, to pay school fees, medical expenses, funerals etc. It's certainly understandable. If the people that fed, raised, housed and protected you expect you to share your wealth with them when you are able, that's how the organism would survive.

The problem is that this can be very soul destroying in today's world, because while many people no longer live in close enough proximity to share land, work, childminding, food etc. and feel part of a larger whole like this, they may still expect you to share your wealth. So it can be hard for people to make a life for themselves, to be independent or plan for the long term. How are people expected to 'get ahead'? And yet it leaves the relatives in a position of continued dependency. So the ancestral communal ethic continues, even in the individualistic cash economy, keeping people alive but gnawing at them.

It's one of the thorny boundary issues of living without money within a money-based society. In your commune, you will need money to pay taxes, for hospital bills, for visas, school fees, 'Black Tax', buying in tools etc. It doesn't mean reverting to individuals working for wages and bringing them into the commune, but it does mean someone has to do it! And the commune needs to discuss how much money it needs in common to pay for these things, and how it will prevent this from creating status differences within the commune.

Another aspect of working outside the commune is that it's not just the money that you're bringing into the commune. You're also bringing in a different way of organizing your time, different status differences, a different social world, different loyalties, different consumption habits etc. etc. In the first stage, where some people work outside and contribute an agreed amount to the commune, it's really important that they are not allowed to use this money to 'buy out' of communal work or to buy a greater say in the political system. It's also important that it doesn't suck all the energy and skills out of the people who might be building the communal livelihoods. Otherwise the commune will never develop a common purse and will remain a dormitory for the money economy.

Some Zapatista communities used to try to stop their members from emigrating. Now they talk to them and say that it's OK to go and work elsewhere but that there are certain things they need to do for the community. I like that.

This is interesting, since many westerners seem to be starting from a position of alienation and isolation and trying to create a tribe, while many Africans seem to be starting from a position of tribal/communal life which is breaking down. If an African does want to live communally and has moved to a city, they may be feeling alienated from both.

You're absolutely right that this is a global discussion and not just a Western lifestyle issue. There is a lot to learn from traditional communities, but you have to approach them with respect and an attitude that you're there to work and learn their language. You can't treat them as curiosities or expect them to provide you with a spiritual service! You wouldn't want people watching your family and poking their noses in all the time, would you?

It's really cheap and straightforward to get residency here. Most people speak French and Wolof, but even if you don't, you can get by- you just have to work harder! English isn't very useful here, sadly. And here the idea of 'Teranga' (hospitality) is definitely still alive. I'm living in a friend's family's house and they refuse to let me pay rent! But like everywhere, there are pockets of suspicion, social conservatism, bureaucracy etc. etc.
