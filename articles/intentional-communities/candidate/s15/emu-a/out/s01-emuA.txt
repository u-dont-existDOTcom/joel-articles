What This Looks Like From Sénégal

Living in Sénégal opened my eyes to the fact that there are communities in which "intentional community" is a nonsensical Western concept.

Sénégal is such a unique place that it's hard for me to know how to describe it. People are devout in a way I've never seen before, praying 5 times per day out in the streets in a really beautiful and soothing way. Sufism is alive and well here, not just in designated "spiritual" places, but it's in the music, in the pictures everywhere, in the street art, in the names, in the conversations.

And the humor! People are outside in the streets laughing for hours at a time. Sénégal is the most joking culture I've been exposed to. I really need to learn how to make jokes in Wolof, because a lot of the jokes are complicated. If I could just get Wolof up to graduate school level then maybe I'd be able to join in when a bunch of people are crying with laughter.

In villages and with my family here, things that we're trying to "re-invent" with intentional community are just done. Land, kids, housing, food, etc. etc. are all taken care of via extended family and the village.

Even in the city of Dakar, I see things like this spray painted on the side of buildings: "Tolerance! Openness! Union! Happiness! Love!" I've seen beautiful street murals in more "normal" places, but it's always been of the various Sufi leaders that surprises me that there is even spiritual graffiti! When I was younger I used to think that most graffiti was of people who couldn't draw a penis correctly.

But all is not well in paradise

For LGBTQ people, it can be a brutal place.

In March 2026 a new law came into effect that increased the penalty for consensual intimacy between people of the same sex to between 5-10 years in prison. The new law, Law No. 2026-08, amended the penal code article on homosexuality to criminalize "any public apology for homosexuality", and to criminalize "financing or any other form of support with the intention of instigating or facilitating the commission of" homosexuality. This is on top of the constant arrests, rejection by family, harassment, assault, etc. that are already endemic here. Some people have said that when homosexuals are arrested, they're actually safer in jail, because then the mob can't get at them.

So, this is a great reminder that even in a culture that is super spiritual and communal, it can still be cruel to those who violate its norms. Community isn't inherently good or bad - it just depends on the community.

The other downside to the generally forgiving social culture is that there are people who will take advantage of that. I believe everyone knows who is stealing our moringa pods in the morning, but no one wants to make a stink because they don't want to cause trouble. And these people could just plant moringa trees in their own yards, but why do that when they can steal ours every morning?😂

And everyone has sheep. Sheep are so cute!

It's almost like living in another world, but things are changing as the youth head for the cities and jobs and phones and imported foods and the wider world (and the perilous sea voyage to Europe for the men). As families break up and people become more individually oriented, the need for cash increases.

Lessons from the Black Tax

Speaking of which - Sénégal is ground zero for the "Black Tax", where if you make any money at all, you're responsible for your entire family. I used to cringe at this, but now I understand that it's a legacy of when everyone had to share because your family raised you and took care of you and protected you and fed you. If you managed to make it in the world, then you shared with everyone because everyone contributed to your success.

I can see how this would be soul destroying in today’s version of this arrangement where people are generally unable to build an independent life or long term stable situation while family members / clans / whatever find themselves even more dependent on the family member trying to make a living than they were before. It’s essentially the same system that used to be more honourable when there was more of a sharing of land and labour and childrearing etc but still applies even though it’s now operating in an individualistic cash economy.

Also as you say relevant to how a commune without money internally deals with the outside world. Taxes, hospitals, visas, school fees, family members, etc etc etc etc.

Another aspect is the effect on the commune of people working outside. Not just because of the money, but the other values, social environment, time schedule etc etc that the outside world imposes on people. Especially the way it encourages people to consume more. In the early stages of a commune, the people working outside should be expected to bring a certain amount of money into the common purse, but not allowed to buy themselves out of doing normal commune work, or to gain extra political power in the commune. And it shouldn’t be allowed to take over the energy and talent that should be going into building up the common purse.

I’ve heard that the Zapatistas used to try to stop their young people migrating out of their communities and into the wider world, but now they’ve given up trying and instead negotiate with them, setting out the terms under which they can work outside but still retain certain obligations to the commune.

Also I think whereas many westerners approach the idea of intentional community from the angle of wanting to cut themselves off from the outside world and create a tribe from scratch, a lot of africans are approaching it from the opposite direction, in that they already have a communal society which is gradually disappearing, and they’re trying to hold on to it. Of course if you’re an african living in a city and are interested in intentional community then in a way you are in a similar position to a westerner in being alienated from your traditional communal life style.

Finally I should add that I see this as a global issue, not just a western thing. Which is why I recommended going and living in a village where this way of life still exists, as long as you do it in a respectful manner and are prepared to make a contribution and learn the language etc, and are not expecting people to provide you with some kind of spiritual service.

PS if you do go to Senegal, the process for getting residency etc is very cheap and straightforward. And you need to learn French and Wolof more than English. And yes, the Senegalese ideal of ‘Teranga’ (hospitality) is very much alive. I’m living in a house belonging to a family of friends and they don’t want any rent. But of course it’s also a very socially conservative place, and like any country full of bureaucracy and other complications.
