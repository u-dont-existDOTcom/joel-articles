What This Looks Like From Sénégal

Here is some interesting food for thought for anyone interested in community as I’m currently living in Sénégal, and interestingly, if you bring up the idea of intentional community, people are like, “what? are you people not doing that in the West already??”

Sénégal is a very unique place. Coming here, the first thing that hits you is the prayer time. Sénégal is a Muslim country, and they follow the prayer times; five times a day, all over the country the call to devotion comes on and people stop what they’re doing and pray. It’s actually very soothing to listen to. The main sect here is Sufi, and the spirituality of the people is very interesting, reflected in their music, in their pictures, in their murals, in their naming practices, and in their conversations.

The next thing is that people are incredibly jovial and laugh a lot. All the time. It’s very common to go outside and hear people laughing for hours. It’s the most “joking” culture I’ve ever been in, and I’m still hoping to reach a level of Wolof that I can understand all the complicated jokes. (Most of my Wolof learning has been at the “I want coffee” level, whereas the jokes are more at the graduate school level).

Also, in general there’s still a strong sense of community, especially in the villages and amongst extended family. A lot of the stuff we’d want to do intentionally, they’re doing because it’s the normal thing to do. Land ownership, child care, housing, food, etc.

There is even “positive” graffiti in the capital city. This one is translated “Tolerance! Openness! Union! Happiness! Love!” - not to mention all the beautiful murals of the Sufi leaders. I had never seen spiritual graffiti before - it’s all been “f**k the police” or things that look like they’re supposed to be penises (but they’re terrible drawings so I don’t really see the appeal).

However, all is not well in paradise.

In particular, Sénégal is absolutely terrible for the LGBTQ community. In March 2026 they passed Law 2026-08, increasing the punishment for homosexuality to 5-10 years in prison. The actual law also makes it illegal to “make apology” for homosexuality in public (what does that even mean?) and makes it illegal to finance, etc. anything that can be perceived to promote homosexuality. I’m not an expert on the subject, but from what I’ve been able to find, homosexuals are often arrested, thrown out by their families, harassed, assaulted, and sometimes imprisoned actually helps because it protects them from the mob.

So, what I’m saying is that yes, you can have a very spiritual, communal, loving, fun, and vibrant society that is also very cruel and nasty to anyone who doesn’t fit in. Community is not always a good thing, it depends on the community.

There are other negatives to the generally “anything goes” social culture - namely, people don’t want to get anyone in trouble, even when they’re stealing your moringa pods every. single. morning. (seriously, why don’t they just plant their own trees?? there are moringa trees everywhere, and they’re so easy to plant. I don’t understand this 😂)

Also, people around here all raise sheep - even in the middle of the city. Including me! And they’re so cute:

But, like I said, all is not well in paradise. It’s like living on another planet, but these old ways are coming to an end. Young people are increasingly looking for jobs, heading to the cities, looking at their phones all the time and wanting imported food, and even making the dangerous journey across the sea to try and make it to Europe. The communal families are breaking apart, and people are increasingly needing to make money.

Lessons from the Black Tax

And yes, I should mention the infamous Black Tax that comes along with the communal living system, where if you happen to make any money at all, you’re responsible for taking care of your entire family. And while this is definitely a problem, it also comes from an ancient time where if anyone in your family had success, it was because of your family’s help in getting you to that point. No one was an individual, and the entire family needed to succeed together, so it made sense that if one person had success, they should share it.

It is soul destroying in our current form since everyone doesn’t share the same land, work, kids, food, etc, but the obligation persists even for those trying to get off the ground to achieve some independence and stability. It destroys the people it is supposed to help since they will always need that money and can’t escape the obligation, but it is a nice reminder that the ancestral way isn’t as dead as it may seem in our highly individualistic cash based society.

This is a variant of the problem of how to deal with the outside world in a money free commune. Taxes, hospitals, visas, school, family, etc are all denominated in money, so how does one decide who will deal with the outside world, how much money the commune will keep to deal with the outside world, and how to prevent the creation of a hierarchy based on who has the best access to the outside world and its money?

The other issue is people dealing with the outside world by working outside. This brings in a different schedule, hierarchy, social situation, consumption needs, etc. Basically in the first stage whoever is working outside needs to give some portion of their money to the commune, but the money can’t be used to buy their way out of work or to exert undue political influence. The other thing to be careful of is people working outside taking up too much time/energy that could be spent on developing commune based jobs so we can get to the common purse stage.

From what I’ve seen the Zapatistas have been working on this problem by negotiating arrangements with people who want to migrate rather than just attempting to prevent them from leaving like they did in the past.

The other thing to realize is while people in the west are attempting to isolate themselves and recreate tribal life, a lot of people (esp. in Africa) are seeing their communal life slowly destroyed by the outside world. This means that a lot of urban Africans are actually looking for ways to create intentional communities since they don’t feel they fit into either world.

So while I focus more on the west this is actually a worldwide issue. If you want to learn about how people are dealing with this just visit some of the remaining villages where this way of life persists, but be respectful and be willing to help out and learn the language. Don’t go there expecting to be served and don’t treat it like a tourist attraction.

Incidentally it was pretty cheap and easy to get residency here in Senegal but you will need to learn some French and Wolof to really make it easy. One of the nice things about Senegal is they still largely practice their ideal of Teranga (hospitality). I’m currently living in a friend’s family’s house and they won’t let me pay rent. Of course it’s not all fun and games there is plenty of social conservatism, bureaucracy, etc to deal with.
