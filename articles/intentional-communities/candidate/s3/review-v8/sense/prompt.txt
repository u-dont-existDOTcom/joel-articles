# Communes Are Hot Again 🔥

Suddenly the longing behind my father’s movie is everywhere again, and in 2023 [the U.S. Surgeon General](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf) even put out an advisory about it. Or about the loneliness under it, at least. The advisory said roughly half of American adults experience loneliness, and it treated social connection as a public-health issue.

Whether you want to call that loneliness an official “epidemic” or not, it’s obvious that a lot of people can’t tolerate modern life in isolation anymore. They may disagree about politics, food, God, sex, or whether shoes are oppressive, but they agree that something about the current arrangement is starvin’ them.

Check out the Google Trends for “[ecovillage](https://trends.google.com/explore?q=ecovillage&date=all&geo=Worldwide)” and “[intentional community](https://trends.google.com/explore?q=intentional%20community&date=all&geo=Worldwide)”. After years of decline, interest climbed sharply in the mid-2020s.

[an image]

I have no proof that the rise of AI caused this turn, but it’s hard to ignore the coincidence in timing. AI became serious around this time, and it started changing people’s jobs and relationships, and how they see the future. Suddenly, forming a village sounded like a good backup plan, not just a hippie utopian whim.

It’s poppin’ on Instagram too! 🔥 Chantress Seba—my favorite light-language love influencer—[posted an application](https://instagram.com/p/DVyl1a2lAuZ/) for people interested in starting a commune. I applied, although I don’t think she’s coming to Africa, where I'm at. When I saw the post, many thousands of people appeared to be interested. Imagine opening your inbox after casually inviting a million followers to co-found a village.

Later, when another influencer with a big following [floated the commune idea](https://instagram.com/p/DaLvZV2qIk2/), the comment section started to become a recruitment board. It was chaotic, but sincere, and it was revealing. There were people in the comments finding prospective members the way you’d usually find a used sofa.

### The Freeloader Problem

Some of the commenters weren’t trying to join. The oldest objection, that maybe most people don’t want to take on the responsibility that anarchism requires and communal property becomes “owned by everyone, cared for by nobody,” showed up almost right away.

[an image]

The commune comments became a miniature political-philosophy seminar, as Instagram comments sometimes do.

I'd say the comment above is more true of the big socialism experiments than small communes, but I still take it seriously, because some people really would rather let somebody else take responsibility. When everybody owns everything, it can get to where nobody knows who's supposed to do what, and the same three people end up doing all the maintenance while everybody else talks about freedom. And then unofficial leaders start gaining power. Partly that's because everyone keeps handing it to them.

Communal ownership entails folks taking on visible and named stewardship duties. It requires knowing what to do if someone is leeching off the group and how to deal with it. It requires developing healthier forms of relationships. No, taking away the idea of a boss does not make everyone less passive, selfish, steal less, or stop trying to act helpless.

People want to go back to the old way of living, but they have no idea why earlier communes failed or what to do when someone wants to own land, the group runs out of money, a baby is born or someone thinks they own something. They just know they are tired and alone and want to change it.

I am not currently recruiting anyone or founding a community, which makes it easier for me to write an article like this, since I’m not trying to get you onto my land. I do plan to buy land, and people may naturally end up living around me. All of the warnings in this article apply to me, too.

(heading) Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways

[1] Most communes of my acquaintance went wrong from the inside, in one of two ways.

(heading) The secular failure

[2] The secular communes had a solution for how to distribute property or labor, but when the deep waters of jealousy or childhood hurt or humiliation or attraction or resentment or grief came up, the group was without a common method for dealing with it. [3] The feelings just became agenda items.

[4] Meeting procedure isn’t going to solve every problem, no matter how sophisticated the system of labor and money and decision making may be in a community of the [Federation of Egalitarian Communities](https://www.egalitariancommunities.org/) or any other income-sharing community. [5] It can’t make it so that two people don’t hate each other. [6] It can only make it so that they take turns talking about it, and that whoever raises it has to find somebody who agrees with him or her before they get to talk about it.

[7] Eventually, when people are together long enough, politics will become a way of carrying emotional material that people can’t name. [8] There will be a fight about a schedule or committee or noise or food or some policy or other, but it won’t be about that.

(heading) The spiritual failure

[9] This comes about when the community realizes the importance of embracing a transformation from within, and then funnels it through a particular person, usually the founder, a teacher, a channel or some other special knower of how things really are.

[10] [The Farm](https://thefarmcommunity.com/), in Tennessee, at its largest about 1,500 people, had many beautiful values. [11] But in the early days, the community’s spirituality and social life was heavily influenced by Stephen Gaskin. [12] From what former members have shared, the community’s doctrine ran deep into members’ intimate decisions. [13] It’s one thing to talk about a universal consciousness. [14] It’s another to struggle to escape one man’s opinions.

[15] Both approaches fail for symmetric reasons. [16] Secular approaches fail to address the inner life (while distributing power), whereas spiritual approaches do address the inner life but concentrate power.

[17] The deeper problem is that someone carries authority earned in one domain into the others. [18] The founder who is a good teacher, the therapist who really helps people, the farmer or business manager that actually carries a lot of the economy. [19] The danger starts when one person has the ability to define the problem, control the information related to the problem, define what the solution is, and make it hard for people to leave or express disagreement.

[20] I want a way to work in depth that isn’t run by a guru, and a way of being in a community with distributed authority where people don’t come in pre-healed, that also has peer practice and enough play that it doesn’t become a permanent repair shop.
