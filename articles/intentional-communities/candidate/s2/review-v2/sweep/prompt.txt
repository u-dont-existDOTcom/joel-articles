The New Age May Dawn Suddenly

I just put a movie my father made in 1988 up on the Internet for the first time: *Where's Utopia?* [linked: https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/wheres-utopia-click-read-more-my] It was the result of years of work, traveling to all kinds of communes and communal experiments around the country, trying to answer a question that for him was primary: How do we live together without recreating the same loneliness, domination, and stupidity we were supposedly escaping?

He had already spent 17 years in the Bruderhof [linked: https://www.bruderhof.com/], a Christian community that doesn’t use money internally. He had no personal money there: income went into the common purse and the community provided what members needed. So when I talk later about moving beyond internal money, I’m not proposing something nobody has ever lived. My father spent most of two decades doing it. They also accepted him as a Jewish atheist, making him a remarkably long-running doctrinal exception. In meditation there, he came to his own understanding that “God is the Universe, and the Universe is Love.”

He might have stayed, but the U.S. antiwar movement during Vietnam needed somebody who knew how to set up printing presses. He eventually founded the Aquarian Research Foundation—not a commune, but a nonprofit devoted to researching how the planet could have a positive future and publishing what it found. His conclusion was simple and enormous: human beings need small tribal communities again, because Civilization Is A Hoax, [linked: https://government-scam.blogspot.com/2020/06/everything-you-were-taught-is-lie.html] as I blogged about at the beginning of the Covid plandemic.

That’s where the ***escuelita*** in my title comes in. *Escuelita* means “little school.” I’m borrowing the word from the Zapatistas in Mexico, whose *Escuelita* [linked: https://schoolsforchiapas.org/advances/schools/la-escuelita/] brought outsiders into their communities for a while to learn autonomy by living it. In the same way, people would live in the healing communities I’m proposing, to heal and to learn the relational and practical methods, so that some of them could eventually leave and set up another one somewhere else. Otherwise, we’ve built a lovely refuge for whoever got there first.

I spent much of my childhood being carried through Twin Oaks [linked: https://twinoaks.org/], Acorn [linked: https://www.ic.org/directory/acorn-community-farm/], East Wind [linked: http://eastwind.org/], Padanaram [linked: https://www.facebook.com/p/Padanaram-100064853281734/], Zendik [linked: https://highlandscurrent.org/2017/05/09/leaving-zendik-farm/], and many other communities. I was usually just a visitor’s kid, close enough to see how people lived but never quite part of the tribe. At the time that meant a lot of boredom, with some adventures mixed in, but those years gave me a great sample to compare against.

From 1999 to 2001, my family actually lived with the Love Israel family [linked: https://uwapress.uw.edu/book/9780295988856/the-love-israel-family/] in Washington while escaping Y2K [linked: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_2000_problem], that brief historical period when otherwise reasonable adults prepared for civilization to be defeated by a two-digit date. We lived in a yurt. There were wild berries of every kind, and I loved being in nature. I was also lonely, bored, irritated by the cult programming, and missing my friends in Philadelphia.

I wanted to befriend the children there, but they treated me as an outsider. My mom never liked the place. The leader was also named Love Israel, and to me he seemed like the fakest person I’d ever met, complete with the expected God complex.

We spent a lot of time on our knees in the community garden growing food they could sell in town. The money was opaque and seemed to travel mostly upward toward Love. Meanwhile everybody sang, “Here in the garden, there’s nothing left to do but eat sleep and keep clean.” Again and again. 🌱

My father eventually gave the community his airplane. They sold it instead of using it. He felt tricked, began exposing what was happening, and then we were asked to leave.

The East Wind [linked: http://eastwind.org/] commune was almost the opposite way. There were maybe eighty people when I was there, enough to have a variety of personalities, small enough to actually make friends. I was happy there. I especially liked showering in a communal shower with both sexes present. I wonder if that still goes on at East Wind? It's the sort of thing that doesn't make it into the history books.

Even at East Wind, though, there was a quieter power issue. They had a nut butter factory that brought in a lot of their income, which seemed to make the woman running it very hard to question. Otherwise I thought the community had a great social life, a nice way of distributing labor, and no obvious guru.

There were like eight people at Acorn [linked: https://www.ic.org/directory/acorn-community-farm/] when I was there, and no kids. I couldn't tell whether it was a commune I might join or a group of people who were already friends, and who happened to live on some really beautiful land and be good at building cool stuff. Joining would have felt less like applying to a commune than asking whether their group chat had an opening.

Twin Oaks [linked: https://twinoaks.org/] was much bigger, like two or three hundred people at the time, and almost institutional. It was friendly and interesting, but I didn't feel like I was in a tribe there. It was more like a tiny hippie town, but with a highly structured political system that didn't seem intuitive for hippies.

Looking back, almost none of these communities had a communal therapy practice. People brought the usual human pain and suffering with them, and it all got translated into politics.

Which meant weekly meetings, run according to Robert's Rules of Order, with enormous agendas, where most of the things you needed to talk about couldn't be talked about, and you generally felt like you'd rather be sleeping. (Some people, including me, solved this problem by just going to sleep in the meeting and openly snoring.)

Zendik [linked: https://highlandscurrent.org/2017/05/09/leaving-zendik-farm/] went in the other direction. They held a brutal community meeting that I’m still recovering from 35 years later. 🤣 Five-year-old me was put in the center and told I was bad because I hadn’t volunteered to help adults with whatever jobs they were doing. Apparently kindergarten had already failed to teach me labor consciousness.

The secular communities often buried hurt under procedure. The spiritual communities often put one person in charge of everybody’s inner life. Most of the rest of this article is my attempt to describe the missing third option.

Communes Are Hot Again 🔥

The longing that shaped my father’s film is suddenly in the air again. In 2023, the U.S. Surgeon General’s advisory [linked: https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf] reported that roughly half of American adults experience loneliness, and treated social connection as a public-health issue rather than a sentimental extra.

Whether you want to call that loneliness an “epidemic” or not, it’s obvious that a lot of people can’t tolerate modern life in isolation anymore. People may disagree about politics, food, God, sex, or whether shoes are oppressive, but they agree that something about the way things are now is starvin’ them.

If you look at Google Trends for “ecovillage [linked: https://trends.google.com/explore?q=ecovillage&date=all&geo=Worldwide]” and “intentional community [linked: https://trends.google.com/explore?q=intentional%20community&date=all&geo=Worldwide]”, you can see that interest drifted down for years, then bent upward sharply in the mid-2020s.

I have no proof that AI caused this turn, but it’s hard to ignore the timing. That’s around when AI stopped being a tech-news curiosity and started reorganizing people’s jobs, feeds, relationships, and expectations of the future. Suddenly “maybe we should form a village” sounded less like a 1972 leftover and more like a backup plan.

It’s poppin’ on Instagram too! 🔥 Chantress Seba—my favorite light-language love influencer—posted an application [linked: https://instagram.com/p/DVyl1a2lAuZ/] for people interested in starting a commune. Imagine opening your inbox after casually inviting a million followers to co-found a village.

Then another large influencer floated the commune idea [linked: https://instagram.com/p/DaLvZV2qIk2/], and the comments started turning into a recruitment board. People were finding future members under a post, the way you’d normally find a second-hand sofa.

The Freeloader Problem

Not everybody in the comments was trying to join. The oldest objection came up almost immediately: maybe most people don’t want the responsibility that anarchism requires, and communal property ends up “owned by everyone, cared for by nobody.”

image 5 [linked: https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!0l9H!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F966a39ab-153b-4d8f-852d-e96421f21f26_714x1228.jpeg]

Those comments became a little seminar on political philosophy, which you don’t normally find on Instagram.

While this objection is more applicable to large-scale experiments in socialism than to small communes, it deserves a serious answer anyway, since some people really would rather let somebody else take responsibility. Shared ownership can blur responsibility until the same three people maintain everything while everybody else discusses freedom. Informal leaders then gain power partly because everyone keeps handing it to them.

Communal ownership needs named stewardship and visible duties, and consequences for chronic freeloading. It also needs enough relational capacity to confront the problem before resentment becomes the real government. A community can’t run on the assumption that getting rid of bosses gets rid of passivity, selfishness, theft, or learned helplessness.

The desire to go back to a natural way of living together has outrun the knowledge of how to actually do it. People know they’re lonely and exhausted. They usually don’t know why so many earlier communities failed, why the same conflicts keep coming back, or how quickly beautiful land stops mattering once money, children, jealousy and ownership come into it.

I am not currently recruiting for a community or attempting to start one, which makes it easier for me to write an article like this, since I’m not trying to get you onto my land. I do plan to buy land, and people may naturally end up living near me. But all of the warnings in this article apply to me, too.
