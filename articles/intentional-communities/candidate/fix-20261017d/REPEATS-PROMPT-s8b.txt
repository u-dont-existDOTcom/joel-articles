[B44] # Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways

[B45] Most communes of my acquaintance went wrong from the inside, in one of two ways.

[B46] ## The secular failure

[B47] The secular communes had a solution for how to distribute property or labor, but when the deep waters of jealousy or childhood hurt or humiliation or attraction or resentment or grief came up, the group was without a common method for dealing with it. The feelings just became agenda items.

[B48] Meeting procedure isn’t going to solve every problem, no matter how sophisticated the system of labor and money and decision making may be in a community of the [Federation of Egalitarian Communities](https://www.egalitariancommunities.org/) or any other income-sharing community. It can’t make it so that two people don’t hate each other. It can only decide whose turn it is to talk about the hatred, and whether a motion about it needs somebody to second it.

[B49] Eventually, when people are together long enough, politics will become a way of carrying emotional material that people can’t name. There will be a fight about a schedule or committee or noise or food or some policy or other, but it won’t be about that. There’s no shared way to talk about the hurt itself, but the meeting agenda is the one place the group lets you push for something and win, so the angry communard goes there, trying to get some justice.

[B50] ## The spiritual failure

[B51] This comes about when the community realizes the importance of embracing a transformation from within, and then funnels it through a particular person, usually the founder, a teacher, a channel or some other special knower of how things really are.

[B52] [The Farm](https://thefarmcommunity.com/), in Tennessee, at its largest about 1,500 people, had many beautiful values. But in the early days, the community’s spirituality and social life followed the founder, Stephen Gaskin. From what former members have shared, the community’s doctrine ran deep into members’ intimate decisions. This is how it often goes: folks talk about oneness with the universe, while one man defines what the universe is.

[B53] The two failures mirror each other. Secular groups distribute power but leave the inner life mostly private or ignored. Spiritual groups attempt to address the inner life, but because it's the inner world being shaped by the outer as a condition of membership, we tend to find both real-world and spiritual power concentrating around whoever defines the path.

[B54] The deeper problem is that somebody who's earned authority in one role can use it in all their other roles. The founder who is a gifted teacher, the therapist who really helps people, the farmer or business manager that actually carries a lot of the economy. The danger starts when one person has the ability to define the problem, control the information related to the problem, define what the solution is, and make it hard for people to leave or express disagreement.

[B55] I dream of a way to work in depth that isn’t run by a guru, and a way of being in a community with distributed authority. A place where people who've done the healing first can join without pretending they're fully finished, and that also has peer practice and enough play that it doesn’t become a permanent repair shop.

[B56] # The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together

[B57] [image 7](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!vVsb!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe8999a94-f206-40e6-9a8d-bb9afa8c38fe_800x560.svg)

[B58] Communities tend to focus on the aspect of their project that was most interesting to the original founders, for example political types getting super into writing by-laws, or spiritual types just meditating all the time, or therapists processing each other, or people who like psychedelics discovering they’re all God together and never getting to the point of deciding who will put their name on the deed.

[B59] I’ve broken the model I propose down into the 4 parts shown in the above diagram. Note, Pl/ork means play + work. Calling therapy “inner work” forgets the inner child, who also wants to play. (Forgive the neologism. I hope *pl/ork* isn’t too corny. 🌽): 1) inner pl/ork (reparenting), 2) relational pl/ork (peer counseling especially), 3) medicine pl/ork (like loveyhuasca or iboga), and 4) the practical logistical foundation which is most of this article, since I have guides separately on the other parts. Obviously the details are complicated, and jealousy, children, money, land, God, and iboga don’t fit neatly into four boxes, but let’s go with it.

[B60] These four parts leak into one another almost immediately. Governance can end up slyly incorporating personal tensions like jealousy. Those who have positions with more control of shared resources will automatically be affected by that psychologically (power corrupts, as they say). Those who know medicine tend to have another type of god complex since they may make life or death decisions for people routinely. How folks choose to raise children can expose values the founders never discussed. And myriad other categories arise, of course.

[B61] And let's face it: food, housing, and logistics are not enough reason to bind whole lives together. That's more like a situationship. A community needs a shared sacred inner practice that gives the difficulty of our life together a deeper meaning. We help each other heal and choose love over fear. And we turn outward with a purpose, using the *escuelita* model to bring our healing to the world.

[B62] ## Founded on Freedom

[B63] Nothing suppressed, everything processed. Each of us guides our own practice, and our practices weave the community into the quilt that keeps us all warm and safe. Whatever answers we find stay fluid and organic.

[B64] Speaking of dynamically weaving ourselves together, though, a community does need some stable agreements that they're not tweaking and revising every time somebody has a revelation in breathwork. But these agreements need to stay open to lived experience.

[B65] When agreements get frozen, religious communities tend to call the frozen version doctrine. Secular communities on the other hand often call it “the process,” which can become just as sacred.

[B66] To give an example of striking the balance: My contemplative practice is grounded in the early [Buddhist suttas](https://suttacentral.net/), with a critical reading. Most are treasure, while some are obviously late additions (sometimes to the point of real absurdity). A living community should relate to its own teachings in that same critical manner while staying devoted to following what they've decided on so far.

[B67] ### 1. Inner pl/ork

[B68] Probably the most obvious type of pl/ork is reparenting. Here’s an example: you teach your inner child geography, while s/he teaches you the world is a cat!

[B69] [image 8](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!7tAF!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F66730244-8446-4321-83f0-f353d46a8e1e_770x844.jpeg)

[B70] [Inner-child reparenting](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/inner-child-self-love-tips-and-guided) means learning to identify as the best holistic inner adult, which is comprised of the three inner adult roles: Nurturer, Protector, and Guide. The newly developed adult reparents their newly recognized inner child. The child receives all that love, protection, and guidance that you give it, and as a result, your little one gains more freedom to remain alive inside you, which brings back the joy of life that many adults have forgotten about.

[B71] And this reparenting journey is built on [somatic regulation](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/somatic-modalities-strategic-sequencing) first. I.e., listening to the body's feelings before making a decision with the mind. And that actually symbolizes the adult/child relationship to me in some way as well.

[B72] ### 2. Relational pl/ork

[B73] [Hearthwork](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/hearthwork-an-integrated-peer-counseling) distributes counseling among peers: equal time, attention without interpretation or fixing, and then you switch.

[B74] The idea is that the capacity to be there for people when they need help isn’t located in one professional or guru, or the founder of the group that everyone emotionally depends on, but in the community as a whole.

[B75] But since we're merging peer therapy with community, we also need to make sure that the person caring about you and listening to you shouldn't translate that information into administration over your life, the way a professional therapist might diagnose you as this or that and then suddenly you're ineligible for certain careers. Although obviously that information can come out regardless when people are in close quarters.

[B76] Another thing is knowing which problems are beyond what peers can handle and need outside or expert help. And another is to deal with jealousy and conflict while it's still between the people involved, before it gets put on the agenda (generally at the bottom of the agenda). Speaking of jealousy, check out my [Romance Guide](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/romance-advice-i-wish-my-parents), since that's a topic each person & each couple first handles by themselves.

[B77] ### 3. Medicine pl/ork

[B78] Community can reach new heights by carefully incorporating serious plant medicines with peer guidance rather than exalting any one person as a special shaman.

[B79] This doesn’t mean that a peer-led ceremony should be done without structure. Things like screening participants, making sure the sitters are sober, having a plan for dealing with emergencies, planning for integration, and other rules and boundaries need to be dealt with beforehand. While there may be some pure learning that could come from having several altered members figure out what rules and boundaries are needed on the spot, it’s not something I recommend.

[B80] ### 4. The container

[B81] This is the part about membership, land, resources, governance, children, leaving, etc.

[B82] Like my father, I’m aiming for communities with less and less money involved, since money is antagonistic to the spirit of community. If I have money, I can get whatever I want, regardless of whether anyone cares about my wellbeing. That's the hegemonic philosophy of life we are trying to overturn. I think the path there has three stages.

[B83] First there will be no money exchanged between members for basic community functions like food, housing, care, tools, education, transport etc. Members might have outside jobs, and they bring an agreed share of that income in. This is probably a good place to start if someone wants to create a community now and have it be realistic.

[B84] Later all wages and other income earned by the members from work outside the community, or from business inside the community, will be paid into the common purse of the community. No member accumulates private wealth while a member of the community. Some version of this is how things have been done in the [Bruderhof](https://www.bruderhof.com/intentional-community) community for generations, and my father lived there for 17 years. It is also done, in a somewhat different way, at [Twin Oaks](https://www.twinoaks.org/about-twinoaks-community/about-income-sharing), and a number of other income-sharing communities. So a common purse isn’t just a theory.

[B85] Eventually, the community would also stop using money outside. People would meet their needs directly, or through exchange (be it trade, gift, work, obligation, or whatever). I couldn't find a well-documented modern community that has gone this far, and in fact, it may be impractical in some locations because of government requirements like taxes on land. So perhaps the practical end point here is a community that is nearly entirely moneyless, but has a small collective cash boundary on the outside.

[B86] None of this does away with scarcity or economic power. There are lots of areas where such power can be wielded: access to food storage, housing, tools, land, medicine, transportation, and the outside world’s currency, to name a few. Even in a moneyless community, someone can control what's scarce. Who decides how these things are distributed? What do members owe one another? What happens when someone wants to leave?

[B87] These are less exciting topics to discuss spiritually. It's rare to see someone come back from an ecstatic vision with news of how to revamp the community’s policies on provisional members, or how to divvy up assets with a departing member, or how to prevent one founder from having control over the land, or what to do when a child reports something serious. And if they do, it's still something that needs a sober thinking-through.

[B88] But no amount of gorgeous land will make up for the mess that is created when one person owns the land or has the final say about what will happen on it. And when members come on board faster than the group can support them, all the peer counseling in the world won't make up for it, any more than medicine makes up for the lack of a written exit agreement.

[B89] The first three parts are the inside of the container, and this container is what gives those a stable place to manifest. It's the practical logistical foundation that gets built once the architects can work well together.

[B90] If one of the four gets skipped, it will need to be dealt with eventually, at high stress, usually after the group has invested land, labor, or other resources and everyone has an opinion on how things should be.

[B91] # The Medicine Part - Yes, I'm Naming It

[B92] Most of the writing on communities hasn't caught up with psychedelics. They've gone from taboo toward regulated use really fast ([licensed service centers for psilocybin started opening in Oregon](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/ph/preventionwellness/pages/oregon-psilocybin-services.aspx) in 2023, Colorado set up a system for regulating natural medicines and began licensing in 2025, and New Mexico enacted a [Medical Psilocybin Act](https://www.nmlegis.gov/Legislation/Legislation?Chamber=S&LegNo=219&LegType=B&year=25) in 2025), and Australia has allowed [authorized psychiatrists limited access](https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/explore-topic/mdma-and-psilocybine-hub) for a list of conditions since 2023, for both psilocybin and MDMA.

[B93] That said, it's not a smooth ride or an inevitability. The US FDA issued a [Complete Response Letter](https://download.open.fda.gov/crl/CRL_NDA215455_20240808.pdf) to the MDMA-assisted therapy application in 2024, where they did not approve it and requested more evidence. And the thing with Portugal is that many people will lazily say "they've legalized everything there" - but that's false, [they've only decriminalized possessing small quantities for personal use](https://www.euda.europa.eu/system/files/publications/642/PolicyProfile_Portugal_WEB_Final_289201.pdf). It's still a crime to traffic drugs.

[B94] All of which means the legality of psychedelics varies by country and by state. It varies by substance, too. And it depends on a group’s religious status and on what exactly it does. So if psychedelic medicine is going to be part of a community at all, that community should definitely get actual local legal advice.

[B95] Most intentional communities using psychedelics are still not public about it, due to possible trouble (even where the law allows some use) from parents worried about children, from neighbors imagining chaos, from insurance providers who might drop them, and from local officials who may not understand any of the distinctions.

[B96] I'm talking about it here because not to talk about it would be to falsify my model. Many of the people who read this article are already familiar with my [writing about psychedelics](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/s/psychedelics), including my [loveyhuasca/Haoma material](https://soma.u-dont-exist.com/). If this article is going to be honest, it has to talk about both the value and the risk.

[B97] There’s a serious case against having medicine as part of a community. Physical and psychological negative effects of ayahuasca were common in the [Global Ayahuasca Survey](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36962494/), although severe effects were far less common and context mattered. And these medicines can open things up faster than a person (or their group) can deal with.

[B98] They do also attract people who are all for revelation and hate the follow-through, people who can collect 12 ceremonies and 6 origin stories and still can't say sorry to their housemate. Any community based mainly around the peak experience is going to find out sooner or later that the insight doesn't do the practical work for it. Sometimes the dishes need doing and a scared kid needs comforting.

[B99] In my experience there are real reasons to take psychedelics communally too - eg they can lower defensive barriers which otherwise dominate every serious conversation, and create strong bonds between members of the community who have been through the harder nights together. But there are also negatives that can come from taking a psychedelic - confusion, grandiosity, dependency, fouling up dynamics between members. Are you mature enough as a community to tell the good from the bad?

[B100] ## Community Is What Makes Psychedelics Pl/ork Best!

[B101] Part of it is integration: an insight needs somewhere to land. What happens when you come back from an amazing experience which gave you insight and clarity about your life, into the same old environment where nobody knows what you’ve been through and you’re faced with the same old cues? It can be super helpful to have gone through the experience in a community, where the people who held you through it can also notice what happens to you over the following months.

[B102] The deeper you go, the more alienated you can feel. After my original ayahuasca ego deaths at 25, I had trouble finding anyone in the physical world who seemed to have experienced anything similar. I couldn’t even find them at Buddhist monasteries despite all the preaching about enlightenment. That helped give me a God complex, a Savior complex, or both, which then attracted exactly the right people to show me my limits. 😂 In a community of people who’ve sat through the same nights, you aren’t the only one who’s been there, and someone can catch the God complex early.

[B103] Do you trust the people you're relying on for support? Support from people you trust is different from paid support, which is often paying people you don't know to look after you for a night. Sometimes they're great, but sometimes the person with the feather has only known you for 4 hours and already has a cosmology to explain everything you say.

[B104] In a community, your sober sitter may be someone who's known you for years, and you feel the difference between being watched and being held. This is especially important when doing iboga or other long and medically risky experiences. For those you need to be properly screened beforehand and monitored continuously.

[B105] Your peers can also reality check any downloads you may have had. Psychedelics can give you insight and they can give you convincing nonsense, all in the same bright shining light. A few friends who know your history may notice the difference between new understanding and… mom.

[B106] One of the benefits of community is rhythm, and many traditional medicinal practices occur within ceremonies that have a communal schedule. The shared schedule of ceremonies, rather than each person doing ceremonies when they want, can serve to limit compulsive use and provide for integration time.

[B107] There are the practical benefits: logistics are easier when it comes to things like having childcare arranged by the group that parents are comfortable leaving their kid(s) with, not needing to drive away from the ceremony, having someone bring water to someone or just sit quietly with them while they come back to normal consciousness, etc.

[B108] And then there will be people to play with! Raves often let strangers fast-track intimacy (getting to a level of intimacy that usually comes after building trust), but imagine doing ecstatic music & dance in a community that already knows each other (and each other’s inconvenient habits). 💃🕺

[B109] But I won’t idealize psychedelics. Some people and communities should avoid them. Where access is ahead of competence, people will become casualties of enthusiasm. Is your community humble enough to say no? To pause? Using the medicine takes that. It also takes humility to refer people out, and admit when an experience did harm.

[B110] ## My Ceremony Principles

[B111] No special shamans here. Traditional lineages usually disagree with me on this, and many ayahuasca and Bwiti communities believe the medicine should stay under trained lineage authority. I respect what those traditions have preserved, and I've learned some "dos & don't dos" from their sensible practices and less sensible practices. For example, although I do highly value loving kindness meditation, inner child reparenting, and nibbana meditation before ceremony, during, and at all times, I notice a lot of people want their ceremony to look and feel like some kind of abstract mix of indigenous rituals & props to make it feel more authentic and sacred than their everyday life. I understand the benefit of setting aside one time and place for sacred experiences (the book, "God is Red" comes to mind), although the shadow side to that is making the spiritual seem like another world apart from your regular life, which is sort of opposite to integration.

[B112] And while I do think it's valuable to have someone highly experienced in navigating the spirit worlds who is able to sense where you are in those and guide you, I also wouldn't trust many of these shamans to guide me, considering it's common to find that even the ones who can heal people very well may still be happy to send evil spirits to kill someone, if that hit job is lucrative. I also don't trust their medical intuition in general, considering the Huni Kuin shamans are famous for claiming that all other ayahuasca shamans are giving people cancer by using the knotted B. caapi vine (since the knots remind them of tumors). They're right though that modern folk do very often decide they're ready to lead ceremonies long before they're really trained and prepared. I'm sure I could still learn a lot from those traditions, and they will continue to develop their understandings as well.

[B113] My basic issue is with placing one charismatic person above everyone else’s direct experience. The facilitator on the retreat circuit may be wise. Or they may be someone who hasn’t been vetted by anybody, who isn’t accountable to anyone, maybe even a sexual predator, or financially dependent on people coming back to them for more feather-waving. And the more deeply someone is opening up, the more it costs not to personally know who's running their ceremony.

[B114] Even in a culture of peer medicine, there could still be an informal "shaman", someone everyone trusts and gradually stops questioning, even though nobody gave them the title. I don't know yet how to avoid this. Things that might help would be to have rotation of roles, and public accountability. Though neither of these seem like a complete solution.

[B115] Still, I want people to learn to guide themselves and care for each other, within the limits of their competence. I’ve written more about [peer-held ceremonies](http://ceremonies.u-dont-exist.com/), and about [the best psychopath shaman I ever met](http://sidney.u-dont-exist.com/).

[B116] Just because it's a peer-led ceremony doesn't mean it's ok to be casually irresponsible. In fact, since the authority is spread around, there needs to be more competence. Some things are a must: screening for contraindications (both medical and psychiatric), reviewing medications and combinations, having the right kind of sober sitter present, and deciding before anybody takes anything what kind of situations would require professional help or emergency care.

[B117] Also, I am especially cautious about medicines and protocols that I see as more destabilizing and/or presenting more of a medical burden. This includes some uses of [ketamine and MDMA](http://mdmaket.u-dont-exist.com/) and [bufo](http://bufo.u-dont-exist.com/). My reasons are given in those articles, respectively, since they need more space.

[B118] Using medicine is secondary to all of the other pl/ork that comes first: reparenting, developing somatic capacity, and learning to do peer counseling. The medicine can deepen an existing practice, but it can't be a pharmacological short cut to what takes several months of honest relationship with other people.

[B119] This prerequisite also takes care of another issue in one shot: it weeds out the experience collectors.  If someone is unwilling to put in 6 months learning to listen without imposing their solution, it is unlikely they will come out of a ceremony more relationally mature, regardless of how many self-transforming fractalized machine elves told them they are the reincarnation of King David & Elvis.😂

[B120] Integration is a term thrown around so much that I'm not sure if it still has meaning. But basically, it's your everyday life. The ceremony is supposed to open something up in you. Look at your life in the weeks after the ceremony and see how things have changed. That includes your relationships, behaviors, sleeping habits, your decision-making, and perhaps most importantly, how well you can deal with frustration without declaring a new spiritual emergency. 😂

[B121] All of the “supports” are important: preparation, nutrition, screening for drug interactions, being disciplined about the dose, and having a plan for dealing with emergencies. They matter more than ceremony aesthetics. My current safety material is at [Altered States Triage](https://badtrips.u-dont-exist.com/).

[B122] Finally, there are the legal issues. These vary a lot, and they are not always well-defined. As a community, you need to write down what is and isn’t allowed, which activities are private (to the individual, to a group in the community, or to the community vs the outside), and what kinds of activities have been reviewed from a legal standpoint. “I don’t think anybody around here has ever heard of that” is information, but it’s not the same thing as a legal opinion.

[B123] ## After the Psychedelic Calling

[B124] [image 9](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5y74!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0844bbea-ba0a-4ff0-8b62-4e46426dae51_1672x941.png)

[B125] A lot of people feel the urge to form a village after going through some serious psychedelic and/or mystical experiences. Finding their people and helping others get to whatever just opened up is part of the same urge.

[B126] I’ve felt that urge myself. The problem is that it can come with grandiosity, and I’ve felt that too. You come back believing love can heal anyone, and then you meet people who don’t want to change, people who sincerely want to change but keep choosing the opposite, and people in such a crisis that their need for help is more than your enthusiasm can hold.

[B127] That call may be real, but forming a community based on omnipotence is a terrible idea.

[B128] A group that begins in collective spiritual euphoria can recruit too quickly and promise too much, and only do the math on how much need it can absorb once several people already depend on it. The sections on capacity and on membership, later on in this article, are partly there to protect the original generosity from that first rush.

[B129] Once the euphoria wears off and people have a more sober view of the call they feel, it can become useful. A community can be a place for people to train up in the practices, and sometimes go off and start another experiment. In fact, if people are training there so they can help others build communities of their own, they’d be working towards forming a real alternative. That answers the reasonable objection that communes are just privileged escape pods, which gets double dipped with the objection that psychedelics are also privileged escape pods (or, alternatively, as some say, "a vacation for the poor").

[B135] I just read this post from Jamie Wheal about the “polycrisis,” which I suppose needs no explanation, but it’s basically a word for summarizing that every kind of shit possible is hitting the fan which was already half broken:

[B137] How accurate are the fears about the future? I don't know exactly. Maybe AI will improve the lot of humanity enormously. Maybe millions of people will lose their jobs to AI. Maybe AI will become the administrative layer between us and nearly everything. Maybe all of these things will happen before Chantress Seba finishes going through all the applications to her commune. 😂

[B138] Ok, so on a personal level, it makes more sense to me to focus on cultivating more human independence from the technosphere, all the machines and systems modern life runs on, than to wait for certainty. It's better to have community and find out I didn't need a way out of the technosphere than to need one after spending ten years optimizing my social media feed, right?

[B139] What connections to the technosphere do you keep, and which do you limit, when you're designing a community? This is also one area where many modern folk will balk. Let the community decide if I can use my social media, or ChatGPT? Are you kidding? I get it. I really do. You're not a child anymore. But on the other hand, these technologies don't just affect you, they affect how you relate to the community as well.

[B142] Medical care, banking, phones, vehicles, schools, supply chains, government ID, internet and energy are each a separate decision. If you opt out of them completely, it can cause needless suffering. If you depend on them completely, the outside system can reach directly into every essential function.

[B143] The Amish, over generations, have made community decisions about where and if they want to use a piece of technology based on how it affects their community. Different Amish communities make different decisions. Sometimes they use it at work but restrict it at home. Sometimes they share it instead of everyone owning one. Sometimes they hold off on using it until they know more about its social effects. Whatever else you think of the Amish, they at least understand that adopting a technology is a decision for the group to make, rather than something that gets downloaded onto your device while you sleep.

[B144] Medical care is one of the toughest dependencies to work out. A community may prefer natural care, but what happens when someone in the community needs trauma surgery, antibiotics, dentistry, emergency obstetric care, or any number of specialist services? The community should think through how members are going to have access to care. What kind of care is going to be guaranteed to community members by the community using common resources? What things might require outside dollars or some form of insurance? And how will the community deal with people who choose to accept (or not accept) different levels of medical care?

[B145] No member should have to prove their ideological loyalty when they're scared or sick. There should be pre-existing agreements in place so that their choices will be protected, both for catastrophic illness and end of life care. Medical privacy should be discussed, but may not be guaranteed, since some medical conditions are contagious.

[B146] At the same time, having social connections is measurably good for a person's health. The Surgeon General's advisory notes an increased risk for a range of health outcomes among people who are socially isolated. Community's not a panacea, but it can make a difference, day to day, in the conditions people get sick, heal, age, and die in.

[B147] # Jealousy Can Capture Monogamy or Free Love

[B148] Any community that lasts will eventually be reorganized by attraction and pair-bonding. When two exes live in the same city, they can move to different sides of the city. When they're part of a thirty-person commune, they may still be in the same kitchen the next morning, working in the same team, attending the same meetings, sharing childcare and the same friends. A couple's jealousy or breakup becomes a problem for the entire community, because it will change the loyalties, the sleeping arrangements, the labor, the kids, the alliances, and sometimes who's leaving.

[B149] The discussion generally goes straight to "should people be monogamous or free-love?" but as I discuss in my [Romance Guide](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/romance-advice-i-wish-my-parents), this is one level too late in the conversation. The community problem is that, in close quarters, jealousy gets magnified, and jealousy can capture either system.

[B150] Rigid monogamy can turn fear and possession into a moral law. If people are doing free love, but aren't doing the inner pl/ork, they can just end up with a larger spreadsheet to distribute their fear over. It's not like calling something by a certain name dissolves the childhood panic, the comparison, the terror of abandonment, or the desire to control another person.

[B151] I don't think it should be up to the community to decide for everyone what form relationships will take. People can make different agreements with each other, in pairs, threes, fours, whatever. They can change those agreements as they wish. And they can ask for support when those agreements bring up old material.

[B152] Whatever forms people choose, I do think that housing, security for kids, membership and access to common necessities should not be contingent on a person staying in some agreement with the people they're romantically involved with.

[B153] [ZEGG](http://zegg.org/), a community near Berlin, and [Tamera](http://tamera.org/), another in Portugal, have spent decades experimenting with relationships inside community. Their answers are not a set of commandments that everyone must follow. But they are an example of how a community can acknowledge the erotic aspects of life. It is not necessary for 20 adults to pretend that they've become neutral colleagues just because the by-laws say so.

[B154] The non-negotiable for me is that jealousy not be allowed to become the hidden governor. This doesn't mean it can't come up. It needs to be able to be talked about and felt in the body. It needs to be explored without humiliation. And it needs to be let go of gradually, and not allowed to turn into punishment, or into community rules.

[B155] There are some ways the group can help, and basic boundaries the group can enforce, without deciding what type of relationships people have. Even so, there will still be disagreements about this. That's more realistic than saying it's all been resolved.

[B156] # The Grown Children Get the Final Review

[B157] Couples who live together, share the same culture and even the same bed still disagree about screens, vaccinations, [schooling](https://www.instagram.com/p/DcDGotaFCeZ/?utm_source=ig_web_copy_link&igsh=MzRlODBiNWFlZA==), religion, food, discipline and medical care. And that's just two people. Now think of twenty adults, all with different childhoods.

[B158] The community has to share enough principles about raising kids to offer them coherence, and leave parents enough room to remain parents.

[B159] My own childhood broke many mainstream rules about what children should see and do. I won’t unpack all of it here. I’m grateful that my mother nursed me until I was five, grateful my parents didn’t vaccinate me, and grateful for nearly all of the unusual world they gave me.

[B168] ## Communal Parenting Adds On

[B169] One of the biggest reasons I think community matters for kids is Jean Liedloff's [*The Continuum Concept*](https://www.arvindguptatoys.com/arvindgupta/conconcept.pdf). It's one outsider's interpretation of the Ye'kuana, an Indigenous people of the Venezuelan rainforest she lived with, and she may have idealized what she saw, but it's still a powerful picture: the babies are carried along in ordinary adult life, the kids are surrounded by people of several ages and learn by taking part, and it doesn't fall on one exhausted parent to be a child's whole social world. While a nuclear household can imitate some of this, a real village can make it normal.

[B170] Experiments where kids sleep and live apart from their parents deserve special caution. Research on Israeli kibbutzim found that [the practice of kids sleeping communally eventually ended for a mix of reasons: developmental findings, parents' preferences and social change](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12395568/). Kids can benefit from high-quality group care and multiple loving adults in their lives, but replacing a secure attachment to their parents is a different matter.

[B171] Tamera's Children's Place has repeated some of these old Kibbutz failings, taking children away from biological parents, because their founders' ideology said that children are traumatized by the nuclear family and its unstable romantic underpinnings. On the other hand, their actual practice did allow parents (including fathers) to stay with their children in Children's Place, so they weren't as dogmatic as I originally thought. My own bias is still toward a system where kids are not solely taken care of by their parents, while still honoring the parent-child bond. If the father is unknown, the whole community can do the fathering, but the biological mom's role should always be honored, since that attachment is formed already from years of nursing.

[B172] ## Children Need Freedom Inside Real Protection

[B173] [image 13](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Svpw!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff76a1545-e84b-406d-8731-1abde6502718_3264x1464.jpeg)

[B174] [caption] African kids enjoying their childhood!

[B175] Kids also need their own territory, places where they can create their own society, invent games and work out ordinary disputes, without always being under the management of adults.

[B176] I got a taste of that at a Unitarian youth camp, and it's still one of the freest memories I have. ZEGG's *Kinderhaus* works from a similar intuition. Adults take care of safety on the outside, but kids still get a world of their own.

[B177] This could be badly misunderstood, so the safeguards need to be explicit. The adults still have to pay attention to what goes on in a children's territory. Older kids can't be allowed to boss the younger ones around there. And keeping secrets can't be treated as independence.

[B178] I also reject the suburban panic that treats every warm relationship between a child and an unrelated adult as suspicious. It's good for kids to have a whole bunch of grownups who know them and enjoy them, who would notice when something changes, and who would act when something's wrong. When adults panic like that, kids can lose all those grownups without the panic actually preventing abuse.

[B179] There are several parts to this protection:

[B180] Give children real agency. When kids say no, it counts. No grownup has a right to their affection, physical contact, obedience outside of legitimate safety needs, or private access to them.

[B181] Create a no-secrets rule. No grownup has a right to ask a kid to keep a secret from their mom or another trusted adult, whether it's about a gift, a game, a touch, a conversation or a relationship. A surprise has an end date. But secrets meant to isolate a kid aren't allowed.

[B182] Protecting children is the duty of adults, but it requires children to help also. "The Boy Who Cried Wolf" is a didactic children's story which continues to be useful generation after generation. The Buddha even taught his son honesty as the most important first lesson. I agree. We must teach children truthfulness before there's a crisis. "Make believe" must be labeled as such. When adults lie to children (like Santa is real etc), that teaches them to misunderstand the difference between reality and make-believe. Make sure kids understand what they say will be taken seriously if they are known to be honest. And those who are known to lie a lot are especially targeted by abusers who know their accusations won't be believed. Don't let the teaching turn into interrogation or punishment when they're not sure. A scared or confused kid may get mixed up telling what happened and still need protection.

[B183] Hear the child even when the accused is someone people love. Communities fail here, because the adult may be charismatic, useful, rich, spiritually important, or everybody's friend. Decide on the response process ahead of time, before anyone's face is attached to it. Keep everyone at risk safe first, right away, then make sure the accusation is investigated competently and fairly. Nobody's status buys silence.

[B184] Make sure kids have relationships and a reporting route outside the community. Grandparents, cousins, local friends, teachers and other trusted adults give them perspective on their life, and someone to talk to. Make sure there's at least one trusted adult, advocate or reporting route they can reach without going through the community's leadership. In 1984, Vermont authorities [raided a religious community in Island Pond](https://vtdigger.org/2024/06/21/40-years-later-island-pond-has-little-interest-in-revisiting-its-historic-raid/) and took 112 kids into custody over suspected abuse. A judge turned down the state's blanket request to hold them, because it couldn't name any specific case, and the kids went home that same day. State intervention can fail spectacularly, and enclosing kids completely in a community can fail too. Kids need more than one world.

[B185] Sometimes we also need to protect people from the rare, but real threat posed by dangerous children. I looked through [the research corpus](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/) for a well-documented case of a child in an intentional community who remained seriously dangerous to others. I was looking to find the reported conduct, the assessment, the intervention, the review, and what happened later. I couldn't find one. It's possible that records are private, or that cases were referred elsewhere. Whatever the reason, I can't claim communities already know how to handle the hardest cases on their own. In those cases, the child, the family, and the people at risk all need access to competent help and review, hopefully including non-intrusive help from outside the home community.

[B186] ## The Door Must Open Outward at Adulthood

[B187] If a community's kids never leave, it has no way of knowing whether they'd come back freely. So let them study, travel and make friends, encounter other ways of living, and disagree with the founders without being treated as traitors. And kids coming back isn't necessarily the best thing for the communal movement either. If they then start their own communities, we may see more evolution than if they simply return and continue what their parents started.

[B188] Amish young people, from about age 16, go through a period of freedom from parental control called *Rumspringa*. During this time they might travel outside, although they normally choose to stay in the community and begin acting as adults, including courtship. At the end of this period, they decide as adults whether to join the church, and [roughly 85 percent or more of them end up joining](https://groups.etown.edu/amishstudies/social-organization/population-growth/). Pop culture makes *Rumspringa* seem like a wilder, more universal teenage free-for-all than it is, but the main point is that they're baptized as adults, which means belonging is a commitment they make.

[B189] Every childhood narrows the future. Most ordinary parents aren't going to give their kid a bunch of money at 18 and say, "Go see the world." They'll try to get them ready for some next step that can actually work, so they can become self-sufficient, and eventually take care of their aging parents. A poor or money-free community may have even less cash to offer, and basic survival may rightly come first.

[B190] Unlimited freedom isn't realistic, but it doesn't need to be artificially impossible for grown kids to leave. Kids in or outside of intentional communities all need to learn enough about how the money world outside operates that they aren't helpless in it. Like how to get a job, pay rent, read contracts, handle banks, get IDs, avoid getting scammed, and how to ask for help when they're unsure about any of that. And all that sounds amorphous if it's not structured into plausible next steps outside the community (school, an apprenticeship, work, family, another community).

[B191] Kids also need transferable skills and education. It helps if they have relationships with people outside the community. They should be able to access their own records and documents. It would be great if the community could also provide resources to smooth the transition, like temporary housing, help with travel, etc. If it can't, it can still avoid punishing departure.

[B192] Belonging and freedom should go hand in hand.

[B193] # The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas

[B194] [image 14](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!1QJp!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F745198f3-56e4-42d3-aa6b-1a608ea4b0ef_860x513.jpeg)

[B195] [caption] A Zapatista Schoolhouse. It reads: Worthy Rage (Digna Rabia)

[B196] In 2005, a few college buddies and I went down to Oventik, one of the Zapatista *caracoles* ("snails"), the regional centers that tie their villages together in Chiapas. We almost died several times getting there. The mountain road ran along cliffs, and our rental car was on its last legs, which is a great combination when you're on your way to visit a revolution.

[B197] San Cristóbal de las Casas is where the only white man we met sat us down in a café and grilled us so directly that he seemed like a CIA agent. Why were we there? Who were we working with? What were our plans? When were we leaving?

[B198] It made us feel important for a minute. We were just college kids in a dying rental car, but apparently the empire had questions for us. 😂

[B199] Oventik itself was like something out of a dream. We played basketball outside with the Zapatista youth, and people gave us talks about their history and philosophy, which I translated for my friends because my Spanish was better. Visitors were coming from all around the world to learn, and to make the communities less easy to attack in silence.

[B200] I felt a deep spirituality from the Zapatistas. I also suspect there may be a private relationship with ayahuasca, but nobody told me that, and it's only my impression, not something to take as fact.

[B201] What impressed me the most was how they do authority. It's *mandar obedeciendo*, to lead by obeying: the representatives carry the decisions people made in the assemblies, and they have to answer to the people who gave them the mandate.

[B202] The Zapatistas are also the closest thing to a whole example that I found, because their autonomy goes beyond how they vote. They've set up their own education and health care, and their own justice, and they run their own collective production and political formation. On top of that they've worked out ways of teaching outsiders, all while coordinating beyond a single village.

[B203] [*The Fire and the Word*](https://www.amazon.com/Fire-Word-History-Zapatista-Movement/dp/087286488X) describes the movement growing from family to village, village to region, and region to larger structures. It also gives one of the most useful Zapatista principles for community design: build the practice first and develop the theory afterward. The autonomous municipalities came first; an association of them became a rehearsal for the Good Government Juntas (Boards). When different municipalities encountered different problems and uneven progress, that experience generated another structure rather than being forced to fit the original plan.

[B204] After a long internal review, in 2023, the Zapatistas announced they were dissolving their previous federated governmental structure, which was split into Rebel Zapatista Autonomous Municipalities and the Good Government Juntas. The original idea was to have power vested in the local communities, but it had crept up and consolidated itself at higher levels over time for practical reasons. Their [new arrangement](https://enlacezapatista.ezln.org.mx/2023/11/13/ninth-part-the-new-structure-of-zapastista-autonomy/) moved authority downward toward a Local Autonomous Government in each community.

[B205] Institutions get more and more layers and offices as they age. We see this same pattern happen repeatedly, everywhere in history, except usually it goes one way without reversal, unless there's a revolution.

[B206] But there is a special reason the Zapatistas were successfully able to reverse this degeneration. Zapatista autonomy grew out of Indigenous communities with long histories. They share language and family ties. They've fought land struggles together, and defended themselves together for decades. A new ecovillage can't copy the diagram everyone can see and get the trust underneath it right away.

[B207] And that's not me saying that. Despite actually trying to train people in their model, the Zapatistas themselves have been clear that people shouldn't expect to directly or easily import/export their philosophy (*Zapatismo*) as something ready-made, shake & bake. The useful thing is how they learn: they build things and ask questions, and when they find out where the practice isn't working, they change the structure.

[B208] Now, the Zapatistas also don't prove my whole money-free economy goal. They still use money internally in some cases, not others, and they still use it externally for trade. They've also benefitted from outside economic help, while at the same time making efforts to remain politically independent from donors and more self-sustaining.

[B209] [image 15](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ba5Q!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fee14d04d-c0b9-4c24-ab61-ec02ec78ff8a_680x452.jpeg)

[B210] [caption] *Escuelita Zapatista — The Zapatista school to teach outsiders their way of life*

[B211] For the 10th anniversary of their autonomous governments, the Zapatistas opened up the [*Escuelita*](https://schoolsforchiapas.org/advances/schools/la-escuelita/). Outsiders lived with families there and learned autonomy by taking part in day to day life. Around 1,500 students came to the first sessions, and thousands more came to later ones.

[B212] One way an *escuelita* could fail is if it becomes a business, where residents spend their lives performing community for paying outsiders, and success gets measured by how many people come instead of how many daughter communities there are. A week of feeling transformed isn't what it's for. It's for teaching enough of the social and material system that some people can build somewhere else.

[B213] As part of [my research](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/) I have also looked for evidence of what happened after the *Escuelita*. While I found information on alumni networks, people bringing practices back with them, and organizing later, I couldn't find a clear chain of somebody having been to the *Escuelita*, then a new communal institution being set up, and then evidence about how its members and their children actually did over time. Zapatista influence was already spreading abroad before the *Escuelita*, so if some institution resembles a Zapatista one later, that alone doesn't prove the *Escuelita* was responsible. I'm borrowing the Zapatistas' teaching method and their ambition to replicate. I'm not claiming the Zapatistas already proved my whole model.

[B214] [Sociocracy](https://www.sociocracyforall.org/sociocracy/) has a very different history, yet some similar features have emerged there as well: circles, decision-making by consent, distributed authority, and linking between the levels. Does this prove there's a universal formula? No. But it means that people building institutions of this sort have another serious system to study, instead of inventing their governance during their first dispute.

[B215] # From One Community to a Movement

[B216] ## Experiment Before You Divorce

[B217] Experimenting with a different rule doesn't have to mean everybody goes out and buys new land right away. Try it inside the parent group first, then as a semi-autonomous project, and then, if it needs more independence, as a seed group nearby that still shares some tools, childcare, business or friendships. Splitting off completely should be just one step along that gradient, and nobody should have to do it just to be allowed to experiment.

[B218] Some experiments (like a different land system, or a different population size, or any number of rules that work together) can't be tried in a way that's cheap. They may look bad when they're starved of the assets they actually need. "We tried it in one room for three months" isn't always a fair test.

[B219] ## Federated Communities Make a Movement

[B220] The problem of how these communities stay connected does eventually need to be solved somehow, if the idea is for them to spread without building another state. It should be solved in a way that allows help between communities and the movement of people and knowledge, and also keeps the differences alive, so communities can keep learning from one another. At the federation level, that outward purpose gets concrete: spreading healing practices, training people, seeding new projects, and keeping any one comfortable enclave from thinking it's the whole movement.

[B221] The [Federation of Egalitarian Communities](https://www.egalitariancommunities.org/) (FEC) says it's rebuilding, and that's a good warning. It takes work to run a federation. Delegates. Records. Compliance. Coordinating resources. Holding meetings. Succession. Whatever dispute function it actually takes on. These things all take up labor and material capacity before any local crisis has eaten into both. If the legal or economic environment requires money, the federation also needs to have a cash budget for dealing with it. But the work of federation doesn't have to be done by a single entity. Two communities might want to be closely linked as friends, and that doesn't stop both of them from also being part of a general federation. There can also be local clusters, or single-function institutions like the FEC's [PEACH health pool](https://www.egalitariancommunities.org/initiatives), which isn't currently governed by the FEC delegates.

[B222] Cooperation also shouldn't slide into assimilation. The FEC has full members and communities working toward full membership, and [Friends](https://www.egalitariancommunities.org/communities) who may just want to stay friends. Also, people need a similarly practical freedom to move between communities, and that takes work: housing, status, health coverage, records, labor accounting, travel costs, taxes, and disputes over the transition all have to be set up, or the freedom to move is just decorative. Fortunately, the interface between communities can often be thin, saying who's responsible for what while somebody crosses between them, and communities don't have to have the same internal systems for it to work.

[B223] Even if nobody uses money internally, communities still need the same honesty about exchanges and obligations. A gift of food, pooled medicine, a vehicle or a tool on loan, trades of labor, emergency housing, a shared building project, a visit that's mainly for training or relationship building: they're all different events, and some of them create future obligations while others don't. The federation can keep track of these for each purpose, without having to put a value on them or require every exchange to be "even". If cash is used for transactions with the outside world, grants and loans need to be kept separate the same way, and so do guarantees and shared enterprise commitments. Solidarity doesn't mean every community is liable for whatever the others owe, and it doesn't mean anyone has an unlimited claim on another community's labor and stores, or its land and tools.

[B224] Whenever a community or a federation creates or applies a rule, it's best practice to store the full case history (with private details protected) so it can be re-evaluated in future: what happened, the principles that actually conflicted, your first judgments, and your final decision and rationale. Any dissenting opinions should go in there as well, along with the consequences in the case. When the rule changes because of a new case, run the old case through again to see how it would turn out. That saves each generation from having to rediscover the same constitutional fight from scratch.

[B225] # Founderism: The Four Questions

[B226] In every community there was somebody who took the initiative to get it started. Somebody found the land, wrote the first documents, taught the method, recruited the first members, or kept things going when everybody else lost interest.

[B227] That person ends up with real influence, and pretending they don't just makes that influence harder to look at. When that power fails to get distributed as the conditions ripen for that, it's called founderism.

[B228] I've seen founderism in many communes that I've visited, and I can see it playing out if I were to be a founder as well. I've got all these proposals and visions here in my guides, and so it would be very easy for me to become the person who decides whether or not everyone else “understood” them correctly, even while I wish to remain humble and open to the input of all involved.

[B229] A few things help keep that in check. One is to judge the method by results people can actually see. Will they see the members safely flourishing without being trapped? Can conflicts get resolved? Are the kids doing well once they grow up? When the method gets a prediction wrong, can the community say so and change course? And if the community is meant to spread, do new projects actually start up and last? Those questions seem more important to me than how big or old the commune gets, or how many decisions are made by consensus.

[B230] Allow for parallel implementations. Fork the method. If the community finds a way to alter Hearthwork and get better results, they've taught me something. If every change they make still needs the founder's “blessing”, then it's not really running in parallel, is it?

[B231] The questions so far have been about the method. These four are about the founder:

[B232] Can the community respectfully override the founder? Can the community replace the role of the founder? Can the community alter the vision of the founder? Can the community exist without the founder?

[B233] Then follow the practical power. Whose name is on the deed? Who's in charge of the housing and the food stores, the vehicles and tools, and any bank account outside the community? Who keeps the records? Who decides who gets admitted, and who picks the people who review the founder? Who controls access to the kids, or to medicine, and who can make a complaint go away? The org chart might look flat and horizontal, while the keys, passwords, titles and even just general reputation all point to one person.

[B234] Remember, it's not an override unless it can actually work on the person in question. If the founder selects the person to review the founder, holds the records, has title to the land, and can kick out complainers, then there's no real way for the community to override the founder, no matter how many meetings it holds.

[B235] Also watch for the daily signs of founderism. Increasing focus on the founder over time. Sense that disagreement is failure. Every bad result is blamed on lack of good implementation. Increasing “insider” knowledge while public accountability decreases. Results are never measured. Sense that former members become morally contaminated the moment they leave.

[B236] Presence of one of these alone isn't proof of abuse, but if several are present, they are worth paying attention to before the founder can explain how the situation is uniquely different in a spiritual way.

[B237] Ask these questions about any community, teacher or method. And ask them about me, if people end up living on land I buy.

[B238] While founderism is a problem in every community, it's especially so in communities which form naturally over time as I'm describing may happen in my case. In such cases, there's no formal community and no formal rules until people decide to come together to make them. It doesn't make sense to do that at first, if, say, I'm the only one there and I don't know who else will be there that I could trust. If there's one other person who comes to visit, it also doesn't make sense, so there's a critical mass point at which people decide "we were just living together like friends, but now we want to formalize the community."

[B239] If I could find a suitable commune to join, that would basically reverse this problem. I would then be the one on probation. But if we are all starting together from scratch, then we would all be at an equal probationary level with each other.

[B240] It's a bit like the process of getting married. At first, you visit your girlfriend or boyfriend when schedules align and each of you agrees. Over time as you get closer to marriage (which hopefully is not a fully unconscious process), you start making decisions together and considering sharing more and more resources. Once married, if you decide to divorce, you split up the resources, although that's also contentious depending on how long the marriage was for, whether one person feels tricked, etc, and unfortunately often ends up in court. Leaving a commune is the divorce half of it, where the shared resources have to get split up. When joining or forming a commune, we can hopefully be more conscious about the process and less romantic.

[B241] Friends who come to visit don't get written terms, though. That's not how anything works, and at first they don't even know if they'll stick around for a day or a week. There are implied terms, like be respectful and be honest, and those shouldn't need to be in writing. If someone is offered something like an internship or a probationary period, that would require written terms. Or if it naturally evolves and they bring up wanting something like that, it could be done.

[B242] ## How My Own Model Could Become a Cult

[B243] [image 16](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!P0dV!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa41679e5-fbf3-48a3-b95b-8236dc943f88_1197x1095.jpeg)

[B244] [caption] I could totally start a cult. Trust me.

[B245] Every practice in this article can be turned against the people using it.

[B246] “Nothing suppressed, everything processed” can become a situation where someone in the group must tell all, even if they don’t want to. I have seen that risk in groups that use the [ZEGG Forum](https://www.theartofrelating.com.au/zegg-forum.html) practice. A group starts out inviting honesty and ends up treating privacy as resistance. But privacy also can be resistance to the communal spirit, so there has to be a dynamic, living, always re-assessed balance between what you and the community owe each other, and what remains your own domain. In general, people can have their own private practices, of course. But some practices go too far against the communal spirit. If someone starts worshipping Satan, say, they get booted.

[B247] Peer counseling can become peer surveillance, even if the tone is warm. And when communal therapy processes are pre-reqs for people to get medicine or to be respected members, it can feel like the community is gatekeeping. Well, it is gatekeeping. Some people simply should not be let in the gate, at least not yet. And some people will resent that, and choose to leave, and that's fine. Some communities will be stricter gatekeepers, and others will be more lax.

[B248] It should go without saying that nobody has to take medicine to have equal respect in the community. But medicine is an optional part of the overall therapy process, and if a member is avoiding the therapy process and becoming hard to deal with, they will naturally not belong at a certain point. If it gets to the point of being impossible to live with, they will be kicked out. But nearly always, people who don't fit in leave way before that.

[B249] They should not be demonized for not trying to fit in. They should be offered compassion, but ultimately it's up to them if they want to fit in and follow the communal processes. And in some cases, the community may make exceptions. If someone is actually sick and needs care, kicking them out could be like a death sentence. It depends on so many factors that I can't decide any of this ahead of time. It's up to the people involved.

[B250] No single person or permanent group of people should handle grievances and have the power to decide who gets into or stays in the community. Each person should have a usable avenue for exit. Dissent is important for growth. That doesn't mean everything everyone says is equally valid, though. Sometimes it really is their wounded ego projection, and that should be acknowledged. But again, balance, because if complaints are too often or too broadly painted as projection, that's as much a failure, if not more so.

[B251] There are some basic human rights that can’t be voted away from you by the majority in a community, unless it's a question of resource availability as a whole. If safety and the conditions for health can be provided, it should be provided. People should be able to maintain relationships with others outside the community, as long as those relationships don't interfere with the community as a whole (for example, you can't bring your boyfriend to live with you if he's not only not a member of the community, but also antagonistic towards it).

[B252] It can be tricky to spell everything out, though. Let's say a grandmother is demonizing the commune. Cutting off contact with her could make things way worse. When it's tricky and dangerous for everyone like that, I think the commune needs to step in and help people decide how to communicate with outsiders. Nobody can give that advice ahead of time, including me. It has to be worked out in the moment, context by context.

[B253] If your human rights are unfairly stripped from you, even by a majority vote, then you can vote with your feet.

[B254] # The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For

[B255] Many perfectly intelligent grownups would rather sit around for six hours talking about solar panels than talk about jealousy, God, death, childhood, money, sex, psychedelics, or somebody wanting to leave, which is what I ask people to do in this article.

[B256] This doesn't surprise me, since we're taught to put on a show of competence in modern society. We're given a lot of incentives to hide ourselves and disappear behind our screens the second things get uncomfortable. Emotional intelligence doesn't pay the bills. Instead we optimize to fit the career market with all the little energy we've got.

[B257] So then a lot of people end up thinking they aren't "good enough" for community, or getting labeled by others that way. And there's a real issue there, but it's more about what fits where & when than good or bad. The community needs to consider how much attention the newcomer currently has available for other people, how willing they are to examine their own reactions. And on the other hand, look at whether the group has enough free resource/capacity to support them without becoming a full-time caretaker operation.

[B258] Some people have engaged in long practices of contemplation or therapy, or have had very good luck in childhood; other people are just trying to survive some emergency in their life and need care more than responsibility. Most of us are all of these at different times in our lives.

[B259] [image 17](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!3uG-!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7daf9ea4-807c-49c1-99b3-6993e837a010_800x540.svg)

[B260] In the model I’m imagining, some of the functions that are outsourced to therapists in modern society are distributed throughout the community. In other words, there’s a lot of [peer counseling](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/hearthwork-an-integrated-peer-counseling) going on, people listening to each other, being there for each other and helping each other process ordinary emotional stuff.

[B261] That does not make the community infinitely absorbent like a Heavy Flow Maxi Pad.

[B262] I’m going to use the phrase “[free resource](https://www.rc.org/),” which comes from Re-evaluation Counseling, to refer to the attention, steadiness, skill and practical capacity people have left over for others once they’ve dealt with enough of their own immediate stuff, and “need” to refer to the amount of help people need at a given time.

[B263] Everyone has a certain amount of both in different ratios.

[B264] My own need has exceeded my free resource at many points. Good Lord. During one stretch, my first wife tried to kill me while psychotic because she believed I was her sociopathic father. Then [she took my son](https://youtu.be/0eOInpVYnsk?si=dbxjP76Rbk5FmjDs). Later she tried to kill my mother, while her pimp stalked my sister’s family until they moved to Canada.

[B265] I could go on and on, but the math is pretty clear by now. There wasn’t much of me left over for anybody else. So when I say someone might need to stabilize and do inner pl/ork before helping found a community, I’m not saying it from a mountaintop.

[B266] The ability to welcome somebody is partly a problem of resources, then, not compassion -- how many people can a group take in without hurting the existing members or the people they are taking in?

[B267] And that also brings us to a difficult catch-22 for fledgling groups. The need for new members in a small community is greater, but the ability of that community to absorb disruption is less. With only eight people in the community, someone still has to grow the food and fix whatever breaks. Somebody has to balance the books and watch the kids. And the water system has to keep working. The water well doesn’t care how beautiful the new member’s heart is.

[B268] So early on, members usually need to have more available resource than acute need, and some practical skills to contribute. They usually need to be willing to learn, too. Once there are 80 members, though, there's usually a lot more free resource to draw on.

[B269] I still don’t know how to keep this language of capacity from becoming a respectable way to exclude people who are disabled, poor, less articulate, maybe simply uncomfortable or unfamiliar with therapeutic language, or for whatever reason deemed inconvenient. Pretending capacity is unlimited won’t solve that. Any real process would need concrete criteria, review by someone who wasn’t part of the decision, and a way to revisit a “not now” decision on membership. Even then, the power to veto membership applications based on capacity remains dangerously tilted towards elitism.

[B270] But before you submit your application to the community, submit it to yourself. This community may not be a good fit for you *right now* if:

[B271] You have a need that far outstrips your free resource right now. This doesn’t mean you’re bad or doomed, it’s about timing; it just means you need to take a first step of finding support where you are and building up your resources. If you don't have a free resource calculator on hand, an easy way to tell is if you’re running from some crisis right now and mainly need a place to land, get treatment, housing, legal aid, etc. A founding group shouldn’t pretend it can give you those.

[B272] You want the feeling of village life but don’t want to take on governance, maintenance, conflict, care, etc.

[B273] You’re not willing to look at yourself when jealousy or conflict comes up.

[B274] Any humane membership process should have ways of not flat-out rejecting people who fall into these categories, or anyone else it can’t take right now, like saying “later,” or “in another role,” or “with more support.”

[B275] # Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community

[B276] Don't worry about getting land yet. If you've got a small handful of people who want to make a community but aren't sure yet, you are already a community, even if it's still an untested one. It may sound cheesy, but it's true all the same.

[B277] Also, don't even think about combining ownership (or any other big resource, for that matter) until you've been through at least one real conflict together and really dealt with it. Not by writing out a vision statement and shaking hands, or any other fake way of "resolving" things, like a truce nobody believes ("let's just leave each other alone"). Dealing with it for real includes the relational pl/ork of each of you naming what really went on, giving each of you time, figuring out what came up for each of you (jealousy, humiliation, fear, old material, whatever), and dealing with it until the emotional pressure changes enough that whatever agreement you come up with means anything. Until then, what's it worth?

[B278] Until that’s happened, you’re all still promising strangers, and you don’t want to take out a mortgage together yet. That’s okay! You can all meet in a rented or borrowed space, or at each other’s houses, long before anyone needs a land surveyor.

[B279] You will also want a budget of what you have in the way of resources. This is the boring stuff. But you will need to know what the group can offer in the way of water, housing, food stores, health care, tools, seasonal labor, transportation, access to markets, schooling, etc. Do you have outside debts to pay? How long will your cash reserves last, if the community uses them? What critical skills will you need? Being great at the emotional stuff won’t stop you from being dead in the water if the one person who knows where everything is and how it works gets too exhausted to keep going.

[B280] ## Please Choose the Values Before Falling in Love With the View

[B281] Have you noticed people often choose their community in the same way they would choose a vacation rental? “Wow! Look at this beautiful land! And a pond, with the sunset in it!! Who needs a beach?! And the people seem really nice, let’s get in here!” Fast forward two years. “Wait, there’s a vaccination policy?? And you need paperwork for it?!?!”

[B282] Bring your values with you when you go to visit a community. Make a list of what you really believe about children, money, property ownership, romance, medicine, food, spiritual practice, technology, privacy, dealing with conflict, leaving the community, etc. It’s a long list, I know. Some of them will just get a question mark, if you’re not sure yet.

[B283] Sexuality and gender belong on there too. Would LGBTQ+ people be able to live openly in your community, date, be in relationships, and stay fully protected by the community? Should children be taught to question their sexual identities from a young age, and what happens if one of the community’s children comes out as gay or transgender? Until you answer these, your community’s values on freedom and on raising children aren’t complete. Yep. Both of them.

[B284] Are you willing to experiment? That’s a real value too. Put it on your list, because the people you live next to might think the experiment ended in 1978, and everything that came out of it is doctrine now.

[B285] Then don’t just listen to what the group says. Look at how they really operate. A recent conflict is a good place to start: how did they handle it? There’s also who can block decisions, and what happened to the last person who left. (And who owns the land!) Does that match your values? It’s uncomfortable to talk about these things, I know, but the view isn’t going anywhere.

[B286] ## Resources, Land, and Exit Terms

[B287] Who will have title to the land? Very important! Whoever has it will have enormous power over the rest of the community. If the deed is in the name of the founder, then he or she is really a landlord with a philosophy. If three out of twelve members own everything, then whether or not your literature says everyone is equal, there are two classes of members.

[B288] What kind of legal entity (cooperative, trust, association, company, whatever fits your jurisdiction) will hold the land? This needs to be decided before it's bought. Then you'll need to decide who has what voting rights, who has what access and use rights to common resources, how you will account for people's contributions (if at all), how inheritance works, what about outside debt, how improvements get handled, and what happens when someone wants to leave the community. Lots of decisions to make!

[B289] And how will you handle the visibility of what the community has? A money-free community still has scarce things and bottlenecks, sorry. It will be important for people to know what's owned or held in common, what you've set aside, what obligations you have to outsiders, who has authority to hand out the major resources, where conflicts of interest are, etc. It won't do to have the transfer of major resources (land, equipment, stores, outside cash, etc.) dependent on one person's memory or permission.

[B290] Good people leave for good reasons -- dying parent, love, work, health, exhaustion, change of mind, etc. You don't want them to lose everything they've put in. That's coercive -- they HAVE to stay if they can't afford to go. Whether or not you use money, you'll need to have a way of providing people with resources when they leave (cash, if you use it, but also tools, transportation, temporary housing, stored up goods, the right to keep using something, or whatever). Otherwise their "right" to leave is only on paper -- it only works when the community happens to have spare money. Write up the exit terms while leaving is still only theoretical -- like a pre-nup when you're planning the wedding. Not romantic, but protective. Much easier than doing it once anybody's furious.

[B291] For the nuts and bolts of all this (members, resources, land, agreements, etc.), I'd go to Diana Leafe Christian's [*Creating a Life Together*](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Ecovillages_and_Low_Impact_Development/Creating_a_Life_Together-Practical_Tools_to_Grow_Ecovillages_and_Intentional_Communities.pdf) (on the reading list at the end).

[B292] ## From Visitor to Member—Slowly

[B293] When [The Farm](https://thefarmcommunity.com/) grew fast, a lot of the people who came were in need, and there weren’t many filters that worked. Being open to everybody felt so spiritual! The practical burden piled up anyway. A membership process is there to protect both the people who are already there and the newcomers.

[B294] First, correspond with them. Did they read the materials? (Not everyone does.) Do they get the basic idea of why this community is here? Are they at least starting to do the relevant practices? It’s a lot cheaper to find out through a thoughtful exchange than after six months of misunderstanding, right?

[B295] The short visit: If they come for a week or two and just participate in normal life, well, most people can be charming for two weeks. Not exactly a high bar. 😅 So that's about all you'll find out.

[B296] The long visit: If you're not around long enough to actually experience inconvenience and conflict with the person in question, then you're not going to learn very much about them. Watch how they react when tired, corrected, attracted, disappointed, bored, etc. (Hopefully not all at once!) Even [a professional faker](http://sidney.u-dont-exist.com/) may be able to pull off an act for months.

[B297] Write down your answers: How long is a provisional membership, and what are a provisional member's rights and responsibilities? How is it reviewed, and how does someone leave it? Who decides on full membership? (Hint: not the person who recruited them! 😜) And what rights does a full member have to the land, the shared resources and a voice in the group, and what do they owe over the long term? (Those go in your founding agreements.)

[B298] At each stage, always judge people based on their conduct. No matter how good it sounds when they talk about their spirituality or their therapy, don’t go by that. What do they actually do? It’s too easy to go by vibes. It’s also easy to think someone’s safe just because they’re going along with everyone else. Six months with someone will give you more data, but there’s still no “good person” detector.

[B299] If someone will have a role in your group that has serious consequences, involving kids, shared resources, medicine, vehicles or private records, you want to know how they handle that specific thing. I wouldn’t hand someone the van keys just because they’re great with kids. You can find out by checking whether you’ve seen them do this thing before, and asking for references if appropriate. Give people access to these things in stages and ensure there’s always some way to appeal decisions about these roles to a separate authority if needed.

[B300] Do stuff together prior to making them provisional members. (Sorry, hanging out doesn't count. I know, it's the fun part.) Can you actually make a decision as a group? Can you actually work together to get something done (an ordinary work or shared-resource task will do)? Can you even process a small conflict afterwards? Do a trial run like that before anybody sells their house to move in. It can show you whether your way of coordinating works.

[B301] Then there are the people who aren't fully "in" the commune politically. Visitors, volunteers, workers, renters and interns at the commune (yes, even interns) are entitled to some rights when decisions really affect their pay or access to common resources. All of them should have clear terms, and the chance to be heard and to have such decisions reviewed. The same is true for decisions really affecting their schedule, safety, housing, records or discipline. Equality that stops at the membership line can end up recreating a servant class that moves from commune to commune.

[B302] ## When Somebody Has to Go

[B303] A safe community can’t promise to keep everyone. There may be times when someone has to go. If someone is a serious or generalized danger to others, or keeps violating essential boundaries, or for whatever reason just makes everyday life together impossible for everyone else, it can be the right call to eject them. If restorative work can help in a reasonable amount of time, all the better. But it’s not the community’s job to be a treatment program for someone who’s endangering everyone else.

[B304] This power to remove people is so easily abused, though. So work out the process before it's about anyone in particular. What counts as cause for immediate protective action? Who decides to put temporary restrictions on someone? Who will make sure all the evidence is preserved? Who will recuse themselves? How will the person be able to reply to the concerns, and have the decision reviewed or appealed without putting others in danger? What protections will there be for the person’s interests (housing, records, family contact, access to common resources, outside funds if any) during the separation? When is it time to bring in outside help? (More on that next.)

[B305] Note that "outside" doesn't have to mean the nation-state. [My research](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/) initially leaned too far that way. The basic need here is for a competent level of authority that is not reviewing its own conduct. That could mean another community, a federation of communities, professionals, autonomous regional institutions, courts, regulatory agencies, emergency services, etc. The key is that it's not the same people. An important correction that came from the Zapatistas: that authority can be an autonomous regional institution, without it turning into the state. And no, autonomy still doesn't mean anyone gets to decide that individual people don't have certain non-waivable rights, or that outside jurisdiction just goes away.

[B306] Also decide on a mediator or ombudsperson in advance. Why bother? People often won't trust someone they haven't already met, who comes in during a crisis as a "neutral stranger." A mediator doesn't obviate the need for evidence, safeguarding, rulings, etc. Mediation is an attempt to restore good relations between people. When people can't or won't consent to being in community any more, they still need a final separation.

[B307] # Dissolution and Exit

[B308] [image 18](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!9Q3c!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fddd2a051-9ac5-4630-868b-5a37226707f9_1536x1024.png)

[B309] Dissolution is something founders rarely want to talk about while they’re picking a name for their community and looking forward to its first harvest. Who would? But that’s exactly when it needs to be talked about.

[B310] If the community dissolves, what happens to the land and any outside debts? What about its buildings, equipment, common stores or funds? What happens to each member’s agreed rights? More immediately, who is responsible for ensuring that everyone has a place to live and food to eat? Who takes responsibility for making sure people get their medications and the other care they need? Who keeps childcare going, and makes sure the kids’ schooling carries on? Who takes responsibility for transportation, for the records, for family contact, for the animals, for making sure land obligations are met, and for any unresolved claims? That’s a lot of loose ends!

[B311] Think of it like a living will, and assign a bridge or successor for each critical function in advance, so basic life doesn’t depend on reviving the commune. The institution may not be salvageable, but who’s making breakfast tomorrow?

[B312] Dissolution terms are also a way for a group to end without turning every friendship into evidence. Who wants that? People may leave hurt, but they keep the transition support they agreed on, and their dignity. And they can tell a coherent story of what happened.

[B313] Institutions and relationships don’t die at the same time, and some of what people learned to give one another may go on too. What a relief! The film *American Commune* follows two sisters who grew up on The Farm. When one of them later became ill, former members who hadn’t seen her in years reached out with support. The Farm’s communal economy had changed dramatically, but the bonds hadn’t vanished.

[B314] My father carried the Bruderhof with him long after he left it. It shaped the foundation he started and the film he made. It shaped the places he took me, too, and the questions I’m still asking (as you may have noticed!).

[B315] # What This Looks Like From Sénégal

[B316] [image 19](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Uv0z!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7db4e048-c31e-445c-9d1d-5c4f48de86ab_340x339.jpeg)

[B317] [caption] The “African Renaissance Monument” in Dakar, Sénégal, which stands at 160 feet tall and took 4 years to build.

[B318] I live in Sénégal, where "intentional community" often sounds like a strange Western phrase. People here are like, "Don't human beings already do that?"

[B319] Sénégal is unlike anywhere else I've lived. Five times a day the prayers pour out into the streets. I find them so soothing. And Sufism doesn't stay inside designated "spiritual" places here. It's in the music, in the pictures everywhere, in the murals, in the names, in the conversations.

[B320] And the humor! People are outside laughing for hours at a time. This is the most joking culture I've ever been in, and I keep wondering if I'll ever understand the really complicated jokes in Wolof. Basic conversation is one thing. But knowing why twelve people are suddenly crying with laughter? That's graduate school.

[B321] In the villages and families I've spent time around, so much is taken care of by the extended family and the local memory, alongside the formal systems. Whether it's land, childcare, housing, food, obligations, etc, etc. Who needs a workshop called "Rebuilding Intergenerational Belonging" when some aunt has already given three people a task?

[B322] Even in Dakar, I found this graffiti on a wall: "Tolerance! Openness! Union! Happiness! Love!" And elsewhere there are murals of Sufi leaders. Spiritual graffiti still surprises me. Where I grew up, a city wall was either selling you a product or showing off somebody's inability to draw a penis.

[B323] [image 20](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!1Hvp!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F98df3b3c-7b43-40df-80d7-112f1bc91d75_849x1037.png)

[B324] [caption] Graffiti in Dakar, Sénégal: Tolerance! Openness! Union! Happiness! Love!

[B325] ## All is not well in paradise

[B326] But does this mean I think Sénégal is some magical place that's kept its communal paradise? No, not quite. The warmth has hard limits here. One noteworthy example is the plight of LGBTQ+ people here, who cross them automatically.

[B327] Sénégal passed a law in March 2026 ([Law No. 2026-08](https://www.humandignitytrust.org/resources/law-no-2026-08-of-march-27-2026-amending-article-319-of-law-no-65-60-of-july-21-1965/)) that increased the penalty for "consensual same-sex intimacy" to 5 to 10 years in prison, and it also made public advocacy for homosexuality a crime. Homosexuals were already facing arrests, family rejection, harassment, assault and mob violence, so ironically, some say they're actually safer once they're arrested, because then at least the mob can't get at them. And also ironically, LGBTQ+ people are using this prejudice in their favor to get refugee status and live in rich countries. Anyway, I'm not going to praise communal belonging and ignore the people who have to hide who they are to survive it, or leave.

[B328] So which is it? Well, in a way, it's both. It's possible for a society to be spiritually alive, generous, funny and super communal, while still becoming cruel to those who break its social categories. Being in community isn't automatically liberating. It all depends on who is allowed to belong without pretending.

[B329] One other shadow side of the otherwise forgiving social culture here is that people may go to great lengths to avoid confrontation. Who stole the sheep? We all know, but nobody wants to cause a stink by bringing it up.

[B330] Where I live, neighbors steal our moringa pods every morning. Every morning! They could plant a moringa tree in their own yard. Apparently maintaining this relationship with ours is more entertaining. 😂

[B331] Everybody here has sheep (even me), and they are super cute. It can feel like you're living in another world, with the music, the animals, the joking, the praying, and just being around people all the time.

[B332] [image 21]

[B333] [Professor Baby Sheep Taught Me Inner Child Shaking Therapy! 😅🫶🏿🐑](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/professor-baby-sheep-taught-me-inner)Copy link

[B334] Of course, the old communal ways here are changing. Young people move to the city, get a job, get a phone, eat imported food, become part of the wider world, etc. (some of the men even make the dangerous journey by sea to Europe). So what about the families? The ones that used to share almost everything are now more reliant on cash and individual opportunities.

[B335] ## Lessons from the Black Tax

[B336] Then there's the so-called "Black Tax," a strange legacy of the old communal system: if you've got any money at all, you're expected to share it with your parents, your siblings, your cousins, to pay school fees, medical bills, funerals, etc. I understand the instinct. In the ancestral way of doing things nobody made it alone, and the people who fed and raised you had a real claim on whatever prosperity came through you later. So why not share in return? That's how the whole organism survived (it wasn't charity).

[B337] Double click to interact with video

[B338] But these days it can be soul destroying. People have a lot less of the communal life that held the obligation up (the shared land, work, childcare, food and daily belonging), but they still carry it. So if one of them makes it into the cash economy, it's really hard to build any independence or long-term stability, because they may be expected to carry everybody. How can they get ahead, when all these people are dependent on them? And the relatives stay dependent too, since the payments don't give them a real alternative. It's the ancestral ethic playing out inside an individualistic cash economy. It can keep people alive, but it eats away at both sides too.

[B339] This is also the thorny boundary issue of living without money inside a money-based society. Life inside the commune can go without cash, but you'll still need money for taxes, hospital bills, visas, school fees, family obligations, buying in tools etc. That doesn't mean going back to individual wages inside the commune, but it does mean someone has to handle it. Who's that going to be? And the commune has to work out how much shared cash it keeps for these things, and how to stop access to outside money from creating a new status hierarchy.

[B340] Another aspect of outside work, even before there's a common purse, is that it's not just money you're bringing into the commune. You're also bringing in a different schedule, a different status system, a different social world, different loyalties, and often different consumption habits etc. etc. And you can't exactly leave all that at the door, can you? In the first stage, where people working outside should contribute an agreed share, it's really important that they can't use the money to buy their way out of everyday communal work, or to buy a greater say in the political system. It's also important that the group notices when outside jobs start draining the people and skills it needs to build the communal livelihoods. Otherwise the commune will stay a dormitory for the money economy instead of moving toward a common purse.

[B341] Some Zapatista communities used to try to stop their young people from leaving, which is a version of the same outside-work problem. Now they talk it through with them instead: it's OK to go and work elsewhere, as long as the migrants keep certain defined obligations to the community. That doesn't make the pull between leaving and belonging go away, but at least now it's something they can name and negotiate. I understand the motivation to negotiate like that, but I think it's not a full solution either.

[B342] Westerners often start out isolated and try to rebuild a tribe. A lot of Africans start out with a communal life they inherited, and watch parts of it fall apart. Kind of the opposite problem. And urban Africans who get interested in intentional community might come to it feeling alienated from both the city and the tradition they left.

[B343] So this is a global issue, far from some Western lifestyle niche. There's still knowledge in the living villages that no book can give you. But if you visit, remember they're people, not museums. Would you like people coming to your house to stare at you and your family? So be respectful, contribute something, learn the language, and don't expect anyone to hand you a spiritual service package.

[B344] Oh, and a practical note: getting my residency in Sénégal was pretty cheap and straightforward. French and Wolof matter a lot more than English. And the country's famous ideal of Teranga (hospitality) is real in my life. I'm staying in a friend's family's house, and they won't let me pay rent! Of course that generosity lives right alongside watchfulness, social conservatism, bureaucracy and all the usual complications of a real country.

[B345] # Practice Comes Before Property

[B346] Most people try and do community in the wrong order. "First let's get land!" "Then let's write down a vision document!" "And whoever's putting in the money can own the place!" And the emotional side of things? That doesn't get done until "later on, when everyone has more time".

[B347] It would be better to get together to practice first, and process a real conflict, and get clear on your values, and test membership, and decide ownership and exit, and then, after all that, think about land. Less cinematic, I know. (It would make for a pretty bad movie.)

[B348] Same with money: do it in stages. Don't try and fly right to the economic destination in one go. Wait until the group can reliably provide for everyone, and fairly decide who gets what from the common, before it goes to a common purse. And work on reducing (or eliminating) outside money based on what the group has shown it CAN do, rather than by declaring "money is impure!" (That sounds kind of weird when somebody else is paying the tax bill.)

[B349] ## If You Can’t Move

[B350] I realize that for most people reading this there are a lot of reasons why they can't just head off and go build community on some land. Jobs, kids, pets, debt, a mortgage, a sick parent, or several of these arranged in a circle... That's fine! The most important pl/ork doesn't need land anyway.

[B351] [image 22](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!LmXZ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8d334efc-a191-40fe-8bbd-164bd0c639f5_4080x3060.jpeg)

[B352] [caption] Too bad you can’t move…

[B353] People sometimes buy forty acres and then bring all their unprocessed stuff with them to a more scenic location. It's a more beautiful view for sure, but now you have the conflict PLUS goats.

[B354] So your inner pl/ork can start now, right where you live. [Peer counseling](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/hearthwork-an-integrated-peer-counseling) takes two people and a private place to sit and talk. And reparenting, somatic work, jealousy work, conflict repair, etc etc? None of it has to wait until somebody has a deed in hand.

[B355] A group that practices together weekly for two years may never own land. So what? It can still make a difference when people are going through a divorce, an illness or grief. Parenting too, and plain old loneliness.

[B356] Here's an idea if you're remote, or stuck where you are: build the part of community you actually can. Have a weekly meal with two households. Set up a counseling exchange with one friend, in person or by video. Share tools or childcare. Transport or food prep work too. Or a sauna. And if you're hippie enough, add acro-yoga, dance improv, a mothers' circle or a [cuddle puddle](http://cuddleparty.com/).

[B357] Trust me on this one. If three households keep showing up for each other like this for six months, you'll learn more than you would from another year of daydreaming about the ideal village.

[B358] ## What to Do This Month

[B359] So what do you actually do this month? Start doing some inner pl/ork, with the free [reparenting guide](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/inner-child-self-love-tips-and-guided) and [Hearthwork manual](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/hearthwork-an-integrated-peer-counseling) or with another serious practice you already trust. And practice before you have an audience.

[B360] Find two or three other people and meet regularly. Call it a practice group, not a commune. Practice listening and switching roles. Get through at least one disagreement too, and keep your commitments. You can talk about a shared future later.

[B361] Where do you find these people? Try an unprogrammed Quaker meeting, a restorative-practice group, an open-hearted congregation, a mature recovery group, a meditation group, or find some people who have gone on long retreats and come back kinder, rather than more impressed with themselves.

[B362] Go visit some real communities. Really, go! [Twin Oaks](https://twinoaks.org/) is one. [East Wind](http://eastwind.org/) is another, and so is [Acorn](https://www.ic.org/directory/acorn-community-farm/), and there are plenty of other [intentional communities](https://www.ic.org/). Lots of [ecovillages](https://ecovillage.org/) are open to visitors too. Check their current visitor requirements first.

[B363] By visiting some communities, you'll get a better idea of how much togetherness you enjoy and what kind of privacy you need, and what kind of labor system irritates you. And whether communal showers feel liberating, or make you start looking at studio apartments! No manifesto is going to tell you any of that!

[B364] Look before you leap. It will really save you some time & money.

[B365] I'm not trying to sell you a retreat or get you to fill out an application or anything. The manuals I mentioned are free. I've just noticed that wherever people are taking real care of each other and playing, feeling useful and having room to become more honest, community tends to gather.

[B368] Maybe some of you will build something useful from this. Maybe that community will teach someone else, who'll build another. Passing it on doesn't need one institution or one founder. And there doesn't have to be one official version of it, either.

[B383] ## For Child-Rearing

[B384] [***The Continuum Concept***](https://www.arvindguptatoys.com/arvindgupta/conconcept.pdf) by Jean Liedloff (1975) is a foundational natural-child-rearing book, based on her time with the Ye’kuana. Is it settled anthropology? No, it's provocative observation, so take it with a grain of salt.

[B385] [***Hunt, Gather, Parent***](https://www.amazon.com/Hunt-Gather-Parent-Ancient-Cultures/dp/1982149671) by Michaeleen Doucleff (2021) is a practical, accessible book about kids taking part in family life. It's also about autonomy, and spreading out the work of caring for them.

[B386] ## Practical Community-Building Guides

[B387] Where do you start with the nuts and bolts? [***Creating a Life Together***](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Ecovillages_and_Low_Impact_Development/Creating_a_Life_Together-Practical_Tools_to_Grow_Ecovillages_and_Intentional_Communities.pdf) by Diana Leafe Christian, for membership, money, land and agreements.

[B388] Want a guide to intentional community that covers the whole system? There's [***Building Belonging***](https://www.ic.org/building-belonging/) by Yana Ludwig.

[B389] ## Governance Manuals

[B390] [***Many Voices One Song***](https://www.sociocracyforall.org/product/many-voices-one-song-ebook/) by Rau and Koch-Gonzalez is a practical manual for doing sociocracy.

[B391] [***Building United Judgment***](https://www.ic.org/community-bookstore/product/building-united-judgment/) by Schaub and colleagues is about consensus decision-making.

[B392] Ludwig and Gimnig's [***The Cooperative Culture Handbook***](https://www.ic.org/community-bookstore/product/the-cooperative-culture-handbook/) has a bunch of exercises for being more cooperative, dealing with conflict and building group culture.

[B393] ## What Helps Communities Last—and Whether Longevity Is Enough

[B394] [***Communities That Abide***](https://philebersole.wordpress.com/2014/06/23/dmitry-orlov-on-communities-that-abide/) by Dmitry Orlov makes an uncomfortable argument: the communities that last a long time often rely on 1) communal property, 2) separation, 3) boundaries and 4) a binding ideology. From a distance, cohesion and capture can look a lot alike.

[B395] Is longevity alone a meaningful measure, when a community may keep going without its members flourishing? That's the question in [“Measuring Success in Intentional Communities”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334857266_Measuring_Success_in_Intentional_Communities_A_Critical_Evaluation_of_Commitment_and_Longevity_Theories), a paper by Rubin, Willis and Ludwig.

[B396] [***Commitment and Community***](https://www.amazon.com/Commitment-Community-Communes-Sociological-Perspective/dp/0674145763) by Rosabeth Moss Kanter is a classic. It compares a bunch of nineteenth-century communes, and the practices that built commitment in them.

[B397] [***Alienation and Charisma***](https://annas-archive.pk/md5/72f23810c1c2ab152c625338eb11c4f5) by Benjamin Zablocki is field research on communes, and on the role charisma plays in them.

[B398] Jo Freeman's [“The Tyranny of Structurelessness”](https://www.jofreeman.com/joreen/tyranny.htm) (1970) is the classic essay on hidden hierarchy. Can it be harder to challenge than explicit authority? She argues it can.

[B399] ## For the Zapatistas

[B400] [***The Fire and the Word***](https://www.amazon.com/Fire-Word-History-Zapatista-Movement/dp/087286488X) by Gloria Muñoz Ramírez and Subcomandante Marcos is about how the Zapatista movement came about, its practice-before-theory approach, how it built autonomous institutions, and the original Good Government Boards.
