# The New Age May Dawn Suddenly

I just put a movie my father made in 1988 up on the Internet for the first time: *Where's Utopia?* It was the result of years of work, traveling to all kinds of communes and communal experiments around the country, trying to answer a question that for him was primary: How do we live together without recreating the same loneliness, domination, and stupidity we were supposedly escaping?

He had already spent 17 years in the Bruderhof, a Christian community that doesn’t use money internally. He had no personal money there: income went into the common purse and the community provided what members needed. So when I talk later about moving beyond internal money, I’m not proposing something nobody has ever lived. My father spent most of two decades doing it. They also accepted him as a Jewish atheist, making him a remarkably long-running doctrinal exception. In meditation there, he came to his own understanding that “God is the Universe, and the Universe is Love.”

[an image]

[caption] This was the banner my mom painted that hung in our living room for decades. I will try to locate the actual photo.

He might have stayed, but the U.S. antiwar movement during Vietnam needed somebody who knew how to set up printing presses. He eventually founded the Aquarian Research Foundation—not a commune, but a nonprofit devoted to researching how the planet could have a positive future and publishing what it found. His conclusion was simple and enormous: human beings need small tribal communities again, because Civilization Is A Hoax, as I blogged about at the beginning of the Covid plandemic.

That’s where the ***escuelita*** in my title comes in. *Escuelita* means “little school.” I’m borrowing the word from the Zapatistas in Mexico, whose *Escuelita* brought outsiders into their communities for a while to learn autonomy by living it. In the same way, people would live in the healing communities I’m proposing, to heal and to learn the relational and practical methods, so that some of them could eventually leave and set up another one somewhere else. Otherwise, we’ve built a lovely refuge for whoever got there first.

[an image]

[A link card: "Where's Utopia?" (Click READ MORE): My father's 1988 documentary on Communal Living in USA, online for first time ever!]

I spent much of my childhood being carried through Twin Oaks, Acorn, East Wind, Padanaram, Zendik, and many other communities. I was usually just a visitor’s kid, close enough to see how people lived but never quite part of the tribe. At the time that meant a lot of boredom, with some adventures mixed in, but those years gave me a great sample to compare against.

From 1999 to 2001, my family actually lived with the Love Israel family in Washington while escaping Y2K, that brief historical period when otherwise reasonable adults prepared for civilization to be defeated by a two-digit date. We lived in a yurt. There were wild berries of every kind, and I loved being in nature. I was also lonely, bored, irritated by the cult programming, and missing my friends in Philadelphia.

I wanted to befriend the children there, but they treated me as an outsider. My mom never liked the place. The leader was also named Love Israel, and to me he seemed like the fakest person I’d ever met, complete with the expected God complex.

We spent a lot of time on our knees in the community garden growing food they could sell in town. The money was opaque and seemed to travel mostly upward toward Love. Meanwhile everybody sang, “Here in the garden, there’s nothing left to do but eat sleep and keep clean.” Again and again. 🌱

My father eventually gave the community his airplane. They sold it instead of using it. He felt tricked, began exposing what was happening, and then we were asked to leave.

The East Wind commune was almost the opposite way. There were maybe eighty people when I was there, enough to have a variety of personalities, small enough to actually make friends. I was happy there. I especially liked showering in a communal shower with both sexes present. I wonder if that still goes on at East Wind? It's the sort of thing that doesn't make it into the history books.

Even at East Wind, though, there was a quieter power issue. They had a nut butter factory that brought in a lot of their income, which seemed to make the woman running it very hard to question. Otherwise I thought the community had a great social life, a nice way of distributing labor, and no obvious guru.

There were like eight people at Acorn when I was there, and no kids. I couldn't tell whether it was a commune I might join or a group of people who were already friends, and who happened to live on some really beautiful land and be good at building cool stuff. Joining would have felt less like applying to a commune than asking whether their group chat had an opening.

Twin Oaks was much bigger, like two or three hundred people at the time, and almost institutional. It was friendly and interesting, but I didn't feel like I was in a tribe there. It was more like a tiny hippie town, but with a highly structured political system that didn't seem intuitive for hippies.

Looking back, almost none of these communities had a communal therapy practice. People brought the usual human pain and suffering with them, and it all got translated into politics.

Which meant weekly meetings, run according to Robert's Rules of Order, with enormous agendas, where most of the things you needed to talk about couldn't be talked about, and you generally felt like you'd rather be sleeping. (Some people, including me, solved this problem by just going to sleep in the meeting and openly snoring.)

Zendik went in the other direction. They held a brutal community meeting that I’m still recovering from 35 years later. 🤣 Five-year-old me was put in the center and told I was bad because I hadn’t volunteered to help adults with whatever jobs they were doing. Apparently kindergarten had already failed to teach me labor consciousness.

The secular communities often buried hurt under procedure. The spiritual communities often put one person in charge of everybody’s inner life. Most of the rest of this article is my attempt to describe the missing third option.

# Communes Are Hot Again 🔥

Suddenly the longing behind my father’s movie is everywhere again, and in 2023 the U.S. Surgeon General even put out an advisory about it. Or about the loneliness under it, at least. The advisory said roughly half of American adults experience loneliness, and it treated social connection as a public-health issue.

Whether you want to call that loneliness an official “epidemic” or not, it’s obvious that a lot of people can’t tolerate modern life in isolation anymore. They may disagree about politics, food, God, sex, or whether shoes are oppressive, but they agree that something about the current arrangement is starvin’ them.

Check out the Google Trends for “ecovillage” and “intentional community”. After years of decline, interest climbed sharply in the mid-2020s.

[an image]

I have no proof that the rise of AI caused this turn, but it’s hard to ignore the coincidence in timing. AI became serious around this time, and it started changing people’s jobs and relationships, and how they see the future. Suddenly, forming a village sounded like a good backup plan, not just a hippie utopian whim.

It’s poppin’ on Instagram too! 🔥 Chantress Seba—my favorite light-language love influencer—posted an application for people interested in starting a commune. I applied, although I don’t think she’s coming to Africa, where I'm at. When I saw the post, many thousands of people appeared to be interested. Imagine opening your inbox after casually inviting a million followers to co-found a village.

Later, when another influencer with a big following floated the commune idea, the comment section started to become a recruitment board. It was chaotic, but sincere, and it was revealing. There were people in the comments finding prospective members the way you’d usually find a used sofa.

### The Freeloader Problem

Some of the commenters weren’t trying to join. The oldest objection, that maybe most people don’t want to take on the responsibility that anarchism requires and communal property becomes “owned by everyone, cared for by nobody,” showed up almost right away.

[an image]

The commune comments became a miniature political-philosophy seminar, as Instagram comments sometimes do.

I'd say the comment above is more true of the big socialism experiments than small communes, but I still take it seriously, because some people really would rather let somebody else take responsibility. When everybody owns everything, it can get to where nobody knows who's supposed to do what, and the same three people end up doing all the maintenance while everybody else talks about freedom. And then unofficial leaders start gaining power. Partly that's because everyone keeps handing it to them.

Communal ownership entails folks taking on visible and named stewardship duties. It requires knowing what to do if someone is leeching off the group and how to deal with it. It requires developing healthier forms of relationships. No, taking away the idea of a boss does not make everyone less passive, selfish, thieving, nor suddenly unlearning learned helplessness.

People want to go back to the old way of living, but they have no idea why earlier communes failed or what to do when someone wants to own land, the group runs out of money, a baby is born or jealousy rears its head. They just know they are tired and alone and want to change it.

# Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways

[P1] Most communes of my acquaintance went wrong from the inside, in one of two ways.

## The secular failure

[P2] The secular communes had a solution for how to distribute property or labor, but when the deep waters of jealousy or childhood hurt or humiliation or attraction or resentment or grief came up, the group was without a common method for dealing with it. The feelings just became agenda items.

[P3] Meeting procedure isn’t going to solve every problem, no matter how sophisticated the system of labor and money and decision making may be in a community of the Federation of Egalitarian Communities or any other income-sharing community. It can’t make it so that two people don’t hate each other. It can only decide whose turn it is to talk about the hatred, and whether a motion about it needs somebody to second it.

[P4] Eventually, when people are together long enough, politics will become a way of carrying emotional material that people can’t name. There will be a fight about a schedule or committee or noise or food or some policy or other, but it won’t be about that. There’s no shared way to talk about the hurt itself, but the meeting agenda is the one place the group lets you push for something and win, so the angry communard goes there, trying to get some justice.

## The spiritual failure

[P5] This comes about when the community realizes the importance of embracing a transformation from within, and then funnels it through a particular person, usually the founder, a teacher, a channel or some other special knower of how things really are.

[P6] The Farm, in Tennessee, at its largest about 1,500 people, had many beautiful values. But in the early days, the community’s spirituality and social life followed the founder, Stephen Gaskin. From what former members have shared, the community’s doctrine ran deep into members’ intimate decisions. This is how it often goes: folks talk about oneness with the universe, while one man defines what the universe is.

[P7] The two failures mirror each other. Secular groups distribute power but leave the inner life mostly private or ignored. Spiritual groups attempt to address the inner life, but because it's the inner world being shaped by the outer as a condition of membership, we tend to find both real-world and spiritual power concentrating around whoever defines the path.

[P8] The deeper problem is that somebody who's earned authority in one role can use it in all their other roles. The founder who is a gifted teacher, the therapist who really helps people, the farmer or business manager that actually carries a lot of the economy. The danger starts when one person has the ability to define the problem, control the information related to the problem, define what the solution is, and make it hard for people to leave or express disagreement.

[P9] I dream of a way to work in depth that isn’t run by a guru, and a way of being in a community with distributed authority. A place where people who've done the healing first can join without pretending they're fully finished, and that also has peer practice and enough play that it doesn’t become a permanent repair shop.
