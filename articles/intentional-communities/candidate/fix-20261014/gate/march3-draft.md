Ok, so on a personal level, it makes more sense to me to focus on cultivating more human independence from the technosphere, all the machines and systems modern life runs on, than to wait for certainty. It's better to have community and find out I didn't need a way out of the technosphere than to need one after spending ten years optimizing my social media feed, right?

I also reject the suburban panic that treats every warm relationship between a child and an unrelated adult as suspicious. It's good for kids to have a whole bunch of grownups who know them and enjoy them, who would notice when something changes, and who would act when something's wrong. When adults panic like that, kids can lose all those grownups without the panic actually preventing abuse.

Make sure kids have relationships and a reporting route outside the community. Grandparents, cousins, local friends, teachers and other trusted adults give them perspective on their life, and someone to talk to. Make sure there's at least one trusted adult, advocate or reporting route they can reach without going through the community's leadership. In 1984, Vermont authorities raided a religious community in Island Pond and took 112 kids into custody over suspected abuse. A judge turned down the state's blanket request to hold them, because it couldn't name any specific case, and the kids went home that same day. State intervention can fail spectacularly, and enclosing kids completely in a community can fail too. Kids need more than one world.

Then follow the practical power. Whose name is on the deed? Who's in charge of the housing and the food stores, the vehicles and tools, and any bank account outside the community? Who keeps the records? Who decides who gets admitted, and who picks the people who handle complaints? Who controls access to the kids, or to medicine, and who can make a complaint go away? The org chart might look flat and horizontal, while the keys, passwords, titles and even just general reputation all point to one person.

Sometimes we also need to protect people from the rare, but real threat posed by dangerous children. I looked through all the research I'd gathered for a well-documented case of a child in an intentional community who remained seriously dangerous to others. I was looking to find the reported conduct, the assessment, the intervention, the review, and what happened later. I couldn't find one. It's possible that records are private, or that cases were referred elsewhere. Whatever the reason, I can't claim communities already know how to handle the hardest cases on their own. In those cases, the child, the family, and the people at risk all need access to competent help and review, hopefully including non-intrusive help from outside the home community.
