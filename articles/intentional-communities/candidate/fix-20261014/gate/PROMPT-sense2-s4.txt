# The New Age May Dawn Suddenly

I just put a movie my father made in 1988 up on the Internet for the first time: *Where's Utopia?* It was the result of years of work, traveling to all kinds of communes and communal experiments around the country, trying to answer a question that for him was primary: How do we live together without recreating the same loneliness, domination, and stupidity we were supposedly escaping?

He had already spent 17 years in the Bruderhof, a Christian community that doesn’t use money internally. He had no personal money there: income went into the common purse and the community provided what members needed. So when I talk later about moving beyond internal money, I’m not proposing something nobody has ever lived. My father spent most of two decades doing it. They also accepted him as a Jewish atheist, making him a remarkably long-running doctrinal exception. In meditation there, he came to his own understanding that “God is the Universe, and the Universe is Love.”

[an image]

[caption] This was the banner my mom painted that hung in our living room for decades. I will try to locate the actual photo.

He might have stayed, but the U.S. antiwar movement during Vietnam needed somebody who knew how to set up printing presses. He eventually founded the Aquarian Research Foundation—not a commune, but a nonprofit devoted to researching how the planet could have a positive future and publishing what it found. His conclusion was simple and enormous: human beings need small tribal communities again, because Civilization Is A Hoax, as I blogged about at the beginning of the Covid plandemic.

That’s where the ***escuelita*** in my title comes in. *Escuelita* means “little school.” I’m borrowing the word from the Zapatistas in Mexico, whose *Escuelita* brought outsiders into their communities for a while to learn autonomy by living it. In the same way, people would live in the healing communities I’m proposing, to heal and to learn the relational and practical methods, so that some of them could eventually leave and set up another one somewhere else. Otherwise, we’ve built a lovely refuge for whoever got there first.

[A link card: "Where's Utopia?" (Click READ MORE): My father's 1988 documentary on Communal Living in USA, online for first time ever!]

I spent much of my childhood being carried through Twin Oaks, Acorn, East Wind, Padanaram, Zendik, and many other communities. I was usually just a visitor’s kid, close enough to see how people lived but never quite part of the tribe. At the time that meant a lot of boredom, with some adventures mixed in, but those years gave me a great sample to compare against.

From 1999 to 2001, my family actually lived with the Love Israel family in Washington while escaping Y2K, that brief historical period when otherwise reasonable adults prepared for civilization to be defeated by a two-digit date. We lived in a yurt. There were wild berries of every kind, and I loved being in nature. I was also lonely, bored, irritated by the cult programming, and missing my friends in Philadelphia.

I wanted to befriend the children there, but they treated me as an outsider. My mom never liked the place. The leader was also named Love Israel, and to me he seemed like the fakest person I’d ever met, complete with the expected God complex.

We spent a lot of time on our knees in the community garden growing food they could sell in town. The money was opaque and seemed to travel mostly upward toward Love. Meanwhile everybody sang, “Here in the garden, there’s nothing left to do but eat sleep and keep clean.” Again and again. 🌱

My father eventually gave the community his airplane. They sold it instead of using it. He felt tricked, began exposing what was happening, and then we were asked to leave.

The East Wind commune was almost the opposite way. There were maybe eighty people when I was there, enough to have a variety of personalities, small enough to actually make friends. I was happy there. I especially liked showering in a communal shower with both sexes present. I wonder if that still goes on at East Wind? It's the sort of thing that doesn't make it into the history books.

Even at East Wind, though, there was a quieter power issue. They had a nut butter factory that brought in a lot of their income, which seemed to make the woman running it very hard to question. Otherwise I thought the community had a great social life, a nice way of distributing labor, and no obvious guru.

There were like eight people at Acorn when I was there, and no kids. I couldn't tell whether it was a commune I might join or a group of people who were already friends, and who happened to live on some really beautiful land and be good at building cool stuff. Joining would have felt less like applying to a commune than asking whether their group chat had an opening.

Twin Oaks was much bigger, like two or three hundred people at the time, and almost institutional. It was friendly and interesting, but I didn't feel like I was in a tribe there. It was more like a tiny hippie town, but with a highly structured political system that didn't seem intuitive for hippies.

Looking back, almost none of these communities had a communal therapy practice. People brought the usual human pain and suffering with them, and it all got translated into politics.

Which meant weekly meetings, run according to Robert's Rules of Order, with enormous agendas, where most of the things you needed to talk about couldn't be talked about, and you generally felt like you'd rather be sleeping. (Some people, including me, solved this problem by just going to sleep in the meeting and openly snoring.)

Zendik went in the other direction. They held a brutal community meeting that I’m still recovering from 35 years later. 🤣 Five-year-old me was put in the center and told I was bad because I hadn’t volunteered to help adults with whatever jobs they were doing. Apparently kindergarten had already failed to teach me labor consciousness.

The secular communities often buried hurt under procedure. The spiritual communities often put one person in charge of everybody’s inner life. Most of the rest of this article is my attempt to describe the missing third option.

# Communes Are Hot Again 🔥

Suddenly the longing behind my father’s movie is everywhere again, and in 2023 the U.S. Surgeon General even put out an advisory about it. Or about the loneliness under it, at least. The advisory said roughly half of American adults experience loneliness, and it treated social connection as a public-health issue.

Whether you want to call that loneliness an official “epidemic” or not, it’s obvious that a lot of people can’t tolerate modern life in isolation anymore. They may disagree about politics, food, God, sex, or whether shoes are oppressive, but they agree that something about the current arrangement is starvin’ them.

Check out the Google Trends for “ecovillage” and “intentional community”. After years of decline, interest climbed sharply in the mid-2020s.

[an image]

I have no proof that the rise of AI caused this turn, but it’s hard to ignore the coincidence in timing. AI became serious around this time, and it started changing people’s jobs and relationships, and how they see the future. Suddenly, forming a village sounded like a good backup plan, not just a hippie utopian whim.

It’s poppin’ on Instagram too! 🔥 Chantress Seba—my favorite light-language love influencer—posted an application for people interested in starting a commune. I applied, although I don’t think she’s coming to Africa, where I'm at. When I saw the post, many thousands of people appeared to be interested. Imagine opening your inbox after casually inviting a million followers to co-found a village.

Later, when another influencer with a big following floated the commune idea, the comment section started to become a recruitment board. It was chaotic, but sincere, and it was revealing. There were people in the comments finding prospective members the way you’d usually find a used sofa.

### The Freeloader Problem

Some of the commenters weren’t trying to join. The oldest objection, that maybe most people don’t want to take on the responsibility that anarchism requires and communal property becomes “owned by everyone, cared for by nobody,” showed up almost right away.

[an image]

The commune comments became a miniature political-philosophy seminar, as Instagram comments sometimes do.

I'd say the comment above is more true of the big socialism experiments than small communes, but I still take it seriously, because some people really would rather let somebody else take responsibility. When everybody owns everything, it can get to where nobody knows who's supposed to do what, and the same three people end up doing all the maintenance while everybody else talks about freedom. And then unofficial leaders start gaining power. Partly that's because everyone keeps handing it to them.

Communal ownership entails folks taking on visible and named stewardship duties. It requires knowing what to do if someone is leeching off the group and how to deal with it. It requires developing healthier forms of relationships. No, taking away the idea of a boss does not make everyone less passive, selfish, thieving, nor suddenly unlearning learned helplessness.

People want to go back to the old way of living, but they have no idea why earlier communes failed or what to do when someone wants to own land, the group runs out of money, a baby is born or jealousy rears its head. They just know they are tired and alone and want to change it.

# Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways

Most communes of my acquaintance went wrong from the inside, in one of two ways.

## The secular failure

The secular communes had a solution for how to distribute property or labor, but when the deep waters of jealousy or childhood hurt or humiliation or attraction or resentment or grief came up, the group was without a common method for dealing with it. The feelings just became agenda items.

Meeting procedure isn’t going to solve every problem, no matter how sophisticated the system of labor and money and decision making may be in a community of the Federation of Egalitarian Communities or any other income-sharing community. It can’t make it so that two people don’t hate each other. It can only decide whose turn it is to talk about the hatred, and whether a motion about it needs somebody to second it.

Eventually, when people are together long enough, politics will become a way of carrying emotional material that people can’t name. There will be a fight about a schedule or committee or noise or food or some policy or other, but it won’t be about that. There’s no shared way to talk about the hurt itself, but the meeting agenda is the one place the group lets you push for something and win, so the angry communard goes there, trying to get some justice.

## The spiritual failure

This comes about when the community realizes the importance of embracing a transformation from within, and then funnels it through a particular person, usually the founder, a teacher, a channel or some other special knower of how things really are.

The Farm, in Tennessee, at its largest about 1,500 people, had many beautiful values. But in the early days, the community’s spirituality and social life followed the founder, Stephen Gaskin. From what former members have shared, the community’s doctrine ran deep into members’ intimate decisions. This is how it often goes: folks talk about oneness with the universe, while one man defines what the universe is.

The two failures mirror each other. Secular groups distribute power but leave the inner life mostly private or ignored. Spiritual groups attempt to address the inner life, but because it's the inner world being shaped by the outer as a condition of membership, we tend to find both real-world and spiritual power concentrating around whoever defines the path.

The deeper problem is that somebody who's earned authority in one role can use it in all their other roles. The founder who is a gifted teacher, the therapist who really helps people, the farmer or business manager that actually carries a lot of the economy. The danger starts when one person has the ability to define the problem, control the information related to the problem, define what the solution is, and make it hard for people to leave or express disagreement.

I dream of a way to work in depth that isn’t run by a guru, and a way of being in a community with distributed authority. A place where people who've done the healing first can join without pretending they're fully finished, and that also has peer practice and enough play that it doesn’t become a permanent repair shop.

# The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together

[an image]

[P1] Communities tend to focus on the aspect of their project that was most interesting to the original founders, for example political types getting super into writing by-laws, or spiritual types just meditating all the time, or therapists processing each other, or people who like psychedelics discovering they’re all God together and never getting to the point of deciding who will put their name on the deed.

[P2] I’ve broken the model I propose down into the 4 parts shown in the above diagram. Note, Pl/ork means play + work. Calling therapy “inner work” forgets the inner child, who also wants to play. (Forgive the neologism. I hope *pl/ork* isn’t too corny. 🌽): 1) inner pl/ork (reparenting), 2) relational pl/ork (peer counseling especially), 3) medicine pl/ork (like loveyhuasca or iboga), and 4) the practical logistical foundation which is most of this article, since I have guides separately on the other parts. Obviously the details are complicated, and jealousy, children, money, land, God, and iboga don’t fit neatly into four boxes, but let’s go with it.

[P3] These four parts leak into one another almost immediately. Governance can end up slyly incorporating personal tensions like jealousy. Those who have positions with more control of shared resources will automatically be affected by that psychologically (power corrupts, as they say). Those who know medicine tend to have another type of god complex since they may make life or death decisions for people routinely. How folks choose to raise children can expose values the founders never discussed. And myriad other categories arise, of course.

[P4] And let's face it: food, housing, and logistics are not enough reason to bind whole lives together. That's more like a situationship. A community needs a shared sacred inner practice that gives the difficulty of our life together a deeper meaning. We help each other heal and choose love over fear. And we turn outward with a purpose, using the *escuelita* model to bring our healing to the world.

## Founded on Freedom

[P5] Nothing suppressed, everything processed. Each of us guides our own practice, and our practices weave the community into the quilt that keeps us all warm and safe. Whatever answers we find stay fluid and organic, and don't freeze into doctrine.

[P6] Speaking of dynamically weaving ourselves together, though, a community does need some stable agreements that they're not tweaking and revising every time somebody has a revelation in breathwork. But these agreements need to stay open to lived experience.

[P7] When agreements get frozen, religious communities tend to call the frozen version doctrine. Secular communities on the other hand often call it “the process,” which can become just as sacred.

[P8] To give an example of striking the balance: My contemplative practice is grounded in the early Buddhist suttas, with a critical reading. Most are treasure, while some are obviously late additions (sometimes to the point of real absurdity). A living community should relate to its own teachings in that same critical manner while staying devoted to following what they've decided on so far.

### 1. Inner pl/ork

[P9] Probably the most obvious type of pl/ork is reparenting. Here’s an example: you teach your inner child geography, while s/he teaches you the world is a cat!

[an image]

[P10] Inner-child reparenting means learning to identify as the best inner adult, which is comprised of the three inner adult roles: Nurturer, Protector, and Guide. The newly developed adult reparents their newly recognized inner child. The child receives all that love, protection, and guidance that you give it, and in this process actually gains more freedom to remain alive inside you.

[P11] And inner pl/ork is built on somatic regulation first. Listening to the body's feelings before making a decision with the mind. And that actually symbolizes the adult/child relationship to me in some way as well.

### 2. Relational pl/ork

[P12] Hearthwork distributes counseling among peers: equal time, attention without interpretation or fixing, and then you switch.

[P13] The idea is that the capacity to be there for people when they need help isn’t located in one professional or guru, or the founder of the group that everyone emotionally depends on, but in the community as a whole.

[P14] But since we're merging peer therapy with community, we also need to make sure that the person caring about you and listening to you shouldn't translate that information into administration over your life, the way a professional therapist might diagnose you as this or that and then suddenly you're ineligible for certain careers. Although obviously that information can come out regardless when people are in close quarters.

[P15] Another thing is knowing which problems are beyond what peers can handle and need outside or expert help. And another is to deal with jealousy and conflict while it's still between the people involved, before it gets put on the agenda (generally at the bottom of the agenda). Speaking of jealousy, check out my Romance Guide, since that's a topic each person & each couple first handles by themselves.

### 3. Medicine pl/ork

[P16] Community can reach new heights by carefully incorporating serious plant medicines with peer guidance rather than exalting any one person as a special shaman.

[P17] This doesn’t mean that a peer-led ceremony should be done without structure. Things like screening participants, making sure the sitters are sober, having a plan for dealing with emergencies, planning for integration, and other rules and boundaries need to be dealt with beforehand. While there may be some pure learning that could come from having several altered members figure out what rules and boundaries are needed on the spot, it’s not something I recommend.

### 4. The container

[P18] This is the part about membership, land, resources, governance, children, leaving, etc.

[P19] Like my father, I’m aiming for communities with less and less money involved, since money is antagonistic to the spirit of community. If I have money, I can get whatever I want, regardless of whether anyone cares about my wellbeing. That's the hegemonic philosophy of life we are trying to overturn. I think the path there has three stages.

[P20] First there will be no money exchanged between members for basic community functions like food, housing, care, tools, education, transport etc. Members might have outside jobs, and they bring an agreed share of that income in. This is probably a good place to start if someone wants to create a community now and have it be realistic.

[P21] Later all wages and other income earned by the members from work outside the community, or from business inside the community, will be paid into the common purse of the community. No member accumulates private wealth while a member of the community. Some version of this is how things have been done in the Bruderhof community for generations, and my father lived there for 17 years. It is also done, in a somewhat different way, at Twin Oaks, and a number of other income-sharing communities. So a common purse isn’t just a theory.

[P22] Eventually, the community would also stop using money outside. People would meet their needs directly, or through exchange (be it trade, gift, work, obligation, or whatever). I couldn't find a well-documented modern community that has gone this far, and in fact, it may be impractical in some locations because of government requirements like taxes on land. So perhaps the practical end point here is a community that is nearly entirely moneyless, but has a small collective cash boundary on the outside.

[P23] None of this does away with scarcity or economic power. There are lots of areas where such power can be wielded: access to food storage, housing, tools, land, medicine, transportation, and the outside world’s currency, to name a few. Even in a moneyless community, someone can control what's scarce. Who decides how these things are distributed? What do members owe one another? What happens when someone wants to leave?

[P24] These are less exciting topics to discuss spiritually. It's rare to see someone come back from an ecstatic vision with news of how to revamp the community’s policies on provisional members, or how to divvy up assets with a departing member, or how to prevent one founder from having control over the land, or what to do when a child reports something serious. And if they do, it's still something that needs a sober thinking-through.

[P25] But no amount of gorgeous land will make up for the mess that is created when one person owns the land or has the final say about what will happen on it. And when members come on board faster than the group can support them, all the peer counseling in the world won't make up for it, any more than medicine makes up for the lack of a written exit agreement.

[P26] The first three parts are the inside of the container, and this container is what gives those a stable place to manifest.

[P27] If one of them gets skipped, it will need to be dealt with eventually, at high stress, usually after the group has invested land, labor, or other resources and everyone has an opinion on how things should be.
