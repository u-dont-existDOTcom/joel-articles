SECTION: The New Age May Dawn Suddenly

PUBLISHED:

I recently put my father’s 1988 film, Where’s Utopia?, online for the first time. He made it in 1988 after spending years visiting communal experiments around the United States and asking a question that never really left him: how could human beings live together without reproducing the same loneliness, domination, and stupidity we were supposedly escaping?

He had already spent 17 years in the Bruderhof, a Christian community that doesn’t use money internally. He had no personal money there: income went into the common purse and the community provided what members needed. So when I talk later about moving beyond internal money, I’m not proposing something nobody has ever lived. My father spent most of two decades doing it. They also accepted him as a Jewish atheist, making him a remarkably long-running doctrinal exception. In meditation there, he came to his own understanding that “God is the Universe, and the Universe is Love.”

[caption] This was the banner my mom painted that hung in our living room for decades. I will try to locate the actual photo.

He might have stayed, but the U.S. antiwar movement during Vietnam needed somebody who knew how to set up printing presses. He eventually founded the Aquarian Research Foundation—not a commune, but a nonprofit devoted to researching how the planet could have a positive future and publishing what it found. His conclusion was simple and enormous: human beings need small tribal communities again, because Civilization Is A Hoax, as I blogged about at the beginning of the Covid plandemic.

That’s where the **escuelita** in my title comes in. Escuelita means “little school.” I’m borrowing the word from the Zapatistas, whose Escuelita brought outsiders into their communities to learn autonomy by living it. The healing communities I’m proposing should do the same: people live there, heal, learn the relational and practical methods, and some eventually leave to start the next one. Otherwise, we’ve built a lovely refuge for whoever got there first.

That’s how I spent much of my childhood being carried through Twin Oaks, Acorn, East Wind, Padanaram, Zendik, and many other experiments. I was usually the visitor’s kid, close enough to see how people lived but never quite part of the tribe. At the time this mostly meant boredom. In retrospect, it gave me a strangely useful comparison sample.

From 1999 to 2001, my family actually lived with the Love Israel family in Washington while escaping Y2K, that brief historical period when otherwise reasonable adults prepared for civilization to be defeated by a two-digit date. We lived in a yurt. There were wild berries of every kind, and I loved being in nature. I was also lonely, bored, irritated by the cult programming, and missing my friends in Philadelphia.

I wanted to befriend the children there, but they treated me as an outsider. My mom never liked the place. The leader was also named Love Israel, and to me he seemed like the fakest person I’d ever met, complete with the expected God complex.

We spent a lot of time on our knees in the community garden growing food they could sell in town. The money was opaque and seemed to travel mostly upward toward Love. Meanwhile everybody sang, “Here in the garden, there’s nothing left to do but eat sleep and keep clean.” Again and again. 🌱

My father eventually gave the community his airplane. They sold it instead of using it. He felt tricked, began exposing what was happening, and then we were asked to leave.

East Wind felt almost like the opposite social experience. It had roughly eighty people when I visited: large enough to have real variety, small enough that friendship still felt possible. I was happy there. I also loved the mixed-sex communal showers, and I still wonder whether those survived. These are the questions history books neglect.

East Wind still had a quieter power problem. The woman directing the nut-butter factory brought in much of the community’s income, which seemed to make her unusually difficult to question. The group had a warm social life, distributed labor, and no obvious guru, but the factory’s importance still affected who could be challenged.

Acorn had something like eight people when I visited. There were no children there. I couldn’t tell whether it was a commune I might join or a group of friends who happened to own beautiful land and build lovely things together. Joining an established group of eight friends felt less like applying to a community than asking whether their group chat had an opening.

Twin Oaks was much larger—perhaps two or three hundred people at the time—and felt almost institutional. It was friendly and interesting, but I didn’t feel a tribe. It felt more like a tiny hippie town with an unusually complicated labor-credit system.

Across nearly all of these places, the same absence kept appearing: no communal therapy practice. Human pain still entered the group, of course. It simply got translated into politics.

I remember weekly meetings run under Robert’s Rules of Order, with enormous agendas, little room for what anyone actually needed to discuss, and the persistent thought, “I’d rather be sleeping.” Sometimes people solved that conflict by snoring through the meeting.

Zendik went in the other direction. They held a brutal community meeting that I’m still recovering from 35 years later. 🤣 Five-year-old me was put in the center and told I was bad because I hadn’t volunteered to help adults with whatever jobs they were doing. Apparently kindergarten had already failed to teach me labor consciousness.

The secular communities often buried hurt under procedure. The spiritual communities often put one person in charge of everybody’s inner life. Most of the rest of this article is my attempt to describe the missing third option.

SECTION: Communes Are Hot Again 🔥

PUBLISHED:

The longing that shaped my father’s film is suddenly everywhere again. The U.S. Surgeon General’s 2023 advisory reported that roughly half of American adults experience loneliness and treated social connection as a public-health issue rather than a sentimental extra.

I’m less interested in declaring an official “epidemic” than in the obvious fact underneath it: a lot of people can’t tolerate isolated modern life anymore. They may disagree about politics, food, God, sex, and whether shoes are oppressive, but they agree that something about the current arrangement is starving them.

Look at Google Trends for “ecovillage” and “intentional community.” Interest had been drifting downward for years, then bent upward sharply in the mid-2020s.

I can’t prove AI caused that turn. The timing is still hard to ignore. Around the same period, AI stopped being a tech-news curiosity and began reorganizing people’s jobs, feeds, relationships, and expectations of the future. Suddenly “maybe we should form a village” sounded less like a 1972 leftover and more like a backup plan.

It’s poppin’ on Instagram too! 🔥 Chantress Seba—my favorite light-language love influencer—posted an application for people interested in starting a commune. I applied, although I don’t think she’s coming to Africa. When I saw the post, many thousands of people appeared to be interested. Imagine opening your inbox after casually inviting a million followers to co-found a village.

Another large influencer later floated the commune idea, and the comments began turning into a recruitment board. People were finding prospective members under a post the way they might normally find a used sofa. It was chaotic, sincere, and revealing.

### The Freeloader Problem

Not everybody in the comments was trying to join. The oldest objection arrived almost immediately: maybe most people don’t want the responsibility anarchism requires, and communal property becomes “owned by everyone, cared for by nobody.”

The commune comments became a miniature political-philosophy seminar, as Instagram comments sometimes do.

Although that comment applies more to large socialism experiments than small communes, that objection deserves a serious answer. Some people really would rather let somebody else take responsibility. Shared ownership can blur responsibility until the same three people maintain everything while everybody else discusses freedom. Informal leaders then gain power partly because everyone keeps handing it to them.

Communal ownership therefore needs named stewardship, visible duties, consequences for chronic freeloading, and enough relational capacity to confront the problem before resentment becomes the real government. A community cannot run on the assumption that removing bosses removes passivity, selfishness, theft, or learned helplessness.

The desire to return to a natural way of living together has outrun the knowledge. People know they’re lonely and exhausted. They usually don’t know why so many earlier communities failed, why the same conflicts keep returning, or how quickly beautiful land becomes irrelevant once money, children, jealousy, and ownership enter the picture.

I’m not currently recruiting anyone or founding a community. That makes this easier to write because I’m not trying to get you onto my land. I do plan to buy land, and people may naturally end up living around me. Every warning in this article applies to me too.

SECTION: Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways

PUBLISHED:

The communes I knew tended to fail inwardly in one of two directions.

## The secular failure

This failure is caused when the community distributes ownership and labor but has no shared way to address jealousy, childhood wounds, humiliation, attraction, resentment, or grief. Those things don’t disappear. They acquire agenda items.

The Federation of Egalitarian Communities and other income-sharing groups developed sophisticated systems for labor, money, and decision-making. Yet a meeting procedure can’t make two people stop hating each other. It can only determine whose turn it is to speak about the hatred and whether the motion requires a second.

Eventually politics begins carrying emotional material that nobody knows how to name. People argue about schedules, committees, noise, food, or policy while the real subject sits nearby with its arms crossed.

## The spiritual failure

This is caused when the community recognizes that inner transformation matters, then routes it through a founder, teacher, channel, or special interpreter of reality.

The Farm in Tennessee grew to around 1,500 people and held many beautiful values. Its early spiritual and social life was also deeply shaped by Stephen Gaskin. Accounts from former members describe community doctrine reaching into intimate decisions. A group can talk about universal consciousness while one man’s opinions become unusually difficult to escape.

The two failures mirror each other. Secular groups distribute power but leave the inner life largely private. Spiritual groups address the inner life but concentrate power around whoever defines the path.

The deeper failure is that authority earned in one role can be spent in every other role. A founder may be a gifted teacher. A therapist may genuinely help people. A farmer or business manager may carry much of the economy. The danger begins when the same person can define the problem, control the evidence, choose the remedy, and make disagreement or exit costly.

I want depth without a guru owning it, and distributed authority without pretending that people arrive emotionally finished. That requires practice between peers, plus enough play that the community doesn’t become a permanent repair shop.

Most communes of my acquaintance went wrong from the inside, in one of two ways.

## The secular failure

The secular communes had a solution for how to distribute property or labor, but when the deep waters of jealousy or childhood hurt or humiliation or attraction or resentment or grief came up, the group was without a common method for dealing with it. The feelings just became agenda items.

Meeting procedure isn’t going to solve every problem, no matter how sophisticated the system of labor and money and decision making may be in a community of the Federation of Egalitarian Communities or any other income-sharing community. It can’t make it so that two people don’t hate each other. It can only make it so that they take turns talking about it, and that whoever raises it has to find somebody who agrees with him or her before they get to talk about it.

Eventually, when people are together long enough, politics will become a way of carrying emotional material that people can’t name. There will be a fight about a schedule or committee or noise or food or some policy or other, but it won’t be about that. ⟦author⟧There’s no shared way to talk about the hurt itself, but the meeting agenda is the one place the group lets you push for something and win, so the angry communard goes there, trying to get some justice.⟦/author⟧
