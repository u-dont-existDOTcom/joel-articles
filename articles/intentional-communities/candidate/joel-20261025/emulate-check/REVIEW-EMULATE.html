The New Age May Dawn Suddenly

P1I just put a movie my father made in 1988 up on the Internet for the first time: *Where’s Utopia?* It was the result of years of work, traveling to all kinds of communes and communal experiments around the country, trying to answer a question that for him was primary: How do we live together without recreating the same loneliness, domination, and stupidity we were supposedly escaping?

P2He had already spent 17 years in the Bruderhof, a Christian community that doesn’t use money internally. They also accepted my dad as a Jewish atheist, making him a remarkably long-running doctrinal exception. In meditation there, he came to his own understanding that “God is the Universe, and the Universe is Love.”

Otherwise, we’ve built a lovely refuge for whoever got there first.

Most of my big little article here is me attempting to describe the missing bridge between those.

Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways

P1Most communes of my acquaintance traded inner freedom of the heart (spirituality) for outer freedom (secular, nondogmatic, political). The two pendulum ends of this trade-off mirror each other. I believe we can combine the best of both, but first, let’s see how each fails individually.

Outer-freedom-only models

P1The secular communes (and all the manuals I’ve seen on building them) had a solution for how to distribute property or labor in a way folks could agree on, but when the deep waters of jealousy or childhood hurt or humiliation or attraction or resentment or grief came up, the group was without a common method for dealing with it.

P2Eventually, when people are together long enough, politics will become a way of carrying emotional material that people can’t name. There will be a fight about a schedule or committee or noise or food or some policy or other, but it won’t be about that. There’s no shared way to talk about the hurt itself, but the meeting agenda is the one place the group lets you push for something and win, so the angry communard goes there, trying to get some justice.

Spiritual dogma models attempting inner freedom

P1Many people have already realized that they are more interested in spiritual (or emotional) liberation than political liberation, so they tend to congregate in communities built for that. But because these communes focus so much on the inner work that the founder believes in, it can often look like some kind of prison-monastery cult, where you feel free inside as long as you don’t question anything.

P2The Farm, in Tennessee, at its largest about 1,500 people, had many beautiful values. But in the early days, the community’s spirituality and social life followed the founder, Stephen Gaskin. From what former members have shared, the community’s doctrine ran deep into members’ intimate decisions. This is how it often goes: folks talk about oneness with the universe, while one man defines what the universe is.

We Want Freedom Inside & Out!

P1Let’s face it: food, housing, and logistics are not enough reason to bind whole lives together. That’s more like a situationship. A community needs a shared sacred inner practice that gives the difficulty of our life together a deeper meaning. We help each other heal and choose love over fear. And we turn outward with a purpose, using the escuelita model to bring our healing to the world.

P2I dream of a way to work in depth that isn’t run by a guru, and a way of being in a community with distributed authority. A place where people who’ve done the healing first can join without pretending they’re fully finished, and that also has peer practice and enough play that it doesn’t become a permanent repair shop.

Choose Values Before Falling in Love With the View

P1Have you noticed people often choose their community in the same way they would choose a vacation rental? “Wow! Look at this beautiful land! And a pond, with the sunset in it!! Who needs a beach?! And the people seem really nice, let’s get in here!” Fast forward two years. “Wait, there’s a vaccination policy?? And you need paperwork for it?!?!”

P2Bring your values with you when you go to visit a community. Some of them will just get a question mark, if you’re not sure yet.

Practice Before Committing

P1So, build the part of community you actually can. **Call it a practice group**, for now, not a commune. Fun and nice things first give incentive. Have a weekly meal with two households. Set up a counseling exchange with one friend, in person or by video call. Share tools or childcare. Transport or food prep work too. Or a sauna. And if you’re hippie enough, add acro-yoga, dance improv, a mothers’ circle or a cuddle puddle.

P2And then the hard stuff comes as a test before any talk of a shared future together. Keep your commitments, or renegotiate when they feel wrong, rather than just avoiding. Get through at least one big disagreement. Dealing with it for real includes the relational pl/ork of each of you naming what really went on, giving each of you time, figuring out what came up for each of you (jealousy, humiliation, fear, old material, whatever), and dealing with it until the emotional pressure changes enough that whatever agreement you come up with means something.

P3Until all of those phases are passed thru, you’re all still promising strangers, and you don’t want to take out a mortgage together yet. And once you’re past those phases, if you still want to live together, you can all meet in a rented or borrowed space, or at each other’s houses, long before anyone needs a land surveyor.

The Math of Absorption

P1Many perfectly intelligent grownups would rather sit around for six hours talking about solar panels than talk about jealousy, God, death, childhood, money, sex, psychedelics, or somebody wanting to leave, which is what I ask people to do in this article.

P2This doesn’t surprise me, since we’re taught to put on a show of competence in modern society. We’re given a lot of incentives to hide ourselves and disappear behind our screens the second things get uncomfortable. Emotional intelligence doesn’t pay the bills. Instead we optimize to fit the career market with all the little energy we’ve got.

P3So then a lot of people end up thinking they aren’t “good enough” for community, or getting labeled by others that way. And there’s a real issue there, but it’s more about what fits where & when than good or bad.

P4Some people have engaged in long practices of contemplation or therapy, or have had very good luck in childhood; other people are just trying to survive some emergency in their life and need care more than responsibility. Most of us are all of these at different times in our lives.

P5![Diagram, “The Math of Absorption”: a community can absorb only as much need as its members have free resource to meet. P6In the model I’m imagining, some of the functions that are outsourced to therapists in modern society are distributed throughout the community.

P7That does not make the community infinitely absorbent like a Heavy Flow Maxi Pad.

P8I’m going to use the phrase “free resource,” which comes from Re-evaluation Counseling, to refer to the attention, steadiness, skill and practical capacity people have left over for others once they’ve dealt with enough of their own immediate stuff, and “need” to refer to the amount of help people need at a given time.

P9My own need has exceeded my free resource at many points. Good Lord. During one stretch, my first wife tried to kill me while psychotic because she believed I was her sociopathic father. Then she took my son. Later she tried to kill my mother, while her pimp stalked my sister’s family until they moved to Canada.

P10I could go on and on, but the math is pretty clear by now. My free resource for others was approaching zero. So when I say someone might need to stabilize and do inner pl/ork before doing community (or whatever relationship), I’m not saying it just from a theory.

P11The ability to welcome somebody is partly a problem of resources, then, not compassion -- how many people can a group take in without hurting the existing members or the people they are taking in?

P12And that also brings us to a difficult catch-22 for fledgling groups. The need for new members in a small community is greater, but the ability of that community to absorb disruption is less. With only eight people in the community, someone still has to grow the food and fix whatever breaks. Somebody has to balance the books and watch the kids. And the water system has to keep working. The water well doesn’t care how beautiful the new member’s heart is.

P13So early on, members usually need to have more available resource than acute need, and some practical skills to contribute. They usually need to be willing to learn, too. Once there are 80 members, though, there’s usually a lot more free resource to draw on.

P14I still don’t know how to keep this language of capacity from becoming a respectable way to exclude people who are disabled, poor, less articulate, maybe simply uncomfortable or unfamiliar with therapeutic language, or for whatever reason deemed inconvenient. Pretending capacity is unlimited won’t solve that. Any real process would need concrete criteria, review by someone who wasn’t part of the decision, and a way to revisit a “not now” decision on membership. Even then, the power to veto membership applications based on capacity remains dangerously tilted towards elitism.

Who This Isn’t For

P1But before you submit your application to the community, submit it to yourself. This community may not be a good fit for you right now if:

•You have a need that far outstrips your free resource right now. You need to take a first step of finding support where you are and building up your resources. If you don’t have a free resource calculator on hand, an easy way to tell is if you’re running from some crisis right now and mainly need a place to land, get treatment, housing, legal aid, etc. A founding group shouldn’t pretend it can give you those. - You want the feeling of village life but don’t want to take on governance, maintenance, conflict, care, etc. - You’re not willing to look at yourself when jealousy or conflict comes up.

From Visitor to Member—Slowly

P1When The Farm grew fast, a lot of the people who came were in need, and there weren’t many filters being applied. Being open to everybody was part of the spiritual “we are all one” new age philosophy. But practical burden piled up. A membership process is there to protect both the people who are already there and the newcomers.

P21.**First, correspond with the visitor.** Did they read the materials? (Not everyone does.) Do they get the basic idea of why this community is here? We almost died, literally, a few times on the way there. P4San Cristóbal de las Casas is where the only white man we met sat us down in a café and grilled us so directly that he seemed like a CIA agent. Why were we there? Who were we working with? What were our plans? When were we leaving?

P5It made us feel important for a minute. We were just college kids in a dying rental car, but apparently the empire had questions for us. 😂

P6Oventik itself was like something out of a dream. Zap spirituality enigma

P1I felt a deep spirituality from the Zapatistas. The Mayan language, as they explained, doesn’t even have subject/object structure. Instead of “I put the glass there” it’s something like “With me, the glass moved there.” I also suspect there may be a private relationship they have with ayahuasca, since Mimosa hostilis (a potent DMT ayahuasca plant) mainly comes from that region, and the Mexican government vaguely accuses them of narcotrafficking. That’s another reason to look at them as an example of much of my model, since if they are using ayahuasca, it could explain some things about their political struggles and spiritual successes, including the fact that they have the most beautiful poetic rhetoric I’ve ever seen published.

Democratic anarcho-communalism actualized

P1What impressed me the most was how they do authority. It’s mandar obedeciendo, to lead by obeying: the representatives carry the decisions people made in the assemblies, and they have to answer to the people who gave them the mandate.

P2The Zapatistas are also the closest thing to a whole political-economic example that I found, because their autonomy goes beyond how they vote. They’ve set up their own education and health care, and their own justice, and they run their own collective production.

P3*The Fire and the Word* describes the movement growing from family to village, village to region, and region to larger structures. It also gives one of the most useful Zapatista principles for community design: build the practice first and develop the theory afterward. The autonomous municipalities came first; an association of them became a rehearsal for the Good Government Juntas (Boards). When different municipalities encountered different problems and uneven progress, that experience generated another structure rather than being forced to fit the original plan.

Real democracy is a continual struggle

P1After a long internal review, in 2023, the Zapatistas announced they were dissolving their previous federated governmental structure, which was split into Rebel Zapatista Autonomous Municipalities and the Good Government Juntas. The original idea was to have power vested in the local communities, but it had crept up and consolidated itself at higher levels over time for practical reasons. Their new arrangement moved authority downward toward a Local Autonomous Government in each community.

P2Institutions get more and more layers and offices as they age. We see this same pattern happen repeatedly, everywhere in history, except usually it goes one way without reversal, unless there’s a revolution.

P3But there is a special reason the Zapatistas were successfully able to reverse this degeneration. Zapatista autonomy grew out of Indigenous communities with long histories. They share language and family ties. They’ve fought land struggles together, and defended themselves together for decades.

P1Now, the Zapatistas also don’t prove my whole money-free economy goal. They still use money internally in some cases, not others, and they still use it externally for trade. P2One way an escuelita could fail is if it becomes a business, where residents spend their lives performing community for paying outsiders, and success gets measured by how many people come instead of how many daughter communities there are. A week of feeling transformed isn’t what it’s for.

P1As part of my research I have also looked for evidence of what happened after the Escuelita. P2Sociocracy has a very different history, yet some similar features have emerged there as well: circles, decision-making by consent, distributed authority, and linking between the levels. Lessons from the Black Tax

P1Then there’s the so-called “Black Tax,” a strange legacy of the old communal system: if you’ve got any money at all, you’re expected to share it with your parents, your siblings, your cousins, to pay school fees, medical bills, funerals, etc. I understand the instinct. The medicine can deepen the well you already learned how to dig, or it can show you a window into the future you want to see, but it’s not a simple shortcut.

P2This prerequisite also takes care of another issue in one shot: it weeds out the experience collectors. If someone is unwilling to put in 6 months learning to listen without imposing their solution, it is unlikely they will come out of a ceremony more relationally mature, regardless of how many self-transforming fractalized machine elves told them they are the reincarnation of King David & Elvis.😂

P3Integration is a term thrown around so much that I’m not sure if it still has meaning. But basically, post-ceremony integration is your everyday life. The ceremony is supposed to open something up in you. Look at your life in the weeks after the ceremony and see how things have changed. That includes your relationships, behaviors, sleeping habits, your decision-making, and perhaps most importantly, how well you can deal with frustration without declaring a new spiritual emergency. 😂

P4As a community, you need to write down what is and isn’t allowed, and which activities are private (to the individual, to a group in the community, or to the community vs the outside).

Community Is What Makes Psychedelics Pl/ork Best!

The Case Against Psychedelics

P1Physical and psychological negative effects of ayahuasca were common in the Global Ayahuasca Survey, although severe effects were far less common and context mattered. And these medicines can open things up faster than a person (or their group) can deal with. One of the best resources on this topic is the Challenging Psychedelic Experiences project.

P2Some people and communities should avoid them. Where access is ahead of competence, people will become casualties of enthusiasm. Is your community humble enough to say no? To pause? Using the medicine takes that. It also takes humility to refer people out, and admit when an experience did harm.

P3They do also attract people who are all for revelation and hate the follow-through, people who can collect 12 ceremonies and 6 origin stories and still can’t say sorry to their housemate. Any community based mainly around the peak experience is going to find out sooner or later that the insight doesn’t do the practical work for it. Sometimes the dishes need doing and a scared kid needs comforting.

No Special Shamans

P1Traditional lineages usually disagree with me on this, and many ayahuasca tribes and Bwiti iboga communities believe the medicine should stay under trained lineage authority. I respect what those traditions have preserved, and I’ve learned some “dos & don’t dos” from their sensible practices and less sensible practices. For example, although I do highly value loving kindness meditation, inner child reparenting, and nibbana meditation before ceremony, during, and at all times, I notice a lot of people want their ceremony to look and feel like some kind of abstract mix of indigenous rituals & props to make it feel more authentic and sacred than their everyday life. I understand the benefit of setting aside one time and place for sacred experiences (the book, “God is Red” comes to mind), although the shadow side to that is making the spiritual seem like another world apart from your regular life, which is sort of opposite to integration.

P2And while I do think it’s valuable to have someone highly experienced in navigating the spirit worlds who is able to sense where you are in those and guide you, I also wouldn’t trust many of these shamans to guide me, considering it’s common to find that even the ones who can heal people very well may still be happy to send evil spirits to kill someone, if that hit job is lucrative. I also don’t trust their medical intuition in general, considering the Huni Kuin shamans are famous for claiming that all other ayahuasca shamans are giving people cancer by using the knotted B. caapi vine (since the knots remind them of tumors). I’m sure I could still learn a lot from those traditions, and they will continue to develop their understandings as well.

Jealousy Can Capture Monogamy or Free Love

P1Any community that lasts will eventually be reorganized by attraction and pair-bonding. When two exes live in the same city, they can move to different sides of the city. When they’re part of a thirty-person commune, they may still be in the same barn the next morning, working in the same team, attending the same meetings, sharing childcare and the same friends. A couple’s jealousy or breakup becomes a problem for the entire community, because it will change the loyalties, the sleeping arrangements, the labor, the kids, the alliances, and sometimes who’s leaving.

P2The discussion on this topic generally goes straight to “should people be monogamous or poly?” But as I discuss in my Romance Guide, this is one level too late in the conversation. The community problem is that, in close quarters, jealousy gets magnified, and jealousy can capture either system.

P3Rigid monogamy can turn fear and possession into a moral law. If people are doing free love, but aren’t doing the inner pl/ork, they can just end up with a larger spreadsheet to distribute their fear over. It’s not like calling something by a certain name dissolves the childhood panic, the comparison, the terror of abandonment, or the desire to control another person.

P4I don’t think it should be up to the community to decide for everyone what form relationships will take. People can make different agreements with each other, in pairs, threes, fours, whatever. They can change those agreements as they wish. And they can ask for support when those agreements bring up old material.

P5Whatever forms people choose, I do think that housing, security for kids, membership and access to common necessities should not be contingent on a person staying in some agreement with the people they’re romantically involved with.

P6ZEGG, a community near Berlin, and Tamera, a ZEGG offshoot in Portugal, have spent decades experimenting with relationships inside community. They are an example of how a community can acknowledge the erotic aspects of life.

P7The non-negotiable for me is that jealousy not be allowed to become the hidden governor. It needs to be able to be talked about and felt in the body. It needs to be explored without humiliation. And it needs to be let go of gradually, and not allowed to turn into punishment, or into community rules.

P8Even so, there will still be disagreements about this.

Communal Parenting Adds On

P1One of the biggest reasons I think community matters for kids is Jean Liedloff’s *The Continuum Concept*. Liedloff was writing about what she learned while living with the Ye’kuana tribe in the Venezuelan rainforest. Some say she idealized what she saw, but I still find it powerful: the babies are carried by the adults (and not just by one exhausted parent) all the time, so they participate in everything adults do from the start. Instead of age-matched cohorts like we have in our schools, kids are with others of all ages. Some modern parents try to adopt some of the Ye’kuana child-rearing practices, but the full stack requires the actual communal support they had.

P2Experiments where kids sleep and live apart from their parents deserve special caution. Research on Israeli kibbutzim found that the practice of kids sleeping communally eventually ended due to developmental findings, parents’ preferences and social change.

P3!A summary card, “What happens to adults raised with different caregiving practices?”: in a 1994 study of 48 infants on Israeli kibbutzim, 48% were securely attached with communal sleeping and 80% with sleeping at home, a 32-point difference in favor of family sleeping; the families weren’t randomly assigned, and the sample was small.

P3My own bias is still toward a system where kids are not solely taken care of by their parents, while still honoring the parent-child bond. P4I got a taste of that at a Unitarian youth camp, and it’s still one of the freest memories I have.

P5The adults still have to pay attention to what goes on in the children’s territory. Older kids can’t be allowed to boss the younger ones around there. And keeping secrets shouldn’t be part of how independence manifests.

P6I feel very much saddened by the way that adults are seen as (and actually often are) so dangerous for children in the West, especially when they are fond of each other. It’s good for kids to have a whole bunch of grownups who know them and enjoy them, who would notice when something changes, and who would act when something’s wrong. When adults panic like that, kids can lose all those grownups without the panic actually preventing abuse. That’s real freedom!

P7So here’s how I see this protection being ideally structured:

1.**Give children real agency.** When kids say no to an adult who wants to be alone with them or touch them, that’s a hard line that can’t be crossed. Apart from that, obedience should not be demanded from kids outside of legitimate safety needs. 2. **Create a no-secrets rule.** No grownup has a right to ask a kid to keep a secret from their mom or another trusted adult, whether it’s about a gift, a game, a touch, a conversation or a relationship. (A surprise has an end date. But secrets meant to isolate a kid aren’t allowed.) 3. **Teach Honesty**. Protecting children is the duty of adults, but it requires children to help also. “The Boy Who Cried Wolf” is a didactic children’s story which continues to be useful generation after generation. The Buddha even taught his son Rahula honesty as the most important first lesson. I agree. We must teach children truthfulness before there’s a crisis. “Make believe” must be labeled as such. When adults lie to children (like “Santa is real!” etc), that teaches them to misunderstand the difference between reality and make-believe. Make sure kids understand what they say will be taken seriously if they are known to be honest. Those who are known to lie a lot are especially targeted by abusers who know their accusations won’t be believed. Don’t let the teaching turn into interrogation or punishment when they’re not sure. A scared or confused kid may get mixed up telling what happened and still need protection. 4. **Hear the child even when the accused is someone people love.** Communities fail here, because the adult may be charismatic, useful, rich, spiritually important, or everybody’s friend. Decide on the response process ahead of time, before anyone’s face is attached to it. Keep everyone at risk safe first, right away, then make sure the accusation is investigated competently and fairly. 5. **Make sure kids have relationships and a reporting route outside the community.** Grandparents, cousins, local friends, teachers and other trusted adults give them perspective on their life, and someone to talk to. State intervention can fail spectacularly, and enclosing kids completely in a community can fail too. Kids need more than one world. In 1984, Vermont authorities raided a religious community in Island Pond and took 112 kids into custody over suspected abuse. A judge turned down the state’s blanket request to hold them, because it couldn’t name any specific case, and the kids went home that same day. There was apparently some physical abuse going on there as a disciplinary practice, but that’s something which varies a lot in every society also.

The Door Must Open Outward at Adulthood

P1If a community’s kids never leave, it has no way of knowing whether they’d come back freely. So let them study, travel and make friends, encounter other ways of living, and disagree with the founders without being treated as traitors. And kids coming back isn’t necessarily the best thing for the communal movement either. If they then start their own communities, we may see more evolution than if they simply return and continue what their parents started.

P2Amish young people, from about age 16, go through an indefinite period of freedom from parental control called Rumspringa. During this time they might travel outside, although they normally choose to stay in the community and begin acting as adults, including courtship. At the end of this period, they decide as adults whether to join the church, and roughly 85 percent or more of them end up joining. Pop culture makes Rumspringa seem like a wilder, more universal teenage free-for-all than it is, but the main point is that they’re baptized as adults, which means belonging is a commitment they make.

Every childhood narrows the future

P1Most ordinary parents aren’t going to give their kid a bunch of money at 18 and say, “Go see the world!” They’ll try to get them ready for some next step that can actually work, so they can become self-sufficient, and eventually take care of their aging parents. A poor or money-free community may have even less cash to offer.

P2Unlimited freedom isn’t realistic, but it doesn’t need to be artificially impossible for grown kids to leave. Kids in or outside of intentional communities all need to learn enough about how the money world outside operates that they aren’t helpless in it. Like how to get a job, pay rent, read contracts, handle banks, get IDs, avoid getting scammed, and how to ask for help when they’re unsure about any of that. And all that sounds amorphous if it’s not structured into plausible next steps outside the community (school, an apprenticeship, work, family, another community).

P3Kids also need transferable skills and education. It helps if they have relationships with people outside the community. They should be able to access their own records and documents. It would be great if the community could also provide resources to smooth the transition, like temporary housing, help with travel, etc. If it can’t, it can still avoid punishing departure.

Money, Land, and Shared Resources

P1What kind of legal or illegal entity (cooperative, trust, association, company, or tribal squatting?) will hold the land? This needs to be decided before it’s bought or occupied. Then you’ll need to decide who has what voting rights, who has what access and use rights to common resources, how you will account for people’s contributions (if at all), how inheritance works, outside debts… oh my! Read the founderism questions next, and agree on the exit terms in “Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution” before anyone commits.

Moving Beyond Money

P1Like my father, I’m aiming for communities with less and less money involved, since money is antagonistic to the spirit of community. If I have money, I can get whatever I want, regardless of whether anyone cares about my wellbeing. That’s the hegemonic philosophy of life we are trying to overturn. I think the path there has three stages.

P2First there will be no money exchanged between members for basic community functions like food, housing, care, tools, education, transport etc. Members might have outside jobs, and they bring an agreed share of that income in. This is probably a good place to start if someone wants to create a community now and have it be realistic.

P3Later all wages and other income earned by the members from work outside the community, or from business inside the community, will be paid into the common purse of the community. No member accumulates private wealth while a member of the community. Some version of this is how things have been done in the Bruderhof community for generations, and my father lived there for 17 years. It is also done, in a somewhat different way, at Twin Oaks, and a number of other income-sharing communities.

P4Eventually, the community would also stop using money outside. People would meet their needs directly, or through exchange (be it trade, gift, work, obligation, or whatever). I couldn’t find a well-documented modern community that has gone this far, and in fact, it may be impractical in some locations because of government requirements like taxes on land. So perhaps the practical end point here is a community that is nearly entirely moneyless, but has a small collective cash boundary on the outside.

P5None of this does away with scarcity or economic power. There are lots of areas where such power can be wielded: access to food storage, housing, tools, land, medicine, transportation, and the outside world’s currency, to name a few. Even in a moneyless community, someone can control what’s scarce. Who decides how these things are budgeted and distributed?

Founderism: The Four Questions

P1In every community there was somebody (or some founding group) who took the initiative to get it started. They found the land, wrote the first documents, taught the method, recruited the first members, or kept things going when everybody else lost interest. Like the U.S. Founding Fathers.

P2The founder(s) end(s) up with real influence, and pretending they don’t just makes that influence harder to look at. When that power fails to get distributed as the conditions ripen for that, it’s called founderism, which is a fledgling form of what can become a subtle dictatorship.

P3I’ve seen founderism in many communes that I’ve visited, and I can see it playing out if I were to be a founder as well. I’ve got all these proposals and visions here in my guides, and so it would be very easy for me to become the person who decides whether or not everyone else “understood” them correctly, even while I wish to remain humble and open to the input of all involved.

P4There are some things we can do to keep founders in check. For one, we can judge the lofty methodology by results people can actually see. Will insiders and outsiders see the members safely flourishing without being trapped? Able to leave without it being stigmatized? Can folks resolve conflicts in healthy ways? Are the kids doing well once they grow up? And if the community is meant to spread, do we see daughter projects actually start up and last? Those questions seem more revealing to me than how big or old the commune gets, or how many decisions are made by some form of consensus.

P5The questions so far have been about the method. These four are about the founder:

1.***Can the community respectfully override the founder(s)?*** 2. ***Can the community replace the role of the founder(s)?*** 3. ***Can the community alter the vision of the founder(s)?*** 4. ***Can the community exist without the founder(s)?***

P6Then follow the practical power. Whose name is on the deed? Who’s in charge of the resources? Who keeps the records? Who decides who gets admitted, and who picks the people who review the founder? Who controls access to the kids, or to medicine, and who can make a complaint go away? The org chart might look flat and horizontal, while the keys, passwords, titles and even just general reputation all point to one person or clique.

P7If several are present, they are worth paying attention to before the founder(s) can explain how the situation is uniquely different in a spiritual way. Ask these questions about any community, teacher or method, including me.

P8While founderism is a problem in every community, it’s especially so in communities which form naturally over time as I’m describing may happen in my case. In such cases, there’s no formal community and no formal rules until people decide to come together to make them. It doesn’t make sense to do that at first, if, say, I’m the only one there and I don’t know who else will be there that I could trust. If there’s one other person who comes to visit, it also doesn’t make sense, so there’s a critical mass point at which people decide “we were just living together like friends, but now we want to formalize the community.”

P9If I could find a suitable commune to join, that would basically reverse this problem. I would then be the one on probation. And if we are all starting together from scratch, then we would all be at an equal probationary level with each other.

P2I could totally start a cult. Trust me.

P3As if a founderism-type subtle dictatorship isn’t bad enough, we often find groups that are even proudly and overtly controlled by some Pharaoh at the top of the pyramid, especially when the organization of the group is as advanced as what it took to build the Great Pyramids.

P4Every practice in this article can be turned against the people using it.

P5“Nothing suppressed, everything processed” can become a situation where someone in the group must tell all, even if they don’t want to. I have seen that risk in groups that use the ZEGG Forum practice. But privacy also can be resistance to the communal spirit, so there has to be a living, always re-assessed balance between what you and the community owe each other, and what remains your own domain. But some practices go too far against the communal spirit. If someone starts worshipping Satan, say, they get booted.

P6Peer counseling can become peer surveillance, even if the tone is warm.

P7Dissent is important for growth. Sometimes it really is their wounded ego projection, and that should be acknowledged. But again, balance, because if complaints are too often or too broadly painted as projection, that’s as much a failure, if not more so.

P8There are some basic human rights that can’t be voted away from you by the majority in a community, unless it’s a question of resource availability as a whole. If safety and the conditions for health can be provided, they should be provided. People should be able to maintain relationships with others outside the community, as long as those relationships don’t interfere with the community as a whole (for example, you can’t bring your boyfriend from outside to live with you if he’s antagonistic towards the commune).

P9Let’s say a grandmother is demonizing the commune. Cutting off contact with her could make things way worse. When it’s tricky and dangerous for everyone like that, I think the commune needs to step in and help people decide how to communicate with outsiders.

From One Community to a Movement

P1The problem of how these communities stay connected does eventually need to be solved somehow, if the idea is for them to spread without building another state. It should be solved in a way that allows help between communities and the movement of people and knowledge, and also keeps the differences alive, so communities can keep learning from one another. And communities already do have federations which provide some degrees of assistance in training people, seeding new projects, logging experiences and lessons, and so forth.

P2The Federation of Egalitarian Communities (FEC) says it’s rebuilding, and that’s a good warning. It takes work to run a federation. Delegates. Records. Compliance. Coordinating resources. Holding meetings. Succession. Whatever dispute function it actually takes on. These things all take up labor and material capacity before any local crisis has eaten into both. But the work of federation doesn’t have to be done by a single entity. There can also be local clusters, or single-function institutions like the FEC’s PEACH health pool, which isn’t currently governed by the FEC delegates.

P3Cooperation also shouldn’t slide into assimilation. The FEC has full members and communities working toward full membership, and Friends who may just want to stay friends. Fortunately, the interface between communities can often be thin, saying who’s responsible for what while somebody crosses between them, and communities don’t have to have the same internal systems for it to work.

P4Solidarity doesn’t mean every community is liable for whatever the others owe, and it doesn’t mean anyone has an unlimited claim on another community’s labor and stores, or its land and tools. Even if nobody uses money internally, communities still need the same honesty about exchanges and obligations. Decide in advance which offers of help are purely goodwill and which go on a ledger of mutual aid. The federation can keep track of these for each purpose, without having to put a value on them or require every exchange to be “even”.

P5Whenever a community or a federation creates or applies a rule, it’s best practice to store the full case history (with private details protected) so it can be re-evaluated in future: what happened, the principles that actually conflicted, your first judgments, and your final decision and rationale. Any dissenting opinions should go in there as well, along with the consequences in the case. When the rule changes because of a new case, run the old case through again to see how it would turn out. That saves each generation from having to rediscover the same constitutional fight from scratch.

Experiment Before You Split Off

P1Experimenting with a different rule doesn’t have to mean everybody goes out and buys new land right away. Try it inside the parent group first, then as a semi-autonomous project, and then, if it needs more independence, as a seed group nearby that still shares some tools, childcare, business or friendships.

P2Some experiments (like a different land system, or a different population size, or any number of rules that work together) can’t be tried in a way that’s cheap. They may look bad when they’re starved of the assets they actually need. “We tried it in one room for three months” isn’t always a fair test.

Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution

Leaving Voluntarily

P1Good people leave for good reasons -- dying parent, love, work, health, exhaustion, change of mind, etc. You don’t want them to lose everything they’ve put in. Whether or not you use money, you’ll need to have a way of providing people with resources when they leave (cash, if you use it, but also tools, transportation, temporary housing, stored up goods, the right to keep using something, or whatever). Otherwise their “right” to leave is only on paper -- it only works when the community happens to have spare money. Write up the exit terms while leaving is still only theoretical -- like a pre-nup when you’re planning the wedding. Not romantic, but protective. Much easier than doing it once anybody’s furious.

When Somebody Has to Go

P1A safe community can’t promise to keep everyone. There may be times when someone has to go. If someone is a serious or generalized danger to others, or keeps violating essential boundaries, or for whatever reason just makes everyday life together impossible for everyone else, it can be the right call to eject them. If restorative work can help in a reasonable amount of time, all the better. But it’s not the community’s job to be a treatment program for someone who’s endangering everyone else.

P2This power to remove people is so easily abused, though. So work out the process before it’s about anyone in particular. What counts as cause for immediate protective action? Who decides to put temporary restrictions on someone? Who will make sure all the evidence is preserved? How will the person be able to reply to the concerns? What protections will there be for the person’s interests (housing, records, family contact, access to common resources, outside funds if any) during the separation? When is it time to bring in outside help?

P4Also decide on a mediator or ombudsperson in advance. Why bother? When the Community Dissolves

P1![A wedding with a coffin in front of the altar: the bride and groom look startled, a guest in sunglasses throws rose petals, and a sign reads “Till Death Do Us Party”.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!9Q3c!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fddd2a051-9ac5-4630-868b-5a37226707f9_1536x1024.png)

P2Dissolution is something founders rarely want to talk about while they’re picking a name for their community and looking forward to its first harvest. Who would? But that’s exactly when it needs to be talked about.

P3If the community dissolves, what happens to the land and any outside debts? What about its buildings, equipment, common stores or funds? What happens to each member’s agreed rights? More immediately, who is responsible for ensuring that everyone has a place to live and food to eat? Who takes responsibility for making sure people get their medications and the other care they need? Who keeps childcare going, and makes sure the kids’ schooling carries on? Who takes responsibility for transportation, for the records, for family contact, for the animals, for making sure land obligations are met, and for any unresolved claims? That’s a lot of loose ends!

P4Think of it like a living will, and assign a bridge or successor for each critical function in advance, so basic life doesn’t depend on reviving the commune. The institution may not be salvageable, but who’s making breakfast tomorrow?

P5Dissolution terms are also a way for a group to end without turning every friendship into evidence. Who wants that? People may leave hurt, but they keep the transition support they agreed on, and their dignity. And they can tell a coherent story of what happened.

P6Institutions and relationships don’t die at the same time, and some of what people learned to give one another may go on too. What a relief! The film American Commune follows two sisters who grew up on The Farm. When one of them later became ill, former members who hadn’t seen her in years reached out with support. The Farm’s communal economy had changed dramatically, but the bonds hadn’t vanished.

What Helps Communities Last—and Whether Longevity Is Enough

P1•***Communities That Abide*** by Dmitry Orlov makes an uncomfortable argument: the communities that last a long time often rely on a religious ideology which makes members feel alienated from the wider society. Basically, the more cultish, the more durable, since cults are designed to capture people.

P2It makes one wonder, is longevity alone a meaningful measure, when a community may keep going without its members flourishing? That’s the question in “Measuring Success in Intentional Communities”, a paper by Rubin, Willis and Ludwig. - ***Commitment and Community*** by Rosabeth Moss Kanter is a classic. It compares a bunch of nineteenth-century communes, and the practices that built commitment in them. - ***Alienation and Charisma*** by Benjamin Zablocki is field research on communes, and on the role charisma plays in them. - Jo Freeman’s “The Tyranny of Structurelessness” (1970) is the classic essay on hidden hierarchy. Can it be harder to challenge than explicit authority? She argues it can.
