Outer-freedom-only models

The secular communes (and all the manuals I’ve seen on building them) had a solution for how to distribute property or labor in a way folks could agree on, but when the deep waters of jealousy or childhood hurt or humiliation or attraction or resentment or grief came up, the group was without a common method for dealing with it.

Spiritual dogma models attempting inner freedom

Many people have already realized that they are more interested in spiritual (or emotional) liberation than political liberation, so they tend to congregate in communities built for that.

But because these communes focus so much on the inner work that the founder believes in, it can often look like some kind of prison-monastery cult, where you feel free inside as long as you don’t question anything.

Practice Before Committing

So, build the part of community you actually can.

Call it a practice group, for now, not a commune.

Fun and nice things first give incentive.

Set up a [counseling exchange](http://hearthwork.u-dont-exist.com/) with one friend, in person or by video call.

And then the hard stuff comes as a test before any talk of a shared future together.

Keep your commitments, or renegotiate when they feel wrong, rather than just avoiding.

Get through at least one big disagreement.

Dealing with it for real includes the relational pl/ork of each of you naming what really went on, giving each of you time, figuring out what came up for each of you (jealousy, humiliation, fear, old material, whatever), and dealing with it until the emotional pressure changes enough that whatever agreement you come up with means something.

Until all of those phases are passed thru, you’re all still promising strangers, and you don’t want to take out a mortgage together yet.

And once you’re past those phases, if you still want to live together, you can all meet in a rented or borrowed space, or at each other’s houses, long before anyone needs a land surveyor.

In every community there was somebody (or some founding group) who took the initiative to get it started.

Founding Fathers.

The founder(s) end(s) up with real influence, and pretending they don’t just makes that influence harder to look at.

When that power fails to get distributed as the conditions ripen for that, it’s called founderism, which is a fledgling form of what can become a subtle dictatorship.

There are some things we can do to keep founders in check.

For one, we can judge the lofty methodology by results people can actually see.

Will insiders and outsiders see the members safely flourishing without being trapped?

Able to leave without it being stigmatized?

Can folks resolve conflicts in healthy ways?

And if the community is meant to spread, do we we see daughter projects actually start up and last?

Those questions seem more revealing to me than how big or old the commune gets, or how many decisions are made by some form of consensus.

Can the community respectfully override the founder(s)?

Can the community replace the role of the founder(s)?

Can the community alter the vision of the founder(s)?

Can the community exist without the founder(s)?

Who’s in charge of the resources?

The org chart might look flat and horizontal, while the keys, passwords, titles and even just general reputation all point to one person or clique.

Presence of one of these alone isn’t proof of abuse, but if several are present, they are worth paying attention to before the founder(s) can explain how the situation is uniquely different in a spiritual way.

Ask these questions about any community, teacher or method, including me.
