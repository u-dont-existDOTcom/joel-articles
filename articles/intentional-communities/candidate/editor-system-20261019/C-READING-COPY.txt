Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways

[C034] p Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways /
Most communes of my acquaintance traded inner freedom of the heart (spirituality) for outer freedom (secular, nondogmatic, political). The two pendulum ends of this trade-off mirror each other. I believe we can combine the best of both, but first, let’s see how each fails individually.

Outer-freedom-only models

[C036] p Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways / Outer-freedom-only models
The secular communes (and all the manuals I’ve seen on building them) had a solution for how to distribute property or labor in a way folks could agree on, but when the deep waters of jealousy or childhood hurt or humiliation or attraction or resentment or grief came up, the group was without a common method for dealing with it.

[C037] p Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways / Outer-freedom-only models
Eventually, when people are together long enough, politics will become a way of carrying emotional material that people can’t name. There will be a fight about a schedule or committee or noise or food or some policy or other, but it won’t be about that. There’s no shared way to talk about the hurt itself, but the meeting agenda is the one place the group lets you push for something and win, so the angry communard goes there, trying to get some justice.

The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together

Communities tend to focus on the aspect of their project that was most interesting to the original founders, for example political types getting super into writing by-laws, or spiritual types just meditating all the time, or therapists processing each other, or people who like psychedelics discovering they’re all God together and never getting to the point of deciding who will put their name on the deed.

[C050] p The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together /
I’ve broken the model I propose down into the 4 parts shown in the above diagram. Note, Pl/ork means play + work. Calling therapy “inner work” forgets the inner child, who also wants to play. (Forgive the neologism. I hope pl/ork isn’t too corny. 🌽): 1) inner pl/ork (reparenting), 2) relational pl/ork (peer counseling especially), 3) medicine pl/ork (like loveyhuasca or iboga), and 4) the practical logistical foundation which is most of this article, since I have guides separately on the other parts. Obviously the details are complicated, and jealousy, children, money, land, God, and iboga don’t fit neatly into four boxes, but let’s go with it.

Inner-child reparenting means learning to identify as the best holistic inner adult, which is comprised of the three inner adult roles: Nurturer, Protector, and Guide. The newly developed adult reparents their newly recognized inner child. The child receives all that love, protection, and guidance that you give it, and as a result, your little one gains more freedom to remain alive inside you, which brings back the joy of life that many adults have forgotten about.

[C054] p The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together / 1. Inner pl/ork
And this reparenting journey is built on somatic regulation first. I.e., listening to the body’s feelings before making a decision with the mind. And that actually symbolizes the adult/child relationship to me in some way as well.

The idea is that the capacity to be there for people when they need help isn’t located in one professional or guru, or the founder of the group that everyone emotionally depends on, but in the community as a whole.

San Cristóbal de las Casas is where the only white man we met sat us down in a café and grilled us so directly that he seemed like a CIA agent. Why were we there? Who were we working with? What were our plans? When were we leaving?

[C070] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas /
It made us feel important for a minute. We were just college kids in a dying rental car, but apparently the empire had questions for us. 😂

[C071] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas /
Oventik itself was like something out of a dream. We played basketball outside with the Zapatista youth, and people gave us talks about their history and philosophy, which I translated for my friends because my Spanish was better. Zap spirituality enigma

[C073] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Zap spirituality enigma
I felt a deep spirituality from the Zapatistas. The Mayan language, as they explained, doesn’t even have subject/object structure. Instead of “I put the glass there” it’s something like “With me, the glass moved there.” I also suspect there may be a private relationship they have with ayahuasca, since Mimosa hostilis (a potent DMT ayahuasca plant) mainly comes from that region, and the Mexican government vaguely accuses them of narcotrafficking. [C074] h2 The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Democratic anarcho-communalism actualized
Democratic anarcho-communalism actualized

[C075] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Democratic anarcho-communalism actualized
What impressed me the most was how they do authority. It’s mandar obedeciendo, to lead by obeying: the representatives carry the decisions people made in the assemblies, and they have to answer to the people who gave them the mandate.

[C076] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Democratic anarcho-communalism actualized
The Zapatistas are also the closest thing to a whole political-economic example that I found, because their autonomy goes beyond how they vote. They’ve set up their own education and health care, and their own justice, and they run their own collective production. On top of that they’ve worked out ways of teaching outsiders, all while coordinating beyond a single village.

[C077] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Democratic anarcho-communalism actualized
The Fire and the Word describes the movement growing from family to village, village to region, and region to larger structures. It also gives one of the most useful Zapatista principles for community design: build the practice first and develop the theory afterward. The autonomous municipalities came first; an association of them became a rehearsal for the Good Government Juntas (Boards). When different municipalities encountered different problems and uneven progress, that experience generated another structure rather than being forced to fit the original plan.

[C078] h2 The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Real democracy is a continual struggle
Real democracy is a continual struggle

[C079] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Real democracy is a continual struggle
After a long internal review, in 2023, the Zapatistas announced they were dissolving their previous federated governmental structure, which was split into Rebel Zapatista Autonomous Municipalities and the Good Government Juntas. The original idea was to have power vested in the local communities, but it had crept up and consolidated itself at higher levels over time for practical reasons. Their new arrangement moved authority downward toward a Local Autonomous Government in each community.

[C080] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Real democracy is a continual struggle
Institutions get more and more layers and offices as they age. We see this same pattern happen repeatedly, everywhere in history, except usually it goes one way without reversal, unless there’s a revolution.

[C081] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Real democracy is a continual struggle
But there is a special reason the Zapatistas were successfully able to reverse this degeneration. Zapatista autonomy grew out of Indigenous communities with long histories. They share language and family ties. They’ve fought land struggles together, and defended themselves together for decades.

[C084] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Exporting Zapatismo via the Escuelita
For the 10th anniversary of their autonomous governments, the Zapatistas opened up the Escuelita (little school). The goal of the escuelita was to expand their anarcho-communal, spiritual-humanist revolution worldwide. Outsiders came from all over the world, lived with host families there and learned autonomy by taking part in day to day life. Around 1,500 students came to the first sessions, and thousands more came to later ones.

[C085] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Exporting Zapatismo via the Escuelita
But despite actually trying to train people in their model, the Zapatistas themselves have been clear that people shouldn’t expect to directly or easily import/export their philosophy (Zapatismo) as something ready-made, shake & bake. A new ecovillage can’t copy the diagram everyone can see and get the trust underneath it right away.

[C086] h2 The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Money is still a problem
Money is still a problem

[C087] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Money is still a problem
Now, the Zapatistas also don’t prove my whole money-free economy goal. They still use money internally in some cases, not others, and they still use it externally for trade. They’ve also benefitted from outside economic help, while at the same time making efforts to remain politically independent from donors and more self-sustaining.

[C088] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Money is still a problem
One way an escuelita could fail is if it becomes a business, where residents spend their lives performing community for paying outsiders, and success gets measured by how many people come instead of how many daughter communities there are. A week of feeling transformed isn’t what it’s for. It’s for teaching enough of the social and material system that some people can build somewhere else.

[C090] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Are Zapatistas just unicorns?
As part of my research I have also looked for evidence of what happened after the Escuelita. While I found information on alumni networks, people bringing practices back with them, and organizing later, I couldn’t find a clear chain of somebody having been to the Escuelita, then a new communal institution being set up, and then evidence about how its members and their children actually did over time. Zapatista influence was already spreading abroad before the Escuelita, so if some institution resembles a Zapatista one later, that alone doesn’t prove the Escuelita was responsible.

Sociocracy has a very different history, yet some similar features have emerged there as well: circles, decision-making by consent, distributed authority, and linking between the levels. Does this prove there’s a universal formula? No. But it means that people building institutions of this sort have another serious system to study, instead of inventing their governance during their first dispute.

[C092] h1 Practicing Community Can Start Now /
Practicing Community Can Start Now

[C093] p Practicing Community Can Start Now /
I realize that for most people reading this there are a lot of reasons why they can’t just head off and go join or build community on some land. Jobs, kids, pets, debt, a mortgage, a sick parent, or several of these arranged in a circle... That’s fine! The most important pl/ork doesn’t need land anyway.

[C094] p Practicing Community Can Start Now /
People sometimes buy forty acres and then bring all their unprocessed stuff with them to a more scenic location. It’s a more beautiful view for sure, but now you have the conflict PLUS goats. A group that practices together weekly for two years may never own land. So what? It can still make a difference when people are going through a divorce, an illness or grief. Parenting too, and plain old loneliness.

[C095] figcaption Practicing Community Can Start Now /
This place with the beautiful sky map of the Earth has all its own problems also…

[C096] p Practicing Community Can Start Now /
So, build the part of community you actually can. Call it a practice group, for now, not a commune. Fun and nice things first give incentive. Have a weekly meal with two households. Set up a counseling exchange with one friend, in person or by video call. Share tools or childcare. Transport or food prep work too. Or a sauna. And if you’re hippie enough, add acro-yoga, dance improv, a mothers’ circle or a cuddle puddle.

[C097] p Practicing Community Can Start Now /
And then the hard stuff comes as a test before any talk of a shared future together. Keep your commitments, or renegotiate when they feel wrong, rather than just avoiding. Get through at least one big disagreement. Dealing with it for real includes the relational pl/ork of each of you naming what really went on, giving each of you time, figuring out what came up for each of you (jealousy, humiliation, fear, old material, whatever), and dealing with it until the emotional pressure changes enough that whatever agreement you come up with means something.

[C098] p Practicing Community Can Start Now /
Until all of those phases are passed thru, you’re all still promising strangers, and you don’t want to take out a mortgage together yet. And once you’re passed those phases, if you still want to live together, you can all meet in a rented or borrowed space, or at each other’s houses, long before anyone needs a land surveyor.

Uh, but wait, where do you find these people? [C101] p Practicing Community Can Start Now / Practice group of whom?
And if you can get away from “civilization” for a bit, that can be really valuable to get some perspective on how others have already figured things out. Go visit some real intentional communities. Lots of ecovillages are open to visitors too. Check their current visitor requirements first.

[C102] p Practicing Community Can Start Now / Practice group of whom?
Through visits, you’ll also get a better idea of how much togetherness you enjoy and what kind of privacy you need, and what kind of labor system irritates you. And whether communal showers feel liberating, or make you start looking at studio apartments! 😛 No manifesto is going to tell you any of that.

[C103] p Practicing Community Can Start Now / Practice group of whom?
If you’re lucky, you mind find a pre-existing commune that you’d be happy to live in, without starting your own thing.

Choose Values Before Falling in Love With the View

[C105] p Practicing Community Can Start Now / Choose Values Before Falling in Love With the View
Have you noticed people often choose their community in the same way they would choose a vacation rental? “Wow! Look at this beautiful land! And a pond, with the sunset in it!! Who needs a beach?! And the people seem really nice, let’s get in here!” Fast forward two years. “Wait, there’s a vaccination policy?? And you need paperwork for it?!?!”

Bring your values with you when you go to visit a community. Some of them will just get a question mark, if you’re not sure yet.

[C107] p Practicing Community Can Start Now / Choose Values Before Falling in Love With the View
Sexuality and gender belong on there too. Would LGBTQ+ people be able to live openly in your community, date, be in relationships, and stay fully protected by the community? Should children be taught to question their sexual identities from a young age, and what happens if one of the community’s children comes out as gay or transgender? Money, Land, and Shared Resources

[C110] p Practicing Community Can Start Now / Money, Land, and Shared Resources
What kind of legal or illegal entity (cooperative, trust, association, company, or tribal squatting?) will hold the land? This needs to be decided before it’s bought or occupied. Then you’ll need to decide who has what voting rights, who has what access and use rights to common resources, how you will account for people’s contributions (if at all), how inheritance works, outside debts… oh my!

[C111] h3 Practicing Community Can Start Now / Moving Beyond Money
Moving Beyond Money

[C112] p Practicing Community Can Start Now / Moving Beyond Money
Like my father, I’m aiming for communities with less and less money involved, since money is antagonistic to the spirit of community. If I have money, I can get whatever I want, regardless of whether anyone cares about my wellbeing. That’s the hegemonic philosophy of life we are trying to overturn. I think the path there has three stages.

[C113] p Practicing Community Can Start Now / Moving Beyond Money
First there will be no money exchanged between members for basic community functions like food, housing, care, tools, education, transport etc. Members might have outside jobs, and they bring an agreed share of that income in. This is probably a good place to start if someone wants to create a community now and have it be realistic.

[C114] p Practicing Community Can Start Now / Moving Beyond Money
Later all wages and other income earned by the members from work outside the community, or from business inside the community, will be paid into the common purse of the community. No member accumulates private wealth while a member of the community. Some version of this is how things have been done in the Bruderhof community for generations, and my father lived there for 17 years. It is also done, in a somewhat different way, at Twin Oaks, and a number of other income-sharing communities. So a common purse isn’t just a theory.

[C115] p Practicing Community Can Start Now / Moving Beyond Money
Eventually, the community would also stop using money outside. People would meet their needs directly, or through exchange (be it trade, gift, work, obligation, or whatever). I couldn’t find a well-documented modern community that has gone this far, and in fact, it may be impractical in some locations because of government requirements like taxes on land. So perhaps the practical end point here is a community that is nearly entirely moneyless, but has a small collective cash boundary on the outside.

[C116] p Practicing Community Can Start Now / Moving Beyond Money
None of this does away with scarcity or economic power. There are lots of areas where such power can be wielded: access to food storage, housing, tools, land, medicine, transportation, and the outside world’s currency, to name a few. Even in a moneyless community, someone can control what’s scarce. Who decides how these things are budgeted and distributed? What do members owe one another? What happens when someone wants to leave?

[C117] h1 Founderism: The Four Questions /
Founderism: The Four Questions

[C118] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
In every community there was somebody (or some founding group) who took the initiative to get it started. They found the land, wrote the first documents, taught the method, recruited the first members, or kept things going when everybody else lost interest. Like the U.S. Founding Fathers.

[C119] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
The founder(s) end(s) up with real influence, and pretending they don’t just makes that influence harder to look at. When that power fails to get distributed as the conditions ripen for that, it’s called founderism, which is a fledgling form of what can become a subtle dictatorship.

[C120] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
I’ve seen founderism in many communes that I’ve visited, and I can see it playing out if I were to be a founder as well. I’ve got all these proposals and visions here in my guides, and so it would be very easy for me to become the person who decides whether or not everyone else “understood” them correctly, even while I wish to remain humble and open to the input of all involved.

[C121] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
A few things help keep that in check. One is to judge the method by results people can actually see. Will they see the members safely flourishing without being trapped? Able to leave without stigma? Can conflicts get resolved? Are the kids doing well once they grow up? And if the community is meant to spread, do new projects actually start up and last? Those questions seem more important to me than how big or old the commune gets, or how many decisions are made by consensus.

[C122] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
The questions so far have been about the method. These four are about the founder:

[C123] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
Can the community respectfully override the founder(s)?

[C124] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
Can the community replace the role of the founder(s)?

[C125] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
Can the community alter the vision of the founder(s)?

[C126] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
Can the community exist without the founder(s)?

[C127] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
Then follow the practical power. Whose name is on the deed? Who’s in charge of the resources? Who keeps the records? Who decides who gets admitted, and who picks the people who review the founder? Who controls access to the kids, or to medicine, and who can make a complaint go away? The org chart might look flat and horizontal, while the keys, passwords, titles and even just general reputation all point to one person or clique.

[C128] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
Presence of one of these alone isn’t proof of abuse, but if several are present, they are worth paying attention to before the founder(s) can explain how the situation is uniquely different in a spiritual way. Ask these questions about any community, teacher or method, including me.

[C129] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
While founderism is a problem in every community, it’s especially so in communities which form naturally over time as I’m describing may happen in my case. In such cases, there’s no formal community and no formal rules until people decide to come together to make them. It doesn’t make sense to do that at first, if, say, I’m the only one there and I don’t know who else will be there that I could trust. If there’s one other person who comes to visit, it also doesn’t make sense, so there’s a critical mass point at which people decide “we were just living together like friends, but now we want to formalize the community.”

[C130] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
If I could find a suitable commune to join, that would basically reverse this problem. I would then be the one on probation. And if we are all starting together from scratch, then we would all be at an equal probationary level with each other.

How My Own Model Could Become a Cult

I could totally start a cult. Trust me.

[C133] p Founderism: The Four Questions / How My Own Model Could Become a Cult
As if a founderism-type subtle dictatorship isn’t bad enough, we often find groups that are even proudly and overtly controlled by some Pharaoh at the top of the pyramid, especially when the organization of the group is as advanced as what it took to build the Great Pyramids.

[C134] p Founderism: The Four Questions / How My Own Model Could Become a Cult
Every practice in this article can be turned against the people using it.

[C135] p Founderism: The Four Questions / How My Own Model Could Become a Cult
“Nothing suppressed, everything processed” can become a situation where someone in the group must tell all, even if they don’t want to. I have seen that risk in groups that use the ZEGG Forum practice. A group starts out inviting honesty and ends up treating privacy as resistance. But privacy also can be resistance to the communal spirit, so there has to be a living, always re-assessed balance between what you and the community owe each other, and what remains your own domain. In general, people can have their own private practices, of course. But some practices go too far against the communal spirit. If someone starts worshipping Satan, say, they get booted.

[C136] p Founderism: The Four Questions / How My Own Model Could Become a Cult
Peer counseling can become peer surveillance, even if the tone is warm.

[C137] p Founderism: The Four Questions / How My Own Model Could Become a Cult
Dissent is important for growth. That doesn’t mean everything everyone says is equally valid, though. Sometimes it really is their wounded ego projection, and that should be acknowledged. But again, balance, because if complaints are too often or too broadly painted as projection, that’s as much a failure, if not more so.

[C138] p Founderism: The Four Questions / How My Own Model Could Become a Cult
There are some basic human rights that can’t be voted away from you by the majority in a community, unless it’s a question of resource availability as a whole. If safety and the conditions for health can be provided, they should be provided. People should be able to maintain relationships with others outside the community, as long as those relationships don’t interfere with the community as a whole (for example, you can’t bring your boyfriend from outside to live with you if he’s antagonistic towards the commune).

[C139] p Founderism: The Four Questions / How My Own Model Could Become a Cult
It’s not possible to spell everything out ahead of time. Let’s say a grandmother is demonizing the commune. Cutting off contact with her could make things way worse. When it’s tricky and dangerous for everyone like that, I think the commune needs to step in and help people decide how to communicate with outsiders.

[C140] h1 From One Community to a Movement /
From One Community to a Movement

[C141] p From One Community to a Movement /
The problem of how these communities stay connected does eventually need to be solved somehow, if the idea is for them to spread without building another state. It should be solved in a way that allows help between communities and the movement of people and knowledge, and also keeps the differences alive, so communities can keep learning from one another. At the federation level, that outward purpose gets concrete: spreading healing practices, training people, seeding new projects, and keeping any one comfortable enclave from thinking it’s the whole movement.

[C142] p From One Community to a Movement /
The Federation of Egalitarian Communities (FEC) says it’s rebuilding, and that’s a good warning. It takes work to run a federation. Delegates. Records. Compliance. Coordinating resources. Holding meetings. Succession. Whatever dispute function it actually takes on. These things all take up labor and material capacity before any local crisis has eaten into both. If the legal or economic environment requires money, the federation also needs to have a cash budget for dealing with it. But the work of federation doesn’t have to be done by a single entity. Two communities might want to be closely linked as friends, and that doesn’t stop both of them from also being part of a general federation. There can also be local clusters, or single-function institutions like the FEC’s PEACH health pool, which isn’t currently governed by the FEC delegates.

[C143] p From One Community to a Movement /
Cooperation also shouldn’t slide into assimilation. The FEC has full members and communities working toward full membership, and Friends who may just want to stay friends. Fortunately, the interface between communities can often be thin, saying who’s responsible for what while somebody crosses between them, and communities don’t have to have the same internal systems for it to work.

[C144] p From One Community to a Movement /
Solidarity doesn’t mean every community is liable for whatever the others owe, and it doesn’t mean anyone has an unlimited claim on another community’s labor and stores, or its land and tools. Even if nobody uses money internally, communities still need the same honesty about exchanges and obligations. Decide in advance which offers of help are purely goodwill and which go on a ledger of mutual aid. The federation can keep track of these for each purpose, without having to put a value on them or require every exchange to be “even”.

[C145] p From One Community to a Movement /
Whenever a community or a federation creates or applies a rule, it’s best practice to store the full case history (with private details protected) so it can be re-evaluated in future: what happened, the principles that actually conflicted, your first judgments, and your final decision and rationale. Any dissenting opinions should go in there as well, along with the consequences in the case. When the rule changes because of a new case, run the old case through again to see how it would turn out. That saves each generation from having to rediscover the same constitutional fight from scratch.

[C146] h2 From One Community to a Movement / Experiment Before You Split Off
Experiment Before You Split Off

[C147] p From One Community to a Movement / Experiment Before You Split Off
Experimenting with a different rule doesn’t have to mean everybody goes out and buys new land right away. Try it inside the parent group first, then as a semi-autonomous project, and then, if it needs more independence, as a seed group nearby that still shares some tools, childcare, business or friendships. Splitting off completely should be just one step along that gradient, and nobody should have to do it just to be allowed to experiment.

[C148] p From One Community to a Movement / Experiment Before You Split Off
Some experiments (like a different land system, or a different population size, or any number of rules that work together) can’t be tried in a way that’s cheap. They may look bad when they’re starved of the assets they actually need. “We tried it in one room for three months” isn’t always a fair test.

[C149] h1 The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For

[C150] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
Many perfectly intelligent grownups would rather sit around for six hours talking about solar panels than talk about jealousy, God, death, childhood, money, sex, psychedelics, or somebody wanting to leave, which is what I ask people to do in this article.

[C151] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
This doesn’t surprise me, since we’re taught to put on a show of competence in modern society. We’re given a lot of incentives to hide ourselves and disappear behind our screens the second things get uncomfortable. Emotional intelligence doesn’t pay the bills. Instead we optimize to fit the career market with all the little energy we’ve got.

[C152] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
So then a lot of people end up thinking they aren’t “good enough” for community, or getting labeled by others that way. And there’s a real issue there, but it’s more about what fits where & when than good or bad.

[C153] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
Some people have engaged in long practices of contemplation or therapy, or have had very good luck in childhood; other people are just trying to survive some emergency in their life and need care more than responsibility. Most of us are all of these at different times in our lives.

[C154] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
In the model I’m imagining, some of the functions that are outsourced to therapists in modern society are distributed throughout the community.

[C155] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
That does not make the community infinitely absorbent like a Heavy Flow Maxi Pad.

[C156] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
I’m going to use the phrase “free resource,” which comes from Re-evaluation Counseling, to refer to the attention, steadiness, skill and practical capacity people have left over for others once they’ve dealt with enough of their own immediate stuff, and “need” to refer to the amount of help people need at a given time.

[C157] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
My own need has exceeded my free resource at many points. Good Lord. During one stretch, my first wife tried to kill me while psychotic because she believed I was her sociopathic father. Then she took my son. Later she tried to kill my mother, while her pimp stalked my sister’s family until they moved to Canada.

[C158] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
I could go on and on, but the math is pretty clear by now. There wasn’t much of me left over for anybody else. So when I say someone might need to stabilize and do inner pl/ork before helping found a community, I’m not saying it from a mountaintop.

[C159] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
The ability to welcome somebody is partly a problem of resources, then, not compassion -- how many people can a group take in without hurting the existing members or the people they are taking in?

[C160] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
And that also brings us to a difficult catch-22 for fledgling groups. The need for new members in a small community is greater, but the ability of that community to absorb disruption is less. With only eight people in the community, someone still has to grow the food and fix whatever breaks. Somebody has to balance the books and watch the kids. And the water system has to keep working. The water well doesn’t care how beautiful the new member’s heart is.

[C161] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
So early on, members usually need to have more available resource than acute need, and some practical skills to contribute. They usually need to be willing to learn, too. Once there are 80 members, though, there’s usually a lot more free resource to draw on.

[C162] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
I still don’t know how to keep this language of capacity from becoming a respectable way to exclude people who are disabled, poor, less articulate, maybe simply uncomfortable or unfamiliar with therapeutic language, or for whatever reason deemed inconvenient. Pretending capacity is unlimited won’t solve that. Any real process would need concrete criteria, review by someone who wasn’t part of the decision, and a way to revisit a “not now” decision on membership. Even then, the power to veto membership applications based on capacity remains dangerously tilted towards elitism.

[C163] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
But before you submit your application to the community, submit it to yourself. This community may not be a good fit for you right now if:

[C164] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
You have a need that far outstrips your free resource right now. This doesn’t mean you’re bad or doomed, it’s about timing; it just means you need to take a first step of finding support where you are and building up your resources. If you don’t have a free resource calculator on hand, an easy way to tell is if you’re running from some crisis right now and mainly need a place to land, get treatment, housing, legal aid, etc. A founding group shouldn’t pretend it can give you those.

[C165] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
You want the feeling of village life but don’t want to take on governance, maintenance, conflict, care, etc.

[C166] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For /
You’re not willing to look at yourself when jealousy or conflict comes up.

[C167] h2 The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
From Visitor to Member—Slowly

[C168] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
When The Farm grew fast, a lot of the people who came were in need, and there weren’t many filters that worked. Being open to everybody felt so spiritual! The practical burden piled up anyway. A membership process is there to protect both the people who are already there and the newcomers.

[C169] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
First, correspond with the visitor. Did they read the materials? (Not everyone does.) Do they get the basic idea of why this community is here? Are they at least starting to do the relevant practices? It’s a lot cheaper to find out through a thoughtful exchange than after six months of misunderstanding, right?

[C170] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
The short visit: If they come for a week or two and just participate in normal life, well, most people can be charming for two weeks. Not exactly a high bar. 😅 So that’s about all you’ll find out.

[C171] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
The long visit: If you’re not around long enough to actually experience inconvenience and conflict with the person in question, then you’re not going to learn very much about them. Watch how they react when working and making decisions in the group. When tired, corrected, attracted, disappointed, bored, etc. (Hopefully not all at once!) Even a professional faker may be able to pull off an act for months.

[C172] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
Taking on responsibilities: If someone will have a role in your group that has serious consequences, involving kids, shared resources, medicine, vehicles or private records, you want to know how they handle that specific thing. I wouldn’t hand someone the van keys just because they’re great with kids. You can find out by checking whether you’ve seen them do this thing before, and asking for references if appropriate. Give people access to these things in stages and ensure there’s always some way to appeal decisions about these roles to a separate authority if needed.

[C173] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
Write down your answers: How long is a provisional membership, and what are a provisional member’s rights and responsibilities? How is it reviewed, and how does someone leave it? Who decides on full membership? (Hint: not the person who recruited them! 😜) And what rights does a full member have to the land, the shared resources and a voice in the group, and what do they owe over the long term? (Those go in your founding agreements.)

[C174] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
Then there are the people who aren’t fully “in” the commune politically. Visitors, volunteers, workers, renters and interns at the commune (yes, even interns) are entitled to some rights when decisions really affect their pay or access to common resources. All of them should have clear terms, and the chance to be heard and to have such decisions reviewed. The same is true for decisions really affecting their schedule, safety, housing, records or discipline. Equality that stops at the membership line can end up recreating a servant class that moves from commune to commune.

[C175] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
And when communal therapy processes are pre-reqs for people to get medicine or to be respected members, it can feel like the community is gatekeeping. Well, it is gatekeeping. Some people simply should not be let in the gate, at least not yet. And some people will resent that, and choose to leave, and that’s fine. Some communities will be stricter gatekeepers, and others will be more lax.

[C176] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
It should go without saying that nobody has to take medicine to have equal respect in the community. But medicine is an optional part of the overall therapy process, and if a member is avoiding the therapy process and becoming hard to deal with, they will naturally not belong at a certain point. If it gets to the point of being impossible to live with, they will be kicked out. But nearly always, people who don’t fit in leave way before that.

[C177] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
They should not be demonized for not trying to fit in. They should be offered compassion, but ultimately it’s up to them if they want to fit in and follow the communal processes. And in some cases, the community may make exceptions. If someone is actually sick and needs care, kicking them out could be like a death sentence. So it’s up to the people involved.

[C178] h1 Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution /
Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution

[C179] h2 Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / Leaving Voluntarily
Leaving Voluntarily

[C180] p Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / Leaving Voluntarily
Good people leave for good reasons -- dying parent, love, work, health, exhaustion, change of mind, etc. You don’t want them to lose everything they’ve put in. That’s coercive -- they HAVE to stay if they can’t afford to go, and a community of prisoners isn’t ideal. Whether or not you use money, you’ll need to have a way of providing people with resources when they leave (cash, if you use it, but also tools, transportation, temporary housing, stored up goods, the right to keep using something, or whatever). Otherwise their “right” to leave is only on paper -- it only works when the community happens to have spare money. Write up the exit terms while leaving is still only theoretical -- like a pre-nup when you’re planning the wedding. Not romantic, but protective. Much easier than doing it once anybody’s furious.

[C181] h2 Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / When Somebody Has to Go
When Somebody Has to Go

[C182] p Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / When Somebody Has to Go
A safe community can’t promise to keep everyone. There may be times when someone has to go. If someone is a serious or generalized danger to others, or keeps violating essential boundaries, or for whatever reason just makes everyday life together impossible for everyone else, it can be the right call to eject them. If restorative work can help in a reasonable amount of time, all the better. But it’s not the community’s job to be a treatment program for someone who’s endangering everyone else.

[C183] p Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / When Somebody Has to Go
This power to remove people is so easily abused, though. So work out the process before it’s about anyone in particular. What counts as cause for immediate protective action? Who decides to put temporary restrictions on someone? Who will make sure all the evidence is preserved? How will the person be able to reply to the concerns? What protections will there be for the person’s interests (housing, records, family contact, access to common resources, outside funds if any) during the separation? When is it time to bring in outside help?

[C184] p Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / When Somebody Has to Go
Note that “outside” doesn’t have to mean the nation-state. My research initially leaned too far that way. The basic need here is for a competent level of authority that is not reviewing its own conduct. That could mean another community, a federation of communities, professionals, autonomous regional institutions, courts, regulatory agencies, emergency services, etc. The key is that it’s not the same people.

[C185] p Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / When Somebody Has to Go
Also decide on a mediator or ombudsperson in advance. Why bother? People often won’t trust someone they haven’t already met, who comes in during a crisis as a “neutral stranger.” A mediator doesn’t obviate the need for evidence, safeguarding, rulings, etc. Mediation is an attempt to restore good relations between people. When people can’t or won’t consent to being in community any more, they still need a final separation.

[C186] h2 Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / When the Community Dissolves
When the Community Dissolves

[C187] p Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / When the Community Dissolves
Dissolution is something founders rarely want to talk about while they’re picking a name for their community and looking forward to its first harvest. Who would? But that’s exactly when it needs to be talked about.

[C188] p Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / When the Community Dissolves
If the community dissolves, what happens to the land and any outside debts? What about its buildings, equipment, common stores or funds? What happens to each member’s agreed rights? More immediately, who is responsible for ensuring that everyone has a place to live and food to eat? Who takes responsibility for making sure people get their medications and the other care they need? Who keeps childcare going, and makes sure the kids’ schooling carries on? Who takes responsibility for transportation, for the records, for family contact, for the animals, for making sure land obligations are met, and for any unresolved claims? That’s a lot of loose ends!

[C189] p Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / When the Community Dissolves
Think of it like a living will, and assign a bridge or successor for each critical function in advance, so basic life doesn’t depend on reviving the commune. The institution may not be salvageable, but who’s making breakfast tomorrow?

[C190] p Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / When the Community Dissolves
Dissolution terms are also a way for a group to end without turning every friendship into evidence. Who wants that? People may leave hurt, but they keep the transition support they agreed on, and their dignity. And they can tell a coherent story of what happened.

[C191] p Leaving, Removal, and Dissolution / When the Community Dissolves

Even in a culture of peer medicine, there could still be an informal “shaman”, someone everyone trusts and gradually stops questioning, even though nobody gave them the title. I don’t know yet how to avoid this. Things that might help would be to have rotation of roles, and public accountability. Though neither of these seem like a complete solution.

[C277] p 3: The Medicine Part - Yes, I’m Naming It / No Special Shamans
Still, I want people to learn to guide themselves and care for each other, within the limits of their competence.

[C278] h2 3: The Medicine Part - Yes, I’m Naming It / My Communal Ceremony Principles
My Communal Ceremony Principles

[C279] p 3: The Medicine Part - Yes, I’m Naming It / My Communal Ceremony Principles
I’ve written before about best psychedelic ceremony practices, from courses I took, but I will sum up here what’s most relevant to this article.

[C280] p 3: The Medicine Part - Yes, I’m Naming It / My Communal Ceremony Principles
Just because it’s a peer-led ceremony doesn’t mean it’s ok to be casually irresponsible. In fact, since the authority is spread around, there needs to be more competence.

[C281] p 3: The Medicine Part - Yes, I’m Naming It / My Communal Ceremony Principles
All of the “supports” are important: preparation, nutrition, screening for drug interactions, being disciplined about the dose, having the right kind of sober sitter present, and having a plan for dealing with emergencies. Those matter more than ceremony aesthetics. My current safety material is at Altered States Triage.

[C282] p 3: The Medicine Part - Yes, I’m Naming It / My Communal Ceremony Principles
While there may be some pure learning that could come from having several altered members figure out what rules and boundaries are needed on the spot, it’s not something I recommend.

[C283] p 3: The Medicine Part - Yes, I’m Naming It / My Communal Ceremony Principles
Also, I am especially cautious about medicines and protocols that I see as more destabilizing and/or presenting more of a medical burden. This includes some uses of ketamine and MDMA and bufo. My reasons are given in those articles, respectively, since they need more space.

[C284] h2 3: The Medicine Part - Yes, I’m Naming It / After the Psychedelic Calling
After the Psychedelic Calling

[C285] p 3: The Medicine Part - Yes, I’m Naming It / After the Psychedelic Calling
A lot of people feel the urge to form a village after going through some serious psychedelic and/or mystical experiences. Finding their people and helping others get to whatever just opened up is part of the same urge. That call may be real, but forming a community based on omnipotence is a terrible idea.

[C286] p 3: The Medicine Part - Yes, I’m Naming It / After the Psychedelic Calling
I’ve felt that urge myself. The problem is that it can come with grandiosity, and I’ve felt that too. You come back believing love can heal anyone, and then you meet people who don’t want to change, people who have some part of them that sincerely wants to change yet they keep choosing the opposite, and people in such a crisis that their need for help is more than your enthusiasm can hold.

[C287] p 3: The Medicine Part - Yes, I’m Naming It / After the Psychedelic Calling
Once the euphoria wears off and people have a more sober view of the call they feel, it can become useful. A community can be a place for people to train up in the practices, and sometimes go off and start another experiment. In fact, if people are training there so they can help others build communities of their own, they’d be working towards forming a real alternative. That answers the reasonable objection that communes are just privileged escape pods, which gets double dipped with the objection that psychedelics are also privileged escape pods (or, alternatively, as some say, “a vacation for the poor”).

[C288] h1 What Community Looks Like From Sénégal /
What Community Looks Like From Sénégal

[C289] figcaption What Community Looks Like From Sénégal /
The “African Renaissance Monument” in Dakar, Sénégal, which stands at 160 feet tall and took 4 years to build.

[C290] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal /
I live in Sénégal, where “intentional community” often sounds like a strange Western phrase. People here are like, “Don’t human beings already do that?”

[C291] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal /
Westerners often start out isolated and try to rebuild a tribe. A lot of Africans start out with a communal life they inherited, and watch parts of it fall apart. Kind of the opposite problem. And urban Africans who get interested in intentional community might come to it feeling alienated from both the city and the tradition they left.

[C292] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal /
So this is a global issue, far from some Western lifestyle niche. There’s still knowledge in the living villages that no book can give you. But if you visit, remember they’re people, not museums. Would you like people coming to your house to stare at you and your family? So be respectful, contribute something, learn the language, and don’t expect anyone to hand you a spiritual service package.

[C293] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal /
Oh, and a practical note: getting my residency in Sénégal was pretty cheap and straightforward. French and Wolof matter a lot more than English. And the country’s famous ideal of Teranga (hospitality) is real in my life. I’m staying in a friend’s family’s house, and they won’t let me pay rent! Of course that generosity lives right alongside watchfulness, witchcraft, social conservatism, bureaucracy and all the usual complications of a real country.

[C294] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal /
Sénégal is unlike anywhere else I’ve lived. It’s the only Sufi Muslim country (aside from Gambia, a tiny nation inside Sénégal). Sufi muslims are the ones who value a deep, personal connection with God, rather than just following the religious rules. Five times a day the prayers pour out into the streets. I find them so soothing. And Sufism doesn’t stay inside designated “spiritual” places here. It’s in the music, in the pictures everywhere, in the murals, in the names, in the conversations. Prostitution is also legal here.

[C295] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal /
And the humor! People are outside laughing for hours at a time. This is the most joking culture I’ve ever been in, and I keep wondering if I’ll ever understand the really complicated jokes in Wolof. Basic conversation is one thing. But knowing why twelve people are suddenly crying with laughter? That’s graduate school.

[C296] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal /
In the villages and families I’ve spent time around, so much is taken care of by the extended family and the local memory, alongside the formal systems. Whether it’s land, childcare, housing, food, obligations, etc, etc. Who needs a workshop called “Rebuilding Intergenerational Belonging” when some aunt has already given three people a task?

[C297] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal /
Even in Dakar, the overpopulated capital, I found this graffiti on a wall: “Tolerance! Openness! Union! Happiness! Love!” And elsewhere there are murals of Sufi leaders. Spiritual graffiti still surprises me. Where I grew up, a city wall was either selling you a product or showing off somebody’s inability to draw a penis. But the “tolerance” part is funny…

[C298] figcaption What Community Looks Like From Sénégal /
Graffiti in Dakar, Sénégal: Tolerance! Openness! Union! Happiness! Love!

[C299] h2 What Community Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
All is not well in paradise

[C300] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
Does this mean I think Sénégal is some magical place that’s kept its communal paradise? No, not quite. The warmth has hard limits here. One noteworthy example is the plight of LGBTQ+ people here, who cross them automatically.

[C301] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
Sénégal passed a law in March 2026 that increased the penalty for “consensual same-sex intimacy” to 5 to 10 years in prison, and it also made public advocacy for homosexuality a crime. Homosexuals were already facing arrests, family rejection, harassment, assault and mob violence, so ironically, some say they’re actually safer once they’re arrested, because then at least the mob can’t get at them. And also ironically, LGBTQ+ people are using this prejudice in their favor to get refugee status and live in rich countries.

[C302] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
So yes, it’s possible for a society to be spiritually alive, generous, funny and super communal, while still becoming cruel to those who break its social categories. Being in community isn’t automatically liberating. It all depends on who is allowed to belong without pretending.

[C303] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
One other shadow side of the otherwise forgiving social culture here is that people may go to great lengths to avoid confrontation. Who stole the sheep? We all know, but nobody wants to cause a stink by bringing it up. 🙉

[C304] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
Where I live, neighbors steal our moringa pods every morning. Every morning! They could plant a moringa tree in their own yard. Apparently maintaining this relationship with ours is more entertaining. 😂

[C305] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
Everybody here has sheep (even me), and they are super cute. It can feel like you’re living in another world, with the music, the animals, the joking, the praying, and just being around people all the time.

[C306] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
Of course, the old communal ways here are changing. Young people move to the city, get a job, get a phone, eat imported food, become part of the wider world, etc. (some of the men even make the dangerous journey by sea to Europe). So what about the families? The ones that used to share almost everything are now more reliant on cash and individual opportunities.

[C307] h2 What Community Looks Like From Sénégal / Lessons from the Black Tax
Lessons from the Black Tax

[C308] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal / Lessons from the Black Tax
Then there’s the so-called “Black Tax,” a strange legacy of the old communal system: if you’ve got any money at all, you’re expected to share it with your parents, your siblings, your cousins, to pay school fees, medical bills, funerals, etc. I understand the instinct. In the ancestral way of doing things nobody made it alone, and the people who fed and raised you had a real claim on whatever prosperity came through you later. So why not share in return? That’s how the whole organism survived (it wasn’t charity).

[C309] p What Community Looks Like From Sénégal / Lessons from the Black Tax
But these days it can be soul destroying. People have a lot less of the communal life that held the obligation up (the shared land, work, childcare, food and daily belonging), but they still carry it. So if one of them makes it into the cash economy, it’s really hard to build any independence or long-term stability, because they may be expected to carry everybody. How can they get ahead, when all these people are dependent on them? And the relatives stay dependent too, since the payments don’t give them a real alternative. It’s the ancestral ethic playing out inside an individualistic cash economy. It can keep people alive, but it eats away at both sides too.
