Most communes of my acquaintance went wrong from the inside, in one of two ways.

[A037] h2 Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways / The secular failure
The secular failure

[A038] p Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways / The secular failure
The secular communes had a solution for how to distribute property or labor, but when the deep waters of jealousy or childhood hurt or humiliation or attraction or resentment or grief came up, the group was without a common method for dealing with it. The feelings just became agenda items.

[A039] p Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways / The secular failure
Meeting procedure isn’t going to solve every problem, no matter how sophisticated the system of labor and money and decision making may be in a community of the Federation of Egalitarian Communities or any other income-sharing community. It can’t make it so that two people don’t hate each other. It can only decide whose turn it is to talk about the hatred, and whether a motion about it needs somebody to second it.

[A040] p Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways / The secular failure
Eventually, when people are together long enough, politics will become a way of carrying emotional material that people can’t name. There will be a fight about a schedule or committee or noise or food or some policy or other, but it won’t be about that. There’s no shared way to talk about the hurt itself, but the meeting agenda is the one place the group lets you push for something and win, so the angry communard goes there, trying to get some justice.

[A041] h2 Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways / The spiritual failure
The spiritual failure

[A042] p Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways / The spiritual failure
This comes about when the community realizes the importance of embracing a transformation from within, and then funnels it through a particular person, usually the founder, a teacher, a channel or some other special knower of how things really are.

[A043] p Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways / The spiritual failure
The Farm, in Tennessee, at its largest about 1,500 people, had many beautiful values. But in the early days, the community’s spirituality and social life followed the founder, Stephen Gaskin. From what former members have shared, the community’s doctrine ran deep into members’ intimate decisions. This is how it often goes: folks talk about oneness with the universe, while one man defines what the universe is.

[A044] p Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways / The spiritual failure
The two failures mirror each other. Secular groups distribute power but leave the inner life mostly private or ignored. Spiritual groups attempt to address the inner life, but because it’s the inner world being shaped by the outer as a condition of membership, we tend to find both real-world and spiritual power concentrating around whoever defines the path.

[A045] p Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways / The spiritual failure
The deeper problem is that somebody who’s earned authority in one role can use it in all their other roles. The founder who is a gifted teacher, the therapist who really helps people, the farmer or business manager that actually carries a lot of the economy. The danger starts when one person has the ability to define the problem, control the information related to the problem, define what the solution is, and make it hard for people to leave or express disagreement.

[A046] p Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways / The spiritual failure
I dream of a way to work in depth that isn’t run by a guru, and a way of being in a community with distributed authority. A place where people who’ve done the healing first can join without pretending they’re fully finished, and that also has peer practice and enough play that it doesn’t become a permanent repair shop.

[A069] h3 The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together / 4. The container
4. The container

[A070] p The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together / 4. The container
This is the part about membership, land, resources, governance, children, leaving, etc.

[A071] p The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together / 4. The container
Like my father, I’m aiming for communities with less and less money involved, since money is antagonistic to the spirit of community. If I have money, I can get whatever I want, regardless of whether anyone cares about my wellbeing. That’s the hegemonic philosophy of life we are trying to overturn. I think the path there has three stages.

[A072] p The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together / 4. The container
First there will be no money exchanged between members for basic community functions like food, housing, care, tools, education, transport etc. Members might have outside jobs, and they bring an agreed share of that income in. This is probably a good place to start if someone wants to create a community now and have it be realistic.

[A073] p The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together / 4. The container
Later all wages and other income earned by the members from work outside the community, or from business inside the community, will be paid into the common purse of the community. No member accumulates private wealth while a member of the community. Some version of this is how things have been done in the Bruderhof community for generations, and my father lived there for 17 years. It is also done, in a somewhat different way, at Twin Oaks, and a number of other income-sharing communities. So a common purse isn’t just a theory.

[A074] p The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together / 4. The container
Eventually, the community would also stop using money outside. People would meet their needs directly, or through exchange (be it trade, gift, work, obligation, or whatever). I couldn’t find a well-documented modern community that has gone this far, and in fact, it may be impractical in some locations because of government requirements like taxes on land. So perhaps the practical end point here is a community that is nearly entirely moneyless, but has a small collective cash boundary on the outside.

[A075] p The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together / 4. The container
None of this does away with scarcity or economic power. There are lots of areas where such power can be wielded: access to food storage, housing, tools, land, medicine, transportation, and the outside world’s currency, to name a few. Even in a moneyless community, someone can control what’s scarce. Who decides how these things are distributed? What do members owe one another? What happens when someone wants to leave?

[A076] p The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together / 4. The container
These are less exciting topics to discuss spiritually. It’s rare to see someone come back from an ecstatic vision with news of how to revamp the community’s policies on provisional members, or how to divvy up assets with a departing member, or how to prevent one founder from having control over the land, or what to do when a child reports something serious. And if they do, it’s still something that needs a sober thinking-through.

[A077] p The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together / 4. The container
But no amount of gorgeous land will make up for the mess that is created when one person owns the land or has the final say about what will happen on it. And when members come on board faster than the group can support them, all the peer counseling in the world won’t make up for it, any more than medicine makes up for the lack of a written exit agreement.

[A078] p The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together / 4. The container

What impressed me the most was how they do authority. It’s mandar obedeciendo, to lead by obeying: the representatives carry the decisions people made in the assemblies, and they have to answer to the people who gave them the mandate.

[A184] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas /
The Zapatistas are also the closest thing to a whole political-economic example that I found, because their autonomy goes beyond how they vote. They’ve set up their own education and health care, and their own justice, and they run their own collective production. On top of that they’ve worked out ways of teaching outsiders, all while coordinating beyond a single village.

[A185] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas /
The Fire and the Word describes the movement growing from family to village, village to region, and region to larger structures. It also gives one of the most useful Zapatista principles for community design: build the practice first and develop the theory afterward. The autonomous municipalities came first; an association of them became a rehearsal for the Good Government Juntas (Boards). When different municipalities encountered different problems and uneven progress, that experience generated another structure rather than being forced to fit the original plan.

[A186] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas /
After a long internal review, in 2023, the Zapatistas announced they were dissolving their previous federated governmental structure, which was split into Rebel Zapatista Autonomous Municipalities and the Good Government Juntas. The original idea was to have power vested in the local communities, but it had crept up and consolidated itself at higher levels over time for practical reasons. Their new arrangement moved authority downward toward a Local Autonomous Government in each community.

[A187] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas /
Institutions get more and more layers and offices as they age. We see this same pattern happen repeatedly, everywhere in history, except usually it goes one way without reversal, unless there’s a revolution.

[A188] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas /
But there is a special reason the Zapatistas were successfully able to reverse this degeneration. Zapatista autonomy grew out of Indigenous communities with long histories. They share language and family ties. They’ve fought land struggles together, and defended themselves together for decades.

[A189] h2 The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Exporting Zapatismo via the Escuelita

[A190] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Exporting Zapatismo via the Escuelita
For the 10th anniversary of their autonomous governments, the Zapatistas opened up the Escuelita (little school). The goal of the escuelita was to expand their anarcho-communal, spiritual-humanist revolution worldwide. Outsiders came from all over the world, lived with host families there and learned autonomy by taking part in day to day life. Around 1,500 students came to the first sessions, and thousands more came to later ones.

[A191] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Exporting Zapatismo via the Escuelita
But despite actually trying to train people in their model, the Zapatistas themselves have been clear that people shouldn’t expect to directly or easily import/export their philosophy (Zapatismo) as something ready-made, shake & bake. A new ecovillage can’t copy the diagram everyone can see and get the trust underneath it right away.

[A192] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Exporting Zapatismo via the Escuelita
Now, the Zapatistas also don’t prove my whole money-free economy goal. They still use money internally in some cases, not others, and they still use it externally for trade. They’ve also benefitted from outside economic help, while at the same time making efforts to remain politically independent from donors and more self-sustaining.

[A193] figcaption The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Exporting Zapatismo via the Escuelita
Escuelita Zapatista — The Zapatista school to teach outsiders their way of life

[A194] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Exporting Zapatismo via the Escuelita
One way an escuelita could fail is if it becomes a business, where residents spend their lives performing community for paying outsiders, and success gets measured by how many people come instead of how many daughter communities there are. A week of feeling transformed isn’t what it’s for. It’s for teaching enough of the social and material system that some people can build somewhere else.

[A195] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Exporting Zapatismo via the Escuelita
As part of my research I have also looked for evidence of what happened after the Escuelita. While I found information on alumni networks, people bringing practices back with them, and organizing later, I couldn’t find a clear chain of somebody having been to the Escuelita, then a new communal institution being set up, and then evidence about how its members and their children actually did over time. [A196] p The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas / Exporting Zapatismo via the Escuelita
Sociocracy has a very different history, yet some similar features have emerged there as well: circles, decision-making by consent, distributed authority, and linking between the levels. Does this prove there’s a universal formula? No. But it means that people building institutions of this sort have another serious system to study, instead of inventing their governance during their first dispute.

[A198] h2 From One Community to a Movement / Experiment Before You Split Off
Experiment Before You Split Off

[A199] p From One Community to a Movement / Experiment Before You Split Off
Experimenting with a different rule doesn’t have to mean everybody goes out and buys new land right away. Try it inside the parent group first, then as a semi-autonomous project, and then, if it needs more independence, as a seed group nearby that still shares some tools, childcare, business or friendships. Splitting off completely should be just one step along that gradient, and nobody should have to do it just to be allowed to experiment.

[A200] p From One Community to a Movement / Experiment Before You Split Off
Some experiments (like a different land system, or a different population size, or any number of rules that work together) can’t be tried in a way that’s cheap. They may look bad when they’re starved of the assets they actually need. “We tried it in one room for three months” isn’t always a fair test.

[A201] h2 From One Community to a Movement / Federated Communities Make a Movement
Federated Communities Make a Movement

[A202] p From One Community to a Movement / Federated Communities Make a Movement
The problem of how these communities stay connected does eventually need to be solved somehow, if the idea is for them to spread without building another state. It should be solved in a way that allows help between communities and the movement of people and knowledge, and also keeps the differences alive, so communities can keep learning from one another. At the federation level, that outward purpose gets concrete: spreading healing practices, training people, seeding new projects, and keeping any one comfortable enclave from thinking it’s the whole movement.

[A203] p From One Community to a Movement / Federated Communities Make a Movement
The Federation of Egalitarian Communities (FEC) says it’s rebuilding, and that’s a good warning. It takes work to run a federation. Delegates. Records. Compliance. Coordinating resources. Holding meetings. Succession. Whatever dispute function it actually takes on. These things all take up labor and material capacity before any local crisis has eaten into both. If the legal or economic environment requires money, the federation also needs to have a cash budget for dealing with it. But the work of federation doesn’t have to be done by a single entity. Two communities might want to be closely linked as friends, and that doesn’t stop both of them from also being part of a general federation. There can also be local clusters, or single-function institutions like the FEC’s PEACH health pool, which isn’t currently governed by the FEC delegates.

[A204] p From One Community to a Movement / Federated Communities Make a Movement
Cooperation also shouldn’t slide into assimilation. The FEC has full members and communities working toward full membership, and Friends who may just want to stay friends. Fortunately, the interface between communities can often be thin, saying who’s responsible for what while somebody crosses between them, and communities don’t have to have the same internal systems for it to work.

[A205] p From One Community to a Movement / Federated Communities Make a Movement
Even if nobody uses money internally, communities still need the same honesty about exchanges and obligations. A gift of food, pooled medicine, a vehicle or a tool on loan, trades of labor, emergency housing, a shared building project, a visit that’s mainly for training or relationship building: they’re all different events, and some of them create future obligations while others don’t. The federation can keep track of these for each purpose, without having to put a value on them or require every exchange to be “even”.

[A206] p From One Community to a Movement / Federated Communities Make a Movement
If cash is used for transactions with the outside world, grants and loans need to be kept separate the same way, and so do guarantees and shared enterprise commitments. Solidarity doesn’t mean every community is liable for whatever the others owe, and it doesn’t mean anyone has an unlimited claim on another community’s labor and stores, or its land and tools.

[A207] p From One Community to a Movement / Federated Communities Make a Movement
Whenever a community or a federation creates or applies a rule, it’s best practice to store the full case history (with private details protected) so it can be re-evaluated in future: what happened, the principles that actually conflicted, your first judgments, and your final decision and rationale. Any dissenting opinions should go in there as well, along with the consequences in the case. When the rule changes because of a new case, run the old case through again to see how it would turn out. That saves each generation from having to rediscover the same constitutional fight from scratch.

[A208] h1 Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community /
Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community

[A209] p Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community /
Don’t worry about getting land yet. If you’ve got a small handful of people who want to make a community but aren’t sure yet, you are already a community, even if it’s still an untested one. It may sound cheesy, but it’s true all the same.

[A210] p Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community /
Also, don’t even think about combining ownership (or any other big resource, for that matter) until you’ve been through at least one real conflict together and really dealt with it. Not by writing out a vision statement and shaking hands, or any other fake way of “resolving” things, like a truce nobody believes (”let’s just leave each other alone”). Dealing with it for real includes the relational pl/ork of each of you naming what really went on, giving each of you time, figuring out what came up for each of you (jealousy, humiliation, fear, old material, whatever), and dealing with it until the emotional pressure changes enough that whatever agreement you come up with means anything. Until then, what’s it worth?

[A211] p Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community /
Until that’s happened, you’re all still promising strangers, and you don’t want to take out a mortgage together yet. That’s okay! You can all meet in a rented or borrowed space, or at each other’s houses, long before anyone needs a land surveyor.

[A212] p Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community /
You will also want a budget of what you have in the way of resources. This is the boring stuff. But you will need to know what the group can offer in the way of water, housing, food stores, health care, tools, seasonal labor, transportation, access to markets, schooling, etc. Do you have outside debts to pay? How long will your cash reserves last, if the community uses them? What critical skills will you need? Being great at the emotional stuff won’t stop you from being dead in the water if the one person who knows where everything is and how it works gets too exhausted to keep going.

Sexuality and gender belong on there too. Would LGBTQ+ people be able to live openly in your community, date, be in relationships, and stay fully protected by the community? [A218] h2 Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community / Resources, Land, and Exit Terms
Resources, Land, and Exit Terms

[A219] p Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community / Resources, Land, and Exit Terms
Who will have title to the land? Very important! Whoever has it will have enormous power over the rest of the community. If the deed is in the name of the founder, then he or she is really a landlord with a philosophy. If three out of twelve members own everything, then whether or not your literature says everyone is equal, there are two classes of members.

[A220] p Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community / Resources, Land, and Exit Terms
What kind of legal entity (cooperative, trust, association, company, whatever fits your jurisdiction) will hold the land? This needs to be decided before it’s bought. Then you’ll need to decide who has what voting rights, who has what access and use rights to common resources, how you will account for people’s contributions (if at all), how inheritance works, outside debts… oh my!

[A221] p Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community / Resources, Land, and Exit Terms
And how will you handle the visibility of what the community has? A money-free community still has scarce things and bottlenecks, sorry. It will be important for people to know what’s owned or held in common, what you’ve set aside, what obligations you have to outsiders, who has authority to hand out the major resources, where conflicts of interest are, etc. It won’t do to have the transfer of major resources (land, equipment, stores, outside cash, etc.) dependent on one person’s memory or permission.

[A222] p Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community / Resources, Land, and Exit Terms
Good people leave for good reasons -- dying parent, love, work, health, exhaustion, change of mind, etc. You don’t want them to lose everything they’ve put in. That’s coercive -- they HAVE to stay if they can’t afford to go, and a community of prisoners isn’t ideal. Whether or not you use money, you’ll need to have a way of providing people with resources when they leave (cash, if you use it, but also tools, transportation, temporary housing, stored up goods, the right to keep using something, or whatever). Otherwise their “right” to leave is only on paper -- it only works when the community happens to have spare money. Write up the exit terms while leaving is still only theoretical -- like a pre-nup when you’re planning the wedding. Not romantic, but protective. Much easier than doing it once anybody’s furious.

In every community there was somebody (or some founding group) who took the initiative to get it started. They found the land, wrote the first documents, taught the method, recruited the first members, or kept things going when everybody else lost interest.

[A226] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
The founder(s) ends up with real influence, and pretending they don’t just makes that influence harder to look at. When that power fails to get distributed as the conditions ripen for that, it’s called founderism.

[A227] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
I’ve seen founderism in many communes that I’ve visited, and I can see it playing out if I were to be a founder as well. I’ve got all these proposals and visions here in my guides, and so it would be very easy for me to become the person who decides whether or not everyone else “understood” them correctly, even while I wish to remain humble and open to the input of all involved.

[A228] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
A few things help keep that in check. One is to judge the method by results people can actually see. Will they see the members safely flourishing without being trapped? Able to leave without stigma? Those questions seem more important to me than how big or old the commune gets, or how many decisions are made by consensus.

[A229] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
Allow for parallel implementations. Fork the method. If the community finds a way to alter Hearthwork and get better results, they’ve taught me something.

[A232] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
Can the community replace the role of the founder(s)?

[A233] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
Can the community alter the vision of the founder(s)?

[A234] p Founderism: The Four Questions /
Can the community exist without the founder(s)?

Then follow the practical power. Whose name is on the deed? Who’s in charge of the resources? Who keeps the records? Who decides who gets admitted, and who picks the people who review the founder? [A238] h2 Founderism: The Four Questions / Founderism Creeps Up
Founderism Creeps Up

[A239] p Founderism: The Four Questions / Founderism Creeps Up
While founderism is a problem in every community, it’s especially so in communities which form naturally over time as I’m describing may happen in my case. In such cases, there’s no formal community and no formal rules until people decide to come together to make them. It doesn’t make sense to do that at first, if, say, I’m the only one there and I don’t know who else will be there that I could trust. If there’s one other person who comes to visit, it also doesn’t make sense, so there’s a critical mass point at which people decide “we were just living together like friends, but now we want to formalize the community.”

[A240] p Founderism: The Four Questions / Founderism Creeps Up
If I could find a suitable commune to join, that would basically reverse this problem. I would then be the one on probation. And if we are all starting together from scratch, then we would all be at an equal probationary level with each other.

[A241] p Founderism: The Four Questions / Founderism Creeps Up
It’s a bit like the process of getting married. At first, you visit your boo when schedules align and each of you agrees. Over time as you get closer to marriage (which hopefully is not a totally unconscious process), you start making decisions together and considering sharing more and more resources. Once married, if you decide to divorce, you split up the resources, although that’s also contentious depending on how long the marriage was for, whether one person feels tricked, etc, and divorce unfortunately often ends up in court. Leaving a commune is the divorce half of it, where the shared resources have to get split up. When joining or forming a commune, we can hopefully be more conscious about the process and less romantic.

[A273] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
First, correspond with the visitor. Did they read the materials? (Not everyone does.) Do they get the basic idea of why this community is here? Are they at least starting to do the relevant practices? It’s a lot cheaper to find out through a thoughtful exchange than after six months of misunderstanding, right?

[A274] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
The short visit: If they come for a week or two and just participate in normal life, well, most people can be charming for two weeks. Not exactly a high bar. 😅 So that’s about all you’ll find out.

[A275] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
The long visit: If you’re not around long enough to actually experience inconvenience and conflict with the person in question, then you’re not going to learn very much about them. Watch how they react when working and making decisions in the group. When tired, corrected, attracted, disappointed, bored, etc. (Hopefully not all at once!) Even a professional faker may be able to pull off an act for months.

[A276] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
Taking on responsibilities: If someone will have a role in your group that has serious consequences, involving kids, shared resources, medicine, vehicles or private records, you want to know how they handle that specific thing. I wouldn’t hand someone the van keys just because they’re great with kids. You can find out by checking whether you’ve seen them do this thing before, and asking for references if appropriate. Give people access to these things in stages and ensure there’s always some way to appeal decisions about these roles to a separate authority if needed.

[A277] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
Write down your answers: How long is a provisional membership, and what are a provisional member’s rights and responsibilities? How is it reviewed, and how does someone leave it? Who decides on full membership? (Hint: not the person who recruited them! 😜) And what rights does a full member have to the land, the shared resources and a voice in the group, and what do they owe over the long term? (Those go in your founding agreements.)

[A278] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / From Visitor to Member—Slowly
Then there are the people who aren’t fully “in” the commune politically. Visitors, volunteers, workers, renters and interns at the commune (yes, even interns) are entitled to some rights when decisions really affect their pay or access to common resources. All of them should have clear terms, and the chance to be heard and to have such decisions reviewed. The same is true for decisions really affecting their schedule, safety, housing, records or discipline. Equality that stops at the membership line can end up recreating a servant class that moves from commune to commune.

[A279] h2 The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / When Somebody Has to Go
When Somebody Has to Go

[A280] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / When Somebody Has to Go
A safe community can’t promise to keep everyone. There may be times when someone has to go. If someone is a serious or generalized danger to others, or keeps violating essential boundaries, or for whatever reason just makes everyday life together impossible for everyone else, it can be the right call to eject them. If restorative work can help in a reasonable amount of time, all the better. But it’s not the community’s job to be a treatment program for someone who’s endangering everyone else.

[A281] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / When Somebody Has to Go
This power to remove people is so easily abused, though. So work out the process before it’s about anyone in particular. What counts as cause for immediate protective action? Who decides to put temporary restrictions on someone? Who will make sure all the evidence is preserved? How will the person be able to reply to the concerns? What protections will there be for the person’s interests (housing, records, family contact, access to common resources, outside funds if any) during the separation? When is it time to bring in outside help?

[A282] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / When Somebody Has to Go
Note that “outside” doesn’t have to mean the nation-state. My research initially leaned too far that way. The basic need here is for a competent level of authority that is not reviewing its own conduct. That could mean another community, a federation of communities, professionals, autonomous regional institutions, courts, regulatory agencies, emergency services, etc. The key is that it’s not the same people.

[A283] p The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For / When Somebody Has to Go
Also decide on a mediator or ombudsperson in advance. Why bother? People often won’t trust someone they haven’t already met, who comes in during a crisis as a “neutral stranger.” A mediator doesn’t obviate the need for evidence, safeguarding, rulings, etc. Mediation is an attempt to restore good relations between people. When people can’t or won’t consent to being in community any more, they still need a final separation.

[A284] h1 Dissolution and Exit /
Dissolution and Exit

[A285] p Dissolution and Exit /
Dissolution is something founders rarely want to talk about while they’re picking a name for their community and looking forward to its first harvest. Who would? But that’s exactly when it needs to be talked about.

[A286] p Dissolution and Exit /
If the community dissolves, what happens to the land and any outside debts? What about its buildings, equipment, common stores or funds? What happens to each member’s agreed rights? More immediately, who is responsible for ensuring that everyone has a place to live and food to eat? Who takes responsibility for making sure people get their medications and the other care they need? Who keeps childcare going, and makes sure the kids’ schooling carries on? Who takes responsibility for transportation, for the records, for family contact, for the animals, for making sure land obligations are met, and for any unresolved claims? That’s a lot of loose ends!

[A287] p Dissolution and Exit /
Think of it like a living will, and assign a bridge or successor for each critical function in advance, so basic life doesn’t depend on reviving the commune. The institution may not be salvageable, but who’s making breakfast tomorrow?

[A288] p Dissolution and Exit /
Dissolution terms are also a way for a group to end without turning every friendship into evidence. Who wants that? People may leave hurt, but they keep the transition support they agreed on, and their dignity. And they can tell a coherent story of what happened.

[A289] p Dissolution and Exit /
Institutions and relationships don’t die at the same time, and some of what people learned to give one another may go on too. What a relief! The film American Commune follows two sisters who grew up on The Farm. When one of them later became ill, former members who hadn’t seen her in years reached out with support. The Farm’s communal economy had changed dramatically, but the bonds hadn’t vanished.

[A290] p Dissolution and Exit /
My father carried the Bruderhof with him long after he left it. It shaped the foundation he started and the film he made. It shaped the places he took me, too, and the questions I’m still asking (as you may have noticed!).

[A291] h1 What This Looks Like From Sénégal /
What This Looks Like From Sénégal

[A292] figcaption What This Looks Like From Sénégal /
The “African Renaissance Monument” in Dakar, Sénégal, which stands at 160 feet tall and took 4 years to build.

[A293] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal /
I live in Sénégal, where “intentional community” often sounds like a strange Western phrase. People here are like, “Don’t human beings already do that?”

[A294] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal /
Westerners often start out isolated and try to rebuild a tribe. A lot of Africans start out with a communal life they inherited, and watch parts of it fall apart. Kind of the opposite problem. And urban Africans who get interested in intentional community might come to it feeling alienated from both the city and the tradition they left.

[A295] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal /
So this is a global issue, far from some Western lifestyle niche. There’s still knowledge in the living villages that no book can give you. But if you visit, remember they’re people, not museums. Would you like people coming to your house to stare at you and your family? So be respectful, contribute something, learn the language, and don’t expect anyone to hand you a spiritual service package.

[A296] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal /
Oh, and a practical note: getting my residency in Sénégal was pretty cheap and straightforward. French and Wolof matter a lot more than English. And the country’s famous ideal of Teranga (hospitality) is real in my life. I’m staying in a friend’s family’s house, and they won’t let me pay rent! Of course that generosity lives right alongside watchfulness, witchcraft, social conservatism, bureaucracy and all the usual complications of a real country.

[A297] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal /
Sénégal is unlike anywhere else I’ve lived. It’s the only Sufi Muslim country (aside from Gambia, a tiny nation inside Sénégal). Sufi muslims are the ones who value a deep, personal connection with God, rather than just following the religious rules. Five times a day the prayers pour out into the streets. I find them so soothing. And Sufism doesn’t stay inside designated “spiritual” places here. It’s in the music, in the pictures everywhere, in the murals, in the names, in the conversations. Prostitution is also legal here.

[A298] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal /
And the humor! People are outside laughing for hours at a time. This is the most joking culture I’ve ever been in, and I keep wondering if I’ll ever understand the really complicated jokes in Wolof. Basic conversation is one thing. But knowing why twelve people are suddenly crying with laughter? That’s graduate school.

[A299] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal /
In the villages and families I’ve spent time around, so much is taken care of by the extended family and the local memory, alongside the formal systems. Whether it’s land, childcare, housing, food, obligations, etc, etc. Who needs a workshop called “Rebuilding Intergenerational Belonging” when some aunt has already given three people a task?

[A300] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal /
Even in Dakar, the overpopulated capital, I found this graffiti on a wall: “Tolerance! Openness! Union! Happiness! Love!” And elsewhere there are murals of Sufi leaders. Spiritual graffiti still surprises me. Where I grew up, a city wall was either selling you a product or showing off somebody’s inability to draw a penis. But the “tolerance” part is funny…

[A301] figcaption What This Looks Like From Sénégal /
Graffiti in Dakar, Sénégal: Tolerance! Openness! Union! Happiness! Love!

[A302] h2 What This Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
All is not well in paradise

[A303] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
Does this mean I think Sénégal is some magical place that’s kept its communal paradise? No, not quite. The warmth has hard limits here. One noteworthy example is the plight of LGBTQ+ people here, who cross them automatically.

[A304] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
Sénégal passed a law in March 2026 that increased the penalty for “consensual same-sex intimacy” to 5 to 10 years in prison, and it also made public advocacy for homosexuality a crime. Homosexuals were already facing arrests, family rejection, harassment, assault and mob violence, so ironically, some say they’re actually safer once they’re arrested, because then at least the mob can’t get at them. And also ironically, LGBTQ+ people are using this prejudice in their favor to get refugee status and live in rich countries.

[A305] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
So yes, it’s possible for a society to be spiritually alive, generous, funny and super communal, while still becoming cruel to those who break its social categories. Being in community isn’t automatically liberating. It all depends on who is allowed to belong without pretending.

[A306] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
One other shadow side of the otherwise forgiving social culture here is that people may go to great lengths to avoid confrontation. Who stole the sheep? We all know, but nobody wants to cause a stink by bringing it up. 🙉

[A307] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
Where I live, neighbors steal our moringa pods every morning. Every morning! They could plant a moringa tree in their own yard. Apparently maintaining this relationship with ours is more entertaining. 😂

[A308] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
Everybody here has sheep (even me), and they are super cute. It can feel like you’re living in another world, with the music, the animals, the joking, the praying, and just being around people all the time.

[A309] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / All is not well in paradise
Of course, the old communal ways here are changing. Young people move to the city, get a job, get a phone, eat imported food, become part of the wider world, etc. (some of the men even make the dangerous journey by sea to Europe). So what about the families? The ones that used to share almost everything are now more reliant on cash and individual opportunities.

[A310] h2 What This Looks Like From Sénégal / Lessons from the Black Tax
Lessons from the Black Tax

[A311] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / Lessons from the Black Tax
Then there’s the so-called “Black Tax,” a strange legacy of the old communal system: if you’ve got any money at all, you’re expected to share it with your parents, your siblings, your cousins, to pay school fees, medical bills, funerals, etc. I understand the instinct. In the ancestral way of doing things nobody made it alone, and the people who fed and raised you had a real claim on whatever prosperity came through you later. So why not share in return? That’s how the whole organism survived (it wasn’t charity).

[A312] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / Lessons from the Black Tax
But these days it can be soul destroying. People have a lot less of the communal life that held the obligation up (the shared land, work, childcare, food and daily belonging), but they still carry it. So if one of them makes it into the cash economy, it’s really hard to build any independence or long-term stability, because they may be expected to carry everybody. How can they get ahead, when all these people are dependent on them? And the relatives stay dependent too, since the payments don’t give them a real alternative. It’s the ancestral ethic playing out inside an individualistic cash economy. It can keep people alive, but it eats away at both sides too.

[A313] h2 What This Looks Like From Sénégal / If You Can’t Move
If You Can’t Move

[A314] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / If You Can’t Move
I realize that for most people reading this there are a lot of reasons why they can’t just head off and go build community on some land. Jobs, kids, pets, debt, a mortgage, a sick parent, or several of these arranged in a circle... That’s fine! The most important pl/ork doesn’t need land anyway.

[A315] figcaption What This Looks Like From Sénégal / If You Can’t Move
Too bad you can’t move…

[A316] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / If You Can’t Move
People sometimes buy forty acres and then bring all their unprocessed stuff with them to a more scenic location. It’s a more beautiful view for sure, but now you have the conflict PLUS goats.

[A317] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / If You Can’t Move
So your inner pl/ork can start now, right where you live. Peer counseling takes two people and a private place to sit and talk. And reparenting, somatic work, jealousy work, conflict repair, etc etc? None of it has to wait until somebody has a deed in hand.

[A318] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / If You Can’t Move
A group that practices together weekly for two years may never own land. So what? It can still make a difference when people are going through a divorce, an illness or grief. Parenting too, and plain old loneliness.

[A319] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / If You Can’t Move
Here’s an idea if you’re remote, or stuck where you are: build the part of community you actually can. Have a weekly meal with two households. Set up a counseling exchange with one friend, in person or by video. Share tools or childcare. Transport or food prep work too. Or a sauna. And if you’re hippie enough, add acro-yoga, dance improv, a mothers’ circle or a cuddle puddle.

[A320] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / If You Can’t Move
Trust me on this one. If three households keep showing up for each other like this for six months, you’ll learn more than you would from another year of daydreaming about the ideal village.

[A321] h2 What This Looks Like From Sénégal / What to Do This Month
What to Do This Month

[A322] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / What to Do This Month
So what do you actually do this month? Start doing some inner pl/ork, with the free reparenting guide and Hearthwork manual or with another serious practice you already trust. And practice before you have an audience.

[A323] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / What to Do This Month
Find two or three other people and meet regularly. Call it a practice group, not a commune. Practice listening and switching roles. Get through at least one disagreement too, and keep your commitments. You can talk about a shared future later.

[A324] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / What to Do This Month
Where do you find these people? Try an unprogrammed Quaker meeting, a restorative-practice group, an open-hearted congregation, a mature recovery group, a meditation group, or find some people who have gone on long retreats and come back kinder, rather than more impressed with themselves.

[A325] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / What to Do This Month
Go visit some real communities. Really, go! Twin Oaks is one. East Wind is another, and so is Acorn, and there are plenty of other intentional communities. Lots of ecovillages are open to visitors too. Check their current visitor requirements first.

[A326] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / What to Do This Month
By visiting some communities, you’ll get a better idea of how much togetherness you enjoy and what kind of privacy you need, and what kind of labor system irritates you. And whether communal showers feel liberating, or make you start looking at studio apartments! No manifesto is going to tell you any of that!

[A327] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / What to Do This Month
Look before you leap. It will really save you some time & money.

[A328] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / What to Do This Month
I’m not trying to sell you a retreat or get you to fill out an application or anything. The manuals I mentioned are free. I’ve just noticed that wherever people are taking real care of each other and playing, feeling useful and having room to become more honest, community tends to gather.

[A329] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / What to Do This Month
Decades after the Bruderhof shaped my father, I’m publishing the film he made, and writing this.

[A330] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / What to Do This Month
Maybe some of you will build something useful from this. Maybe that community will teach someone else, who’ll build another. Passing it on doesn’t need one institution or one founder. And there doesn’t have to be one official version of it, either.

[A331] p What This Looks Like From Sénégal / What to Do This Month
If you build something real, maybe we’ll hear about each other. And if you teach other people to build, hearing about it matters less.
