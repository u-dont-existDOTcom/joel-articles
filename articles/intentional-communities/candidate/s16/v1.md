# Practice Comes Before Property

One of the main problems I see with how people go about building community is that they do the steps in the wrong order. They get the land first, put the vision in a document, let ownership pile up with whoever had the money, and put off the emotional preparation until everyone has more time.

The better order is less cinematic, more like this: practice together, process a real conflict, clarify your values, test membership, decide ownership and exit, and only then think about land.

Likewise, the economic side has to go in stages. You're probably not going to be able to fly straight to the economic destination! Start with a cluster of households where nobody has to buy the basics of life inside the cluster anymore, and anyone making money on the outside contributes a set share. Only after you've proven that you can reliably provide for everybody, and fairly decide who gets what from the common, should you move to a common purse. Cutting back on outside money, or getting rid of it, comes from what you've shown you can do. ("Money is impure!" is a weird thing to say while somebody else is quietly paying the tax bill.)

## If You Can’t Move

I realize that for most people reading this there are a lot of reasons why they can't just head off and go build community on some land. Jobs, kids, pets, debt, a mortgage, a sick parent, or several of these arranged in a circle... That's fine! The most important pl/ork doesn't need land anyway.

People sometimes buy forty acres and then bring all their unprocessed baggage with them to a more scenic location. Sure, the view's better! But now the conflict has goats.

But there's no reason you can't start the inner pl/ork where you live. [Peer counseling](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/hearthwork-an-integrated-peer-counseling) only needs two people and enough privacy to talk. And reparenting, somatic work, jealousy, conflict repair, etc. don't have to wait for a deed!

A group that practices together weekly for two years already has a real piece of community, even if it never owns land, and it can make a difference in how people get through divorce, illness, grief, parenting and plain old loneliness.

Remote or constrained readers can build the portion available. Hold a weekly meal with two households. Arrange a counseling exchange with one friend in person or by video. Share tools, childcare, transport, food preparation, or a sauna. Add acro-yoga, dance improvisation, a mothers’ circle, or a [cuddle puddle](http://cuddleparty.com/) if you’re hippie enough.

If three households can commit to showing up for each other like this for six months, you'll learn more than you would from another year of daydreaming about the ideal village.

## What to Do This Month

Here's your first month, no heroic land purchase required. Start working on the inner pl/ork, whether it's following the free [reparenting guide](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/inner-child-self-love-tips-and-guided) and [Hearthwork manual](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/hearthwork-an-integrated-peer-counseling) or another serious practice you already trust. And practice before you have an audience!

Find two or three people to form a practice group with (call it that, not a commune). Meet regularly, and learn to listen, switch roles, handle one disagreement and keep your commitments before you start talking about a shared future.

Where do you find them? You can try unprogrammed Quaker meetings, restorative practice groups, open-hearted congregations, mature recovery groups, meditation groups, or people who came back from a long retreat kinder rather than more impressed with themselves.

Then go visit actual communities, like [Twin Oaks](https://twinoaks.org/), [East Wind](http://eastwind.org/), [Acorn](https://www.ic.org/directory/acorn-community-farm/) or other [intentional communities](https://www.ic.org/) (a lot of [ecovillages](https://ecovillage.org/) take visitors too). Just check their current visitor requirements first.

You'll learn things no manifesto can teach you: how much togetherness you actually enjoy, what kind of privacy you need, which labor system drives you nuts, and whether communal showers feel liberating or send you off pricing studio apartments.

So do those three things first, before you spend serious time or money looking for land.

I'm not trying to sell you a retreat or get you to fill out an application or anything, the manuals are free. I'm in Sénégal and I expect to buy land, but it's not like I'm announcing a commune. I've just noticed that community tends to gather wherever people find real care, play, usefulness and room to become more honest.
