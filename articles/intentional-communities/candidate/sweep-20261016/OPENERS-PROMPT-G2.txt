# Jealousy Can Capture Monogamy or Free Love

[P1] Any community that lasts will eventually be reorganized by attraction and pair-bonding. When two exes live in the same city, they can move to different sides of the city. When they're part of a thirty-person commune, they may still be in the same kitchen the next morning, working in the same team, attending the same meetings, sharing childcare and the same friends. A couple's jealousy or breakup becomes a problem for the entire community, because it will change the loyalties, the sleeping arrangements, the labor, the kids, the alliances, and sometimes who's leaving.

[P2] The discussion generally goes straight to "should people be monogamous or free-love?" but as I discuss in my Romance Guide, this is one level too late in the conversation. The community problem is that, in close quarters, jealousy gets magnified, and jealousy can capture either system.

[P3] Rigid monogamy can turn fear and possession into a moral law. If people are doing free love, but aren't doing the inner pl/ork, they can just end up with a larger spreadsheet to distribute their fear over. ⟦author⟧It's not like calling something by a certain name dissolves the childhood panic, the comparison, the terror of abandonment, or the desire to control another person.⟦/author⟧

[P4] I don't think it should be up to the community to decide for everyone what form relationships will take. People can make different agreements with each other, in pairs, threes, fours, whatever. They can change those agreements as they wish. And they can ask for support when those agreements bring up old material.

[P5] Whatever forms people choose, I do think that housing, security for kids, membership and access to common necessities should not be contingent on a person staying in some agreement with the people they're romantically involved with.

[P6] ZEGG, a community near Berlin, and Tamera, another in Portugal, have spent decades experimenting with relationships inside community. Their answers are not a set of commandments that everyone must follow. But they are an example of how a community can acknowledge the erotic aspects of life. It is not necessary for 20 adults to pretend that they've become neutral colleagues just because the by-laws say so.

[P7] The non-negotiable for me is that jealousy not be allowed to become the hidden governor. This doesn't mean it can't come up. It needs to be able to be talked about and felt in the body. It needs to be explored without humiliation. And it needs to be let go of gradually, and not allowed to turn into punishment, or into community rules.

[P8] There are some ways the group can help, and basic boundaries the group can enforce, without deciding what type of relationships people have. Even so, there will still be disagreements about this. That's more realistic than saying it's all been resolved.

# The Grown Children Get the Final Review

[P1] Couples who live together, share the same culture and even the same bed still disagree about screens, vaccinations, schooling, religion, food, discipline and medical care. And that's just two people. Now think of twenty adults, all with different childhoods.

[P2] The community has to share enough principles about raising kids to offer them coherence, and leave parents enough room to remain parents.

[P3] My own childhood broke many mainstream rules about what children should see and do. I won’t unpack all of it here. I’m grateful that my mother nursed me until I was five, grateful my parents didn’t vaccinate me, and grateful for nearly all of the unusual world they gave me.

[P5] I mention vaccination specifically because it's one of the values I would absolutely need to share with people in a community, if I was going to raise children there. I'm not trying to design a village for everyone, only saying that communities need some common ground among the parents on children's health, enough that they can actually trust one another.

## Communal Parenting Adds On

[P11] One of the biggest reasons I think community matters for kids is Jean Liedloff's The Continuum Concept. It's one outsider's interpretation of the Ye'kuana, an Indigenous people of the Venezuelan rainforest she lived with, and she may have idealized what she saw, but it's still a powerful picture: the babies are carried along in ordinary adult life, the kids are surrounded by people of several ages and learn by taking part, and it doesn't fall on one exhausted parent to be a child's whole social world. While a nuclear household can imitate some of this, a real village can make it normal.

[P12] Experiments where kids sleep and live apart from their parents deserve special caution. Research on Israeli kibbutzim found that the practice of kids sleeping communally eventually ended for a mix of reasons: developmental findings, parents' preferences and social change. Kids can benefit from high-quality group care and multiple loving adults in their lives, but replacing a secure attachment to their parents is a different matter.

[P13] Tamera's Children's Place has repeated some of these old Kibbutz failings, taking children away from biological parents, because their founders' ideology said that children are traumatized by the nuclear family and its unstable romantic underpinnings. ⟦author⟧On the other hand, their actual practice did allow parents (including fathers) to stay with their children in Children's Place, so they weren't as dogmatic as I originally thought.⟦/author⟧ My own bias is still toward a system where kids are not solely taken care of by their parents, while still honoring the parent-child bond. ⟦author⟧If the father is unknown, the whole community can do the fathering, but the biological mom's role should always be honored, since that attachment is formed already from years of nursing.⟦/author⟧

## Children Need Freedom Inside Real Protection

[P14] [caption] African kids enjoying their childhood!

[P15] Kids also need their own territory, places where they can create their own society, invent games and work out ordinary disputes, without always being under the management of adults.

[P16] I got a taste of that at a Unitarian youth camp, and it's still one of the freest memories I have. ZEGG's Kinderhaus works from a similar intuition. Adults take care of safety on the outside, but kids still get a world of their own.

[P17] This could be badly misunderstood, so the safeguards need to be explicit. The adults still have to pay attention to what goes on in a children's territory. ⟦author⟧Older kids can't be allowed to quietly boss the younger ones around there.⟦/author⟧ And keeping secrets can't be treated as independence.

[P18] I also reject the suburban panic that treats every warm relationship between a child and an unrelated adult as suspicious. It's good for kids to have a whole bunch of grownups who know them and enjoy them, who would notice when something changes, and who would act when something's wrong. When adults panic like that, kids can lose all those grownups without the panic actually preventing abuse.

[P19] There are several parts to this protection:

[P20] Give children real agency. When kids say no, it counts. No grownup has a right to their affection, physical contact, obedience outside of legitimate safety needs, or private access to them.

[P21] Create a no-secrets rule. No grownup has a right to ask a kid to keep a secret from their mom or another trusted adult, whether it's about a gift, a game, a touch, a conversation or a relationship. A surprise has an end date. But secrets meant to isolate a kid aren't allowed.

[P22] ⟦author⟧Protecting children is the duty of adults, but it requires children to help also.⟦/author⟧ "The Boy Who Cried Wolf" is a didactic children's story which continues to be useful generation after generation. ⟦author⟧The Buddha even taught his son honesty as the most important first lesson.⟦/author⟧ I agree. We must teach children truthfulness before there's a crisis. "Make believe" must be labeled as such. When adults lie to children (like Santa is real etc), that teaches them to misunderstand the difference between reality and make-believe. Make sure kids understand what they say will be taken seriously if they are known to be honest. Don't let the teaching turn into interrogation or punishment when they're not sure. ⟦author⟧A scared or confused kid may get mixed up telling what happened and still need protection.⟦/author⟧

[P23] Hear the child even when the accused is someone people love. Communities fail here, because the adult may be charismatic, useful, rich, spiritually important, or everybody's friend. Decide on the response process ahead of time, before anyone's face is attached to it. Keep everyone at risk safe first, right away, then make sure the accusation is investigated competently and fairly. Nobody's status buys silence.

[P24] Make sure kids have relationships and a reporting route outside the community. Grandparents, cousins, local friends, teachers and other trusted adults give them perspective on their life, and someone to talk to. Make sure there's at least one trusted adult, advocate or reporting route they can reach without going through the community's leadership. In 1984, Vermont authorities raided a religious community in Island Pond and took 112 kids into custody over suspected abuse. A judge turned down the state's blanket request to hold them, because it couldn't name any specific case, and the kids went home that same day. State intervention can fail spectacularly, and enclosing kids completely in a community can fail too. Kids need more than one world.

[P25] Sometimes we also need to protect people from the rare, but real threat posed by dangerous children. ⟦author⟧I looked through the research corpus for a well-documented case of a child in an intentional community who remained seriously dangerous to others.⟦/author⟧ I was looking to find the reported conduct, the assessment, the intervention, the review, and what happened later. I couldn't find one. It's possible that records are private, or that cases were referred elsewhere. Whatever the reason, I can't claim communities already know how to handle the hardest cases on their own. ⟦author⟧In those cases, the child, the family, and the people at risk all need access to competent help and review, hopefully including non-intrusive help from outside the home community.⟦/author⟧

## The Door Must Open Outward at Adulthood

[P26] If a community's kids never leave, it has no way of knowing whether they'd come back freely. So let them study, travel and make friends, encounter other ways of living, and disagree with the founders without being treated as traitors. And kids coming back isn't necessarily the best thing for the communal movement either. ⟦author⟧If they then start their own communities, we may see more evolution than if they simply return and continue what their parents started.⟦/author⟧

[P27] Amish young people, age 16, go through a 2-year period of freedom from parental control called Rumspringa. During this time they might travel outside, although they normally choose to stay in the community and begin acting as adults, including courtship. At the end of this period, they decide as adults whether to join the church, and roughly 85 percent or more of them end up joining. ⟦author⟧Pop culture makes Rumspringa seem like a wilder, more universal teenage free-for-all than it is, but the main point is that they're baptized as adults, which means belonging is a commitment they make.⟦/author⟧

[P28] Every childhood narrows the future. Most ordinary parents aren't going to give their kid a bunch of money at 18 and say, "Go see the world. They'll try to get them ready for some next step that can actually work, so they can become self-sufficient, and eventually take care of their aging parents. A poor or money-free community may have even less cash to offer, and basic survival may rightly come first.

[P29] Unlimited freedom isn't realistic, but it doesn't need to be artificially impossible for grown kids to leave. Kids in or outside of intentional communities all need to learn enough about how the money world outside operates that they aren't helpless in it. Like how to get a job, pay rent, read contracts, handle banks, get IDs, avoid getting scammed, and how to ask for help when they're unsure about any of that. And all that sounds amorphous if it's not structured into plausible next steps outside the community (school, an apprenticeship, work, family, another community).

[P30] Kids also need transferable skills and education. It helps if they have relationships with people outside the community. They should be able to access their own records and documents. It would be great if the community could also provide resources to smooth the transition, like temporary housing, help with travel, etc.

[P31] ⟦author⟧Belonging and freedom should go hand in hand.⟦/author⟧

# The Best Model I’ve Seen: Zapatistas

[P1] [caption] A Zapatista Schoolhouse. It reads: Worthy Rage (Digna Rabia)

[P2] In 2005, a few college buddies and I went down to Oventik, one of the Zapatista caracoles ("snails"), the regional centers that tie their villages together in Chiapas. We almost died several times getting there. The mountain road ran along cliffs, and our rental car was on its last legs, which is a great combination when you're on your way to visit a revolution.

[P3] San Cristóbal de las Casas is where the only white man we met sat us down in a café and grilled us so directly that he seemed like a CIA agent. Why were we there? Who were we working with? What were our plans? When were we leaving?

[P4] It made us feel important for a minute. We were just college kids in a dying rental car, but apparently the empire had questions for us. 😂

[P5] Oventik itself was like something out of a dream. We played basketball outside with the Zapatista youth, and people gave us talks about their history and philosophy, which I translated for my friends because my Spanish was better. Visitors were coming from all around the world to learn, and to make the communities less easy to attack in silence.

[P6] I felt a deep spirituality from the Zapatistas. I also suspect there may be a private relationship with ayahuasca, but nobody told me that, and it's only my impression, not something to take as fact.

[P7] What impressed me the most was how they do authority. It's mandar obedeciendo, to lead by obeying: the representatives carry the decisions people made in the assemblies, and they have to answer to the people who gave them the mandate.

[P8] ⟦author⟧The Zapatistas are also the closest thing to a whole example that I found, because their autonomy goes beyond how they vote.⟦/author⟧ They've set up their own education and health care, and their own justice, and they run their own collective production and political formation. On top of that they've worked out ways of teaching outsiders, all while coordinating beyond a single village.

[P9] The Fire and the Word describes the movement growing from family to village, village to region, and region to larger structures. It also gives one of the most useful Zapatista principles for community design: build the practice first and develop the theory afterward. ⟦author⟧The autonomous municipalities came first; an association of them became a rehearsal for the Good Government Juntas (Boards).⟦/author⟧ When different municipalities encountered different problems and uneven progress, that experience generated another structure rather than being forced to fit the original plan.

[P10] ⟦author⟧After a long internal review, in 2023, the Zapatistas announced they were dissolving their previous federated governmental structure, which was split into Rebel Zapatista Autonomous Municipalities and the Good Government Juntas. The original idea was to have power vested in the local communities, but it had crept up and consolidated itself at higher levels over time for practical reasons.⟦/author⟧ Their new arrangement moved authority downward toward a Local Autonomous Government in each community.

[P11] ⟦author⟧Institutions get more and more layers and offices as they age. We see this same pattern happen repeatedly, everywhere in history, except usually it goes one way without reversal, unless there's a revolution.⟦/author⟧

[P12] ⟦author⟧But there is a special reason the Zapatistas were successfully able to reverse this degeneration.⟦/author⟧ Zapatista autonomy grew out of Indigenous communities with long histories. They share language and family ties. They've fought land struggles together, and defended themselves together for decades. A new ecovillage can't copy the diagram everyone can see and get the trust underneath it right away.

[P13] ⟦author⟧And that's not me saying that. Despite actually trying to train people in their model, the Zapatistas themselves have been clear that people shouldn't expect to directly or easily import/export their philosophy (Zapatismo) as something ready-made, shake & bake.⟦/author⟧ The useful thing is how they learn: they build things and ask questions, and when they find out where the practice isn't working, they change the structure.

[P14] ⟦author⟧Now, the Zapatistas also don't prove my whole money-free economy goal. They still use money internally in some cases, not others, and they still use it externally for trade. They've also benefitted from outside economic help, while at the same time making efforts to remain politically independent from donors and more self-sustaining.⟦/author⟧

[P15] [caption] Escuelita Zapatista — The Zapatista school to teach outsiders their way of life

[P16] For the 10th anniversary of their autonomous governments, the Zapatistas opened up the Escuelita. Outsiders lived with families there and learned autonomy by taking part in day to day life. Around 1,500 students came to the first sessions, and thousands more came to later ones.

[P17] One way an escuelita could fail is if it becomes a business, where residents spend their lives performing community for paying outsiders, and success gets measured by how many people come instead of how many daughter communities there are. A week of feeling transformed isn't what it's for. It's for teaching enough of the social and material system that some people can build somewhere else.

[P18] As part of my research I have also looked for evidence of what happened after the Escuelita. While I found information on alumni networks, people bringing practices back with them, and organizing later, I couldn't find a clear chain of somebody having been to the Escuelita, then a new communal institution being set up, and then evidence about how its members and their children actually did over time. Zapatista influence was already spreading abroad before the Escuelita, so if some institution resembles a Zapatista one later, that alone doesn't prove the Escuelita was responsible. I'm borrowing the Zapatistas' teaching method and their ambition to replicate.

[P19] Sociocracy has a very different history, yet some similar features have emerged there as well: circles, decision-making by consent, distributed authority, and linking between the levels. Does this prove there's a universal formula? No. But it means that people building institutions of this sort have another serious system to study, instead of inventing their governance during their first dispute.

# From One Community to a Movement

## Experiment Before You Divorce

[P1] Experimenting with a different rule doesn't have to mean everybody goes out and buys new land right away. Try it inside the parent group first, then as a semi-autonomous project, and then, if it needs more independence, as a seed group nearby that still shares some tools, childcare, business or friendships. Splitting off completely should be just one step along that gradient, and nobody should have to do it just to be allowed to experiment.

[P2] Some experiments (like a different land system, or a different population size, or any number of rules that work together) can't be tried in a way that's cheap. They may look bad when they're starved of the assets they actually need. "We tried it in one room for three months" isn't always a fair test.

## Federated Communities Make a Movement

[P3] ⟦author⟧The problem of how these communities stay connected does eventually need to be solved somehow, if the idea is for them to spread without building another state.⟦/author⟧ It should be solved in a way that allows help between communities and the movement of people and knowledge, and also keeps the differences alive, so communities can keep learning from one another. At the federation level, that outward purpose gets concrete: spreading healing practices, training people, seeding new projects, and keeping any one comfortable enclave from thinking it's the whole movement.

[P4] The Federation of Egalitarian Communities (FEC) says it's rebuilding, and that's a good warning. It takes work to run a federation. Delegates. Records. Compliance. Coordinating resources. Holding meetings. Succession. Whatever dispute function it actually takes on. These things all take up labor and material capacity before any local crisis has eaten into both. If the legal or economic environment requires money, the federation also needs to have a cash budget for dealing with it. But the work of federation doesn't have to be done by a single entity. Two communities might want to be closely linked as friends, and that doesn't stop both of them from also being part of a general federation. There can also be local clusters, or single-function institutions like the FEC's PEACH health pool, which isn't currently governed by the FEC delegates.

[P5] Cooperation also shouldn't slide into assimilation. The FEC has full members and communities working toward full membership, and Friends who may just want to stay friends. Also, people need a similarly practical freedom to move between communities, and that takes work: housing, status, health coverage, records, labor accounting, travel costs, taxes, and disputes over the transition all have to be set up, or the freedom to move is just decorative. Fortunately, the interface between communities can often be thin, saying who's responsible for what while somebody crosses between them, and communities don't have to have the same internal systems for it to work.

[P6] Even if nobody uses money internally, communities still need the same honesty about exchanges and obligations. A gift of food, pooled medicine, a vehicle or a tool on loan, trades of labor, emergency housing, a shared building project, a visit that's mainly for training or relationship building: they're all different events, and some of them create future obligations while others don't. The federation can keep track of these for each purpose, without having to put a value on them or require every exchange to be "even". If cash is used for transactions with the outside world, grants and loans need to be kept separate the same way, and so do guarantees and shared enterprise commitments. Solidarity doesn't mean every community is liable for whatever the others owe, and it doesn't mean anyone has an unlimited claim on another community's labor and stores, or its land and tools.

[P7] ⟦author⟧Whenever a community or a federation creates or applies a rule, it's best practice to store the full case history so it can be re-evaluated in future: what happened, the principles that actually conflicted, your first judgments, and your final decision and rationale. Any dissenting opinions should go in there as well, along with the consequences in the case. When the rule changes because of a new case, run the old case through again to see how it would turn out. That saves each generation from having to rediscover the same constitutional fight from scratch.⟦/author⟧

# Founderism: The Four Questions

[P1] ⟦author⟧In every community there was somebody who took the initiative to get it started. Somebody found the land, wrote the first documents, taught the method, recruited the first members, or kept things going when everybody else lost interest.⟦/author⟧

[P2] ⟦author⟧That person ends up with real influence, and pretending they don't just makes that influence harder to look at. When that power fails to get distributed as the conditions ripen for that, it's called founderism.⟦/author⟧

[P3] ⟦author⟧I've seen founderism in many communes that I've visited, and I can see it playing out if I were to be a founder as well. I've got all these proposals and visions here in my guides, and so it would be very easy for me to become the person who decides whether or not everyone else “understood” them correctly, even while I wish to remain humble and open to the input of all involved.⟦/author⟧

[P4] ⟦author⟧A few things help keep that in check. One is to judge the method by results people can actually see. Will they see the members safely flourishing without being trapped? Can conflicts get resolved? Are the kids doing well once they grow up? When the method gets a prediction wrong, can the community say so and change course? And if the community is meant to spread, do new projects actually start up and last? Those questions seem more important to me than how big or old the commune gets, or how many decisions are made by consensus.⟦/author⟧

[P5] ⟦author⟧Allow for parallel implementations. Fork the method. If the community finds a way to alter Hearthwork and get better results, they've taught me something. If every change they make still needs the founder's “blessing”, then it's not really running in parallel, is it?⟦/author⟧

[P6] ⟦author⟧The questions so far have been about the method. These four are about the founder:⟦/author⟧

[P7] ⟦author⟧Can the community respectfully override the founder? Can the community replace the role of the founder? Can the community alter the vision of the founder? Can the community exist without the founder?⟦/author⟧

[P8] ⟦author⟧Then follow the practical power. Whose name is on the deed? Who's in charge of the housing and the food stores, the vehicles and tools, and any bank account outside the community? Who keeps the records? Who decides who gets admitted, and who picks the people who review the founder? Who controls access to the kids, or to medicine, and who can make a complaint go away? The org chart might look flat and horizontal, while the keys, passwords, titles and even just general reputation all point to one person.⟦/author⟧

[P9] ⟦author⟧Remember, it's not an override unless it can actually work on the person in question. If the founder selects the person to review the founder, holds the records, has title to the land, and can kick out complainers, then there's no real way for the community to override the founder, no matter how many meetings it holds.⟦/author⟧

[P10] ⟦author⟧Also watch for the daily signs of founderism. Increasing focus on the founder over time. Sense that disagreement is failure. Every bad result is blamed on lack of good implementation. Increasing “insider” knowledge while public accountability decreases. Results are never measured. Sense that former members become morally contaminated the moment they leave.⟦/author⟧

[P11] ⟦author⟧Presence of one of these alone isn't proof of abuse, but if several are present, they are worth paying attention to before the founder can explain how the situation is uniquely different in a spiritual way.⟦/author⟧

[P12] ⟦author⟧Ask these questions about any community, teacher or method. And ask them about me, if people end up living on land I buy.⟦/author⟧

[P13] ⟦author⟧While founderism is a problem in every community, it's especially so in communities which form naturally over time as I'm describing may happen in my case. In such cases, there's no formal community and no formal rules until people decide to come together to make them. It doesn't make sense to do that at first, if, say, I'm the only one there and I don't know who else will be there that I could trust. If there's one other person who comes to visit, it also doesn't make sense, so there's a critical mass point at which people decide "we were just living together like friends, but now we want to formalize the community."⟦/author⟧

[P14] ⟦author⟧If I could find a suitable commune to join, that would basically reverse this problem. I would then be the one on probation. But if we are all starting together from scratch, then we would all be at an equal probationary level with each other.⟦/author⟧

[P15] ⟦author⟧It's a bit like the process of getting married. At first, you visit your girlfriend or boyfriend when schedules align and each of you agrees. Over time as you get closer to marriage (which hopefully is not a fully unconscious process), you start making decisions together and considering sharing more and more resources. Once married, if you decide to divorce, you split up the resources, although that's also contentious depending on how long the marriage was for, whether one person feels tricked, etc, and unfortunately often ends up in court. Leaving a commune is the divorce half of it, where the shared resources have to get split up. When joining or forming a commune, we can hopefully be more conscious about the process and less romantic.⟦/author⟧

[P16] ⟦author⟧Friends who come to visit don't get written terms, though. That's not how anything works, and at first they don't even know if they'll stick around for a day or a week. There are implied terms, like be respectful and be honest, and those shouldn't need to be in writing. If someone is offered something like an internship or a probationary period, that would require written terms. Or if it naturally evolves and they bring up wanting something like that, it could be done.⟦/author⟧

## How My Own Model Could Become a Cult

[P17] [caption] I could totally start a cult. Trust me.

[P18] Every practice in this article can be turned against the people using it.

[P19] “Nothing suppressed, everything processed” can become a situation where someone in the group must tell all, even if they don’t want to. I have seen that risk in groups that use the ZEGG Forum practice. ⟦author⟧A group starts out inviting honesty and ends up treating privacy as resistance. But privacy also can be resistance to the communal spirit, so there has to be a dynamic, living, always re-assessed balance between what you and the community owe each other, and what remains your own domain. In general, people can have their own private practices, of course.⟦/author⟧ But some practices go too far against the communal spirit. If someone starts worshipping Satan, say, they get booted.

[P20] ⟦author⟧Peer counseling can become peer surveillance, even if the tone is warm. And when communal therapy processes are pre-reqs for people to get medicine or to be respected members, it can feel like the community is gatekeeping. Well, it is gatekeeping. Some people simply should not be let in the gate, at least not yet. And some people will resent that, and choose to leave, and that's fine. Some communities will be stricter gatekeepers, and others will be more lax.⟦/author⟧

[P21] ⟦author⟧It should go without saying that nobody has to take medicine to have equal respect in the community. But medicine is an optional part of the overall therapy process, and if a member is avoiding the therapy process and becoming hard to deal with, they will naturally not belong at a certain point.⟦/author⟧ If it gets to the point of being impossible to live with, they will be kicked out. But nearly always, people who don't fit in leave way before that.

[P22] They should not be demonized for not trying to fit in. They should be offered compassion, but ultimately it's up to them if they want to fit in and follow the communal processes. And in some cases, the community may make exceptions. If someone is actually sick and needs care, kicking them out could be like a death sentence. It depends on so many factors that I can't decide any of this ahead of time. ⟦author⟧It's up to the people involved.⟦/author⟧

[P23] ⟦author⟧No single person or permanent group of people should handle grievances and have the power to decide who gets into or stays in the community. Each person should have a usable avenue for exit. Dissent is important for growth. That doesn't mean everything everyone says is equally valid, though. Sometimes it really is their wounded ego projection, and that should be acknowledged. But again, balance, because if complaints are too often or too broadly painted as projection, that's as much a failure, if not more so.⟦/author⟧

[P24] ⟦author⟧There are some basic human rights that can’t be voted away from you by the majority in a community, unless it's a question of resource availability as a whole. If safety and the conditions for health can be provided, it should be provided. People should be able to maintain relationships with others outside the community, as long as those relationships don't interfere with the community as a whole (for example, you can't bring your boyfriend to live with you if he's not only not a member of the community, but also antagonistic towards it).⟦/author⟧

[P25] It can be tricky to spell everything out, though. ⟦author⟧Let's say a grandmother is demonizing the commune.⟦/author⟧ Cutting off contact with her could make things way worse. When it's tricky and dangerous for everyone like that, I think the commune needs to step in and help people decide how to communicate with outsiders. Nobody can give that advice ahead of time, including me. It has to be worked out in the moment, context by context.

[P26] ⟦author⟧If your human rights are unfairly stripped from you, even by a majority vote, then you can vote with your feet.⟦/author⟧

# The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For

[P1] ⟦author⟧Many perfectly intelligent grownups would rather sit around for six hours talking about solar panels than talk about jealousy, God, death, childhood, money, sex, psychedelics, or somebody wanting to leave, which is what I ask people to do in this article.⟦/author⟧

[P2] ⟦author⟧This doesn't surprise me, since we're taught to put on a show of competence in modern society. We're given a lot of incentives to hide ourselves and disappear behind our screens the second things get uncomfortable. Emotional intelligence doesn't pay the bills. Instead we optimize to fit the career market with all the little energy we've got.⟦/author⟧

[P3] ⟦author⟧So then a lot of people end up thinking they aren't "good enough" for community, or getting labeled by others that way. And there's a real issue there, but it's more about what fits where & when than good or bad. The community needs to consider how much attention the newcomer currently has available for other people, how willing they are to examine their own reactions. And on the other hand, look at whether the group has enough free resource/capacity to support them without becoming a full-time caretaker operation.⟦/author⟧

[P4] ⟦author⟧Some people have engaged in long practices of contemplation or therapy, or have had very good luck in childhood; other people are just trying to survive some emergency in their life and need care more than responsibility. Most of us are all of these at different times in our lives.⟦/author⟧

[P5] ⟦author⟧In the model I’m imagining, some of the functions that are outsourced to therapists in modern society are distributed throughout the community. In other words, there’s a lot of peer counseling going on, people listening to each other, being there for each other and helping each other process ordinary emotional stuff.⟦/author⟧

[P6] ⟦author⟧That does not make the community infinitely absorbent like a Heavy Flow Maxi Pad.⟦/author⟧

[P7] ⟦author⟧I’m going to use the phrase “free resource,” which comes from Re-evaluation Counseling, to refer to the attention, steadiness, skill and practical capacity people have left over for others once they’ve dealt with enough of their own immediate stuff, and “need” to refer to the amount of help people need at a given time.⟦/author⟧

[P8] ⟦author⟧Everyone has a certain amount of both in different ratios.⟦/author⟧

[P9] ⟦author⟧My own need has exceeded my free resource at many points.⟦/author⟧ Good Lord. ⟦author⟧During one stretch, my first wife tried to kill me while psychotic because she believed I was her sociopathic father. Then she took my son. Later she tried to kill my mother, while her pimp stalked my sister’s family until they moved to Canada.⟦/author⟧

[P10] ⟦author⟧I could go on and on, but the math is pretty clear by now. There wasn’t much of me left over for anybody else. So when I say someone might need to stabilize and do inner pl/ork before helping found a community, I’m not saying it from a mountaintop.⟦/author⟧

[P11] ⟦author⟧The ability to welcome somebody is partly a problem of resources, then, not compassion -- how many people can a group take in without hurting the existing members or the people they are taking in?⟦/author⟧

[P12] ⟦author⟧And that also brings us to a difficult catch-22 for fledgling groups. The need for new members in a small community is greater, but the ability of that community to absorb disruption is less. With only eight people in the community, someone still has to grow the food and fix whatever breaks. Somebody has to balance the books and watch the kids. And the water system has to keep working. The water well doesn’t care how beautiful the new member’s heart is.⟦/author⟧

[P13] ⟦author⟧So early on, members usually need to have more available resource than acute need, and some practical skills to contribute. They usually need to be willing to learn, too. Once there are 80 members, though, there's usually a lot more free resource to draw on.⟦/author⟧

[P14] ⟦author⟧I still don’t know how to keep this language of capacity from becoming a respectable way to exclude people who are disabled, poor, less articulate, maybe simply uncomfortable or unfamiliar with therapeutic language, or for whatever reason deemed inconvenient. Pretending capacity is unlimited won’t solve that. Any real process would need concrete criteria, review by someone who wasn’t part of the decision, and a way to revisit a “not now” decision on membership. Even then, the power to veto membership applications based on capacity remains dangerously tilted towards elitism.⟦/author⟧

[P15] ⟦author⟧But before you submit your application to the community, submit it to yourself. This community may not be a good fit for you right now if:⟦/author⟧

[P16] ⟦author⟧You have a need that far outstrips your free resource right now. This doesn’t mean you’re bad or doomed, it’s about timing; it just means you need to take a first step of finding support where you are and building up your resources. If you don't have a free resource calculator on hand, an easy way to tell is if you’re running from some crisis right now and mainly need a place to land, get treatment, housing, legal aid, etc. A founding group shouldn’t pretend it can give you those.⟦/author⟧

[P17] ⟦author⟧You want the feeling of village life but don’t want to take on governance, maintenance, conflict, care, etc.⟦/author⟧

[P18] ⟦author⟧You’re not willing to look at yourself when jealousy or conflict comes up.⟦/author⟧

[P19] ⟦author⟧Any humane membership process should have ways of not flat-out rejecting people who fall into these categories, or anyone else it can’t take right now, like saying “later,” or “in another role,” or “with more support.”⟦/author⟧
