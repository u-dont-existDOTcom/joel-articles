# The New Age May Dawn Suddenly

[P1] I just put a movie my father made in 1988 up on the Internet for the first time: Where's Utopia? It was the result of years of work, traveling to all kinds of communes and communal experiments around the country, trying to answer a question that for him was primary: How do we live together without recreating the same loneliness, domination, and stupidity we were supposedly escaping?

[P2] He had already spent 17 years in the Bruderhof, a Christian community that doesn’t use money internally. He had no personal money there: income went into the common purse and the community provided what members needed. So when I talk later about moving beyond internal money, I’m not proposing something nobody has ever lived. My father spent most of two decades doing it. They also accepted him as a Jewish atheist, making him a remarkably long-running doctrinal exception. In meditation there, he came to his own understanding that “God is the Universe, and the Universe is Love.”

[P3] [caption] This was the banner my mom painted that hung in our living room for decades. I will try to locate the actual photo.

[P4] He might have stayed, but the U.S. antiwar movement during Vietnam needed somebody who knew how to set up printing presses. He eventually founded the Aquarian Research Foundation—not a commune, but a nonprofit devoted to researching how the planet could have a positive future and publishing what it found. His conclusion was simple and enormous: human beings need small tribal communities again, because Civilization Is A Hoax, as I blogged about at the beginning of the Covid plandemic.

[P5] That’s where the **escuelita** in my title comes in. Escuelita means “little school. I’m borrowing the word from the Zapatistas in Mexico, whose Escuelita brought outsiders into their communities for a while to learn autonomy by living it. In the same way, people would live in the healing communities I’m proposing, to heal and to learn the relational and practical methods, so that some of them could eventually leave and set up another one somewhere else. Otherwise, we’ve built a lovely refuge for whoever got there first.

[P9] I spent much of my childhood being carried through Twin Oaks, Acorn, East Wind, Padanaram, Zendik, and many other communities. I was usually just a visitor’s kid, close enough to see how people lived but never quite part of the tribe. At the time that meant a lot of boredom, with some adventures mixed in, but those years gave me a great sample to compare against.

[P10] From 1999 to 2001, my family actually lived with the Love Israel family in Washington while escaping Y2K, that brief historical period when otherwise reasonable adults prepared for civilization to be defeated by a two-digit date. We lived in a yurt. There were wild berries of every kind, and I loved being in nature. I was also lonely, bored, irritated by the cult programming, and missing my friends in Philadelphia.

[P11] I wanted to befriend the children there, but they treated me as an outsider. My mom never liked the place. The leader was also named Love Israel, and to me he seemed like the fakest person I’d ever met, complete with the expected God complex.

[P12] We spent a lot of time on our knees in the community garden growing food they could sell in town. The money was opaque and seemed to travel mostly upward toward Love. Meanwhile everybody sang, “Here in the garden, there’s nothing left to do but eat sleep and keep clean. Again and again. 🌱

[P13] My father eventually gave the community his airplane. They sold it instead of using it. He felt tricked, began exposing what was happening, and then we were asked to leave.

[P14] The East Wind commune was almost the opposite way. There were maybe eighty people when I was there, enough to have a variety of personalities, small enough to actually make friends. I was happy there. I especially liked showering in a communal shower with both sexes present. I wonder if that still goes on at East Wind? It's the sort of thing that doesn't make it into the history books.

[P15] Even at East Wind, though, there was a quieter power issue. They had a nut butter factory that brought in a lot of their income, which seemed to make the woman running it very hard to question. Otherwise I thought the community had a great social life, a nice way of distributing labor, and no obvious guru.

[P16] There were like eight people at Acorn when I was there, and no kids. I couldn't tell whether it was a commune I might join or a group of people who were already friends, and who happened to live on some really beautiful land and be good at building cool stuff. Joining would have felt less like applying to a commune than asking whether their group chat had an opening.

[P17] Twin Oaks was much bigger, like two or three hundred people at the time, and almost institutional. It was friendly and interesting, but I didn't feel like I was in a tribe there. ⟦author⟧It was more like a tiny hippie town, but with a highly structured political system that didn't seem intuitive for hippies.⟦/author⟧

[P18] Looking back, almost none of these communities had a communal therapy practice. People brought the usual human pain and suffering with them, and it all got translated into politics.

[P19] Which meant weekly meetings, run according to Robert's Rules of Order, with enormous agendas, where most of the things you needed to talk about couldn't be talked about, and you generally felt like you'd rather be sleeping. ⟦author⟧(Some people, including me, solved this problem by just going to sleep in the meeting and openly snoring.)⟦/author⟧

[P20] Zendik went in the other direction. They held a brutal community meeting that I’m still recovering from 35 years later. 🤣 Five-year-old me was put in the center and told I was bad because I hadn’t volunteered to help adults with whatever jobs they were doing. Apparently kindergarten had already failed to teach me labor consciousness.

[P21] The secular communities often buried hurt under procedure. The spiritual communities often put one person in charge of everybody’s inner life. Most of the rest of this article is my attempt to describe the missing third option.

# Communes Are Hot Again 🔥

[P1] Suddenly the longing behind my father’s movie is everywhere again, and in 2023 the U.S. Surgeon General even put out an advisory about it. Or about the loneliness under it, at least. The advisory said roughly half of American adults experience loneliness, and it treated social connection as a public-health issue.

[P2] Whether you want to call that loneliness an official “epidemic” or not, it’s obvious that a lot of people can’t tolerate modern life in isolation anymore. They may disagree about politics, food, God, sex, or whether shoes are oppressive, but they agree that something about the current arrangement is starvin’ them.

[P3] Check out the Google Trends for “ecovillage” and “intentional community”. After years of decline, interest climbed sharply in the mid-2020s.

[P4] I have no proof that the rise of AI caused this turn, but it’s hard to ignore the coincidence in timing. AI became serious around this time, and it started changing people’s jobs and relationships, and how they see the future. ⟦author⟧Suddenly, forming a village sounded like a good backup plan, not just a hippie utopian whim.⟦/author⟧

[P5] It’s poppin’ on Instagram too! 🔥 Chantress Seba—my favorite light-language love influencer—posted an application for people interested in starting a commune. ⟦author⟧I applied, although I don’t think she’s coming to Africa, where I'm at.⟦/author⟧ When I saw the post, many thousands of people appeared to be interested. Imagine opening your inbox after casually inviting a million followers to co-found a village.

[P6] Later, when another influencer with a big following floated the commune idea, the comment section started to become a recruitment board. It was chaotic, but sincere, and it was revealing. There were people in the comments finding prospective members the way you’d usually find a used sofa.

### The Freeloader Problem

[P7] Some of the commenters weren’t trying to join. The oldest objection, that maybe most people don’t want to take on the responsibility that anarchism requires and communal property becomes “owned by everyone, cared for by nobody,” showed up almost right away.

[P8] ⟦author⟧The commune comments became a miniature political-philosophy seminar, as Instagram comments sometimes do.⟦/author⟧

[P9] ⟦author⟧I'd say the comment above is more true of the big socialism experiments than small communes, but I still take it seriously, because some people really would rather let somebody else take responsibility.⟦/author⟧ When everybody owns everything, it can get to where nobody knows who's supposed to do what, and the same three people end up doing all the maintenance while everybody else talks about freedom. And then unofficial leaders start gaining power. Partly that's because everyone keeps handing it to them.

[P10] ⟦author⟧Communal ownership entails folks taking on visible and named stewardship duties. It requires knowing what to do if someone is leeching off the group and how to deal with it. It requires developing healthier forms of relationships. No, taking away the idea of a boss does not make everyone less passive, selfish, thieving, nor suddenly unlearning learned helplessness.⟦/author⟧

[P11] ⟦author⟧People want to go back to the old way of living, but they have no idea why earlier communes failed or what to do when someone wants to own land, the group runs out of money, a baby is born or jealousy rears its head. They just know they are tired and alone and want to change it.⟦/author⟧

[P12] I am not currently recruiting anyone or founding a community, which makes it easier for me to write an article like this, since I’m not trying to get you onto my land. I do plan to buy land, and people may naturally end up living around me. All of the warnings in this article apply to me, too.

# Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways

[P1] Most communes of my acquaintance went wrong from the inside, in one of two ways.

## The secular failure

[P2] The secular communes had a solution for how to distribute property or labor, but when the deep waters of jealousy or childhood hurt or humiliation or attraction or resentment or grief came up, the group was without a common method for dealing with it. The feelings just became agenda items.

[P3] Meeting procedure isn’t going to solve every problem, no matter how sophisticated the system of labor and money and decision making may be in a community of the Federation of Egalitarian Communities or any other income-sharing community. It can’t make it so that two people don’t hate each other. It can only decide whose turn it is to talk about the hatred, and whether a motion about it needs somebody to second it.

[P4] Eventually, when people are together long enough, politics will become a way of carrying emotional material that people can’t name. There will be a fight about a schedule or committee or noise or food or some policy or other, but it won’t be about that. ⟦author⟧There’s no shared way to talk about the hurt itself, but the meeting agenda is the one place the group lets you push for something and win, so the angry communard goes there, trying to get some justice.⟦/author⟧

## The spiritual failure

[P5] This comes about when the community realizes the importance of embracing a transformation from within, and then funnels it through a particular person, usually the founder, a teacher, a channel or some other special knower of how things really are.

[P6] ⟦author⟧The Farm, in Tennessee, at its largest about 1,500 people, had many beautiful values. But in the early days, the community’s spirituality and social life followed the founder, Stephen Gaskin. From what former members have shared, the community’s doctrine ran deep into members’ intimate decisions. This is how it often goes: folks talk about oneness with the universe, while one man defines what the universe is.⟦/author⟧

[P7] ⟦author⟧The two failures mirror each other. Secular groups distribute power but leave the inner life mostly private or ignored. Spiritual groups attempt to address the inner life, but because it's the inner world being shaped by the outer as a condition of membership, we tend to find both real-world and spiritual power concentrating around whoever defines the path.⟦/author⟧

[P8] The deeper problem is that somebody who's earned authority in one role can use it in all their other roles. The founder who is a gifted teacher, the therapist who really helps people, the farmer or business manager that actually carries a lot of the economy. The danger starts when one person has the ability to define the problem, control the information related to the problem, define what the solution is, and make it hard for people to leave or express disagreement.

[P9] ⟦author⟧I dream of a way to work in depth that isn’t run by a guru, and a way of being in a community with distributed authority. A place where people who've done the healing first can join without pretending they're fully finished, and that also has peer practice and enough play that it doesn’t become a permanent repair shop.⟦/author⟧

# The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together

[P1] ⟦author⟧Communities tend to focus on the aspect of their project that was most interesting to the original founders, for example political types getting super into writing by-laws, or spiritual types just meditating all the time, or therapists processing each other, or people who like psychedelics discovering they’re all God together and never getting to the point of deciding who will put their name on the deed.⟦/author⟧

[P2] ⟦author⟧I’ve broken the model I propose down into the 4 parts shown in the above diagram. Note, Pl/ork means play + work. Calling therapy “inner work” forgets the inner child, who also wants to play. (Forgive the neologism.⟦/author⟧ I hope pl/ork isn’t too corny. 🌽): 1) inner pl/ork (reparenting), 2) relational pl/ork (peer counseling especially), 3) medicine pl/ork (like loveyhuasca or iboga), and 4) the practical logistical foundation which is most of this article, since I have guides separately on the other parts. ⟦author⟧Obviously the details are complicated, and jealousy, children, money, land, God, and iboga don’t fit neatly into four boxes, but let’s go with it.⟦/author⟧

[P3] ⟦author⟧These four parts leak into one another almost immediately. Governance can end up slyly incorporating personal tensions like jealousy. Those who have positions with more control of shared resources will automatically be affected by that psychologically (power corrupts, as they say). Those who know medicine tend to have another type of god complex since they may make life or death decisions for people routinely. How folks choose to raise children can expose values the founders never discussed. And myriad other categories arise, of course.⟦/author⟧

[P4] ⟦author⟧And let's face it: food, housing, and logistics are not enough reason to bind whole lives together. That's more like a situationship. A community needs a shared sacred inner practice that gives the difficulty of our life together a deeper meaning. We help each other heal and choose love over fear. And we turn outward with a purpose, using the escuelita model to bring our healing to the world.⟦/author⟧

## Founded on Freedom

[P5] ⟦author⟧Nothing suppressed, everything processed. Each of us guides our own practice, and our practices weave the community into the quilt that keeps us all warm and safe. Whatever answers we find stay fluid and organic.⟦/author⟧

[P6] ⟦author⟧Speaking of dynamically weaving ourselves together, though, a community does need some stable agreements that they're not tweaking and revising every time somebody has a revelation in breathwork. But these agreements need to stay open to lived experience.⟦/author⟧

[P7] ⟦author⟧When agreements get frozen, religious communities tend to call the frozen version doctrine. Secular communities on the other hand often call it “the process,” which can become just as sacred.⟦/author⟧

[P8] ⟦author⟧To give an example of striking the balance: My contemplative practice is grounded in the early Buddhist suttas, with a critical reading. Most are treasure, while some are obviously late additions (sometimes to the point of real absurdity). A living community should relate to its own teachings in that same critical manner while staying devoted to following what they've decided on so far.⟦/author⟧

### 1. Inner pl/ork

[P9] ⟦author⟧Probably the most obvious type of pl/ork is reparenting. Here’s an example: you teach your inner child geography, while s/he teaches you the world is a cat!⟦/author⟧

[P10] ⟦author⟧Inner-child reparenting means learning to identify as the best holistic inner adult, which is comprised of the three inner adult roles: Nurturer, Protector, and Guide. The newly developed adult reparents their newly recognized inner child. The child receives all that love, protection, and guidance that you give it, and as a result, your little one gains more freedom to remain alive inside you, which brings back the joy of life that many adults have forgotten about.⟦/author⟧

[P11] ⟦author⟧And this reparenting journey is built on somatic regulation first. I.e., listening to the body's feelings before making a decision with the mind. And that actually symbolizes the adult/child relationship to me in some way as well.⟦/author⟧

### 2. Relational pl/ork

[P12] ⟦author⟧Hearthwork distributes counseling among peers: equal time, attention without interpretation or fixing, and then you switch.⟦/author⟧

[P13] ⟦author⟧The idea is that the capacity to be there for people when they need help isn’t located in one professional or guru, or the founder of the group that everyone emotionally depends on, but in the community as a whole.⟦/author⟧

[P14] ⟦author⟧But since we're merging peer therapy with community, we also need to make sure that the person caring about you and listening to you shouldn't translate that information into administration over your life, the way a professional therapist might diagnose you as this or that and then suddenly you're ineligible for certain careers. Although obviously that information can come out regardless when people are in close quarters.⟦/author⟧

[P15] ⟦author⟧Another thing is knowing which problems are beyond what peers can handle and need outside or expert help. And another is to deal with jealousy and conflict while it's still between the people involved, before it gets put on the agenda (generally at the bottom of the agenda). Speaking of jealousy, check out my Romance Guide, since that's a topic each person & each couple first handles by themselves.⟦/author⟧

### 3. Medicine pl/ork

[P16] ⟦author⟧Community can reach new heights by carefully incorporating serious plant medicines with peer guidance rather than exalting any one person as a special shaman.⟦/author⟧

[P17] ⟦author⟧This doesn’t mean that a peer-led ceremony should be done without structure. Things like screening participants, making sure the sitters are sober, having a plan for dealing with emergencies, planning for integration, and other rules and boundaries need to be dealt with beforehand. While there may be some pure learning that could come from having several altered members figure out what rules and boundaries are needed on the spot, it’s not something I recommend.⟦/author⟧

### 4. The container

[P18] ⟦author⟧This is the part about membership, land, resources, governance, children, leaving, etc.⟦/author⟧

[P19] ⟦author⟧Like my father, I’m aiming for communities with less and less money involved, since money is antagonistic to the spirit of community. If I have money, I can get whatever I want, regardless of whether anyone cares about my wellbeing. That's the hegemonic philosophy of life we are trying to overturn. I think the path there has three stages.⟦/author⟧

[P20] ⟦author⟧First there will be no money exchanged between members for basic community functions like food, housing, care, tools, education, transport etc. Members might have outside jobs, and they bring an agreed share of that income in. This is probably a good place to start if someone wants to create a community now and have it be realistic.⟦/author⟧

[P21] ⟦author⟧Later all wages and other income earned by the members from work outside the community, or from business inside the community, will be paid into the common purse of the community. No member accumulates private wealth while a member of the community. Some version of this is how things have been done in the Bruderhof community for generations, and my father lived there for 17 years. It is also done, in a somewhat different way, at Twin Oaks, and a number of other income-sharing communities. So a common purse isn’t just a theory.⟦/author⟧

[P22] ⟦author⟧Eventually, the community would also stop using money outside. People would meet their needs directly, or through exchange (be it trade, gift, work, obligation, or whatever). I couldn't find a well-documented modern community that has gone this far, and in fact, it may be impractical in some locations because of government requirements like taxes on land. So perhaps the practical end point here is a community that is nearly entirely moneyless, but has a small collective cash boundary on the outside.⟦/author⟧

[P23] ⟦author⟧None of this does away with scarcity or economic power. There are lots of areas where such power can be wielded: access to food storage, housing, tools, land, medicine, transportation, and the outside world’s currency, to name a few. Even in a moneyless community, someone can control what's scarce. Who decides how these things are distributed? What do members owe one another? What happens when someone wants to leave?⟦/author⟧

[P24] ⟦author⟧These are less exciting topics to discuss spiritually. It's rare to see someone come back from an ecstatic vision with news of how to revamp the community’s policies on provisional members, or how to divvy up assets with a departing member, or how to prevent one founder from having control over the land, or what to do when a child reports something serious. And if they do, it's still something that needs a sober thinking-through.⟦/author⟧

[P25] ⟦author⟧But no amount of gorgeous land will make up for the mess that is created when one person owns the land or has the final say about what will happen on it. And when members come on board faster than the group can support them, all the peer counseling in the world won't make up for it, any more than medicine makes up for the lack of a written exit agreement.⟦/author⟧

[P26] ⟦author⟧The first three parts are the inside of the container, and this container is what gives those a stable place to manifest.⟦/author⟧

[P27] ⟦author⟧If one of the four gets skipped, it will need to be dealt with eventually, at high stress, usually after the group has invested land, labor, or other resources and everyone has an opinion on how things should be.⟦/author⟧

# The Medicine Part - Yes, I'm Naming It

[P1] ⟦author⟧Most of the writing on communities hasn't caught up with psychedelics.⟦/author⟧ They've gone from taboo toward regulated use really fast (licensed service centers for psilocybin started opening in Oregon in 2023, Colorado set up a system for regulating natural medicines and began licensing in 2025, and New Mexico enacted a Medical Psilocybin Act in 2025), and Australia has allowed authorized psychiatrists limited access for a list of conditions since 2023, for both psilocybin and MDMA.

[P2] That said, it's not a smooth ride or an inevitability. The US FDA issued a Complete Response Letter to the MDMA-assisted therapy application in 2024, where they did not approve it and requested more evidence. And the thing with Portugal is that many people will lazily say "they've legalized everything there" - but that's false, they've only decriminalized possessing small quantities for personal use. It's still a crime to traffic drugs.

[P3] All of which means the legality of psychedelics varies by country and by state. It varies by substance, too. And it depends on a group’s religious status and on what exactly it does. ⟦author⟧So if psychedelic medicine is going to be part of a community at all, that community should definitely get actual local legal advice.⟦/author⟧

[P4] Most intentional communities using psychedelics are still not public about it, due to possible trouble (even where the law allows some use) from parents worried about children, from neighbors imagining chaos, from insurance providers who might drop them, and from local officials who may not understand any of the distinctions.

[P5] I'm talking about it here because not to talk about it would be to falsify my model. Many of the people who read this article are already familiar with my writing about psychedelics, including my loveyhuasca/Haoma material. If this article is going to be honest, it has to talk about both the value and the risk.

[P6] There’s a serious case against having medicine as part of a community. Physical and psychological negative effects of ayahuasca were common in the Global Ayahuasca Survey, although severe effects were far less common and context mattered. And these medicines can open things up faster than a person (or their group) can deal with.

[P7] They do also attract people who are all for revelation and hate the follow-through, people who can collect 12 ceremonies and 6 origin stories and still can't say sorry to their housemate. Any community based mainly around the peak experience is going to find out sooner or later that the insight doesn't do the practical work for it. Sometimes the dishes need doing and a scared kid needs comforting.

[P8] ⟦author⟧In my experience there are real reasons to take psychedelics communally too - eg they can lower defensive barriers which otherwise dominate every serious conversation, and create strong bonds between members of the community who have been through the harder nights together.⟦/author⟧ But there are also negatives that can come from taking a psychedelic - confusion, grandiosity, dependency, fouling up dynamics between members. Are you mature enough as a community to tell the good from the bad?

## Community Is What Makes Psychedelics Pl/ork Best!

[P9] Part of it is integration: an insight needs somewhere to land. What happens when you come back from an amazing experience which gave you insight and clarity about your life, into the same old environment where nobody knows what you’ve been through and you’re faced with the same old cues? It can be super helpful to have gone through the experience in a community, where the people who held you through it can also notice what happens to you over the following months.

[P10] The deeper you go, the more alienated you can feel. ⟦author⟧After my original ayahuasca ego deaths at 25, I had trouble finding anyone in the physical world who seemed to have experienced anything similar.⟦/author⟧ I couldn’t even find them at Buddhist monasteries despite all the preaching about enlightenment. ⟦author⟧That helped give me a God complex, a Savior complex, or both, which then attracted exactly the right people to show me my limits. 😂 In a community of people who’ve sat through the same nights, you aren’t the only one who’s been there, and someone can catch the God complex early.⟦/author⟧

[P11] Do you trust the people you're relying on for support? Support from people you trust is different from paid support, which is often paying people you don't know to look after you for a night. Sometimes they're great, but sometimes the person with the feather has only known you for 4 hours and already has a cosmology to explain everything you say.

[P12] In a community, your sober sitter may be someone who's known you for years, and you feel the difference between being watched and being held. This is especially important when doing iboga or other long and medically risky experiences. For those you need to be properly screened beforehand and monitored continuously.

[P13] Your peers can also reality check any downloads you may have had. Psychedelics can give you insight and they can give you convincing nonsense, all in the same bright shining light. A few friends who know your history may notice the difference between new understanding and… mom.

[P14] One of the benefits of community is rhythm, and many traditional medicinal practices occur within ceremonies that have a communal schedule. The shared schedule of ceremonies, rather than each person doing ceremonies when they want, can serve to limit compulsive use and provide for integration time.

[P15] There are the practical benefits: logistics are easier when it comes to things like having childcare arranged by the group that parents are comfortable leaving their kid(s) with, not needing to drive away from the ceremony, having someone bring water to someone or just sit quietly with them while they come back to normal consciousness, etc.

[P16] And then there will be people to play with! Raves often let strangers fast-track intimacy (getting to a level of intimacy that usually comes after building trust), but imagine doing ecstatic music & dance in a community that already knows each other (and each other’s inconvenient habits). 💃🕺

[P17] But I won’t idealize psychedelics. Some people and communities should avoid them. Where access is ahead of competence, people will become casualties of enthusiasm. Is your community humble enough to say no? To pause? Using the medicine takes that. It also takes humility to refer people out, and admit when an experience did harm.

## My Ceremony Principles

[P18] No special shamans here. Traditional lineages usually disagree with me on this, and many ayahuasca and Bwiti communities believe the medicine should stay under trained lineage authority. ⟦author⟧I respect what those traditions have preserved, and I've learned some "dos & don't dos" from their sensible practices and less sensible practices.⟦/author⟧ For example, although I do highly value loving kindness meditation, inner child reparenting, and nibbana meditation before ceremony, during, and at all times, I notice a lot of people want their ceremony to look and feel like some kind of abstract mix of indigenous rituals & props to make it feel more authentic and sacred than their everyday life. I understand the benefit of setting aside one time and place for sacred experiences (the book, "God is Red" comes to mind), although the shadow side to that is making the spiritual seem like another world apart from your regular life, which is sort of opposite to integration.

[P19] And while I do think it's valuable to have someone highly experienced in navigating the spirit worlds who is able to sense where you are in those and guide you, I also wouldn't trust many of these shamans to guide me, considering it's common to find that even the ones who can heal people very well may still be happy to send evil spirits to kill someone, if that hit job is lucrative. I also don't trust their medical intuition in general, considering the Huni Kuin shamans are famous for claiming that all other ayahuasca shamans are giving people cancer by using the knotted B. caapi vine (since the knots remind them of tumors). ⟦author⟧They're right though that modern folk do very often decide they're ready to lead ceremonies long before they're really trained and prepared. I'm sure I could still learn a lot from those traditions, and they will continue to develop their understandings as well.⟦/author⟧

[P20] My basic issue is with placing one charismatic person above everyone else’s direct experience. The facilitator on the retreat circuit may be wise. ⟦author⟧Or they may be someone who hasn’t been vetted by anybody, who isn’t accountable to anyone, maybe even a sexual predator, or financially dependent on people coming back to them for more feather-waving. And the more deeply someone is opening up, the more it costs not to personally know who's running their ceremony.⟦/author⟧

[P21] Even in a culture of peer medicine, there could still be an informal "shaman", someone everyone trusts and gradually stops questioning, even though nobody gave them the title. I don't know yet how to avoid this. Things that might help would be to have rotation of roles, and public accountability. Though neither of these seem like a complete solution.

[P22] Still, I want people to learn to guide themselves and care for each other, within the limits of their competence. I’ve written more about peer-held ceremonies, and about the best psychopath shaman I ever met.

[P23] ⟦author⟧Just because it's a peer-led ceremony doesn't mean it's ok to be casually irresponsible.⟦/author⟧ In fact, since the authority is spread around, there needs to be more competence. Some things are a must: screening for contraindications (both medical and psychiatric), reviewing medications and combinations, having the right kind of sober sitter present, and deciding before anybody takes anything what kind of situations would require professional help or emergency care.

[P24] Also, I am especially cautious about medicines and protocols that I see as more destabilizing and/or presenting more of a medical burden. This includes some uses of ketamine and MDMA and bufo. ⟦author⟧My reasons are given in those articles, respectively, since they need more space.⟦/author⟧

[P25] Using medicine is secondary to all of the other pl/ork that comes first: reparenting, developing somatic capacity, and learning to do peer counseling. The medicine can deepen an existing practice, but it can't be a pharmacological short cut to what takes several months of honest relationship with other people.

[P26] This prerequisite also takes care of another issue in one shot: it weeds out the experience collectors. ⟦author⟧If someone is unwilling to put in 6 months learning to listen without imposing their solution, it is unlikely they will come out of a ceremony more relationally mature, regardless of how many self-transforming fractalized machine elves told them they are the reincarnation of King David & Elvis.😂⟦/author⟧

[P27] ⟦author⟧Integration is a term thrown around so much that I'm not sure if it still has meaning.⟦/author⟧ But basically, it's your everyday life. The ceremony is supposed to open something up in you. Look at your life in the weeks after the ceremony and see how things have changed. That includes your relationships, behaviors, sleeping habits, your decision-making, and perhaps most importantly, how well you can deal with frustration without declaring a new spiritual emergency. 😂

[P28] All of the “supports” are important: preparation, nutrition, screening for drug interactions, being disciplined about the dose, and having a plan for dealing with emergencies. They matter more than ceremony aesthetics. My current safety material is at Altered States Triage.

[P29] Finally, there are the legal issues. These vary a lot, and they are not always well-defined. ⟦author⟧As a community, you need to write down what is and isn’t allowed, which activities are private (to the individual, to a group in the community, or to the community vs the outside), and what kinds of activities have been reviewed from a legal standpoint.⟦/author⟧ “I don’t think anybody around here has ever heard of that” is information, but it’s not the same thing as a legal opinion.

## After the Psychedelic Calling

[P30] A lot of people feel the urge to form a village after going through some serious psychedelic and/or mystical experiences. Finding their people and helping others get to whatever just opened up is part of the same urge.

[P31] ⟦author⟧I’ve felt that urge myself.⟦/author⟧ The problem is that it can come with grandiosity, and I’ve felt that too. You come back believing love can heal anyone, and then you meet people who don’t want to change, people who sincerely want to change but keep choosing the opposite, and people in such a crisis that their need for help is more than your enthusiasm can hold.

[P32] That call may be real, but forming a community based on omnipotence is a terrible idea.

[P33] A group that begins in collective spiritual euphoria can recruit too quickly and promise too much, and only do the math on how much need it can absorb once several people already depend on it. The sections on capacity and on membership, later on in this article, are partly there to protect the original generosity from that first rush.

[P34] Once the euphoria wears off and people have a more sober view of the call they feel, it can become useful. A community can be a place for people to train up in the practices, and sometimes go off and start another experiment. ⟦author⟧In fact, if people are training there so they can help others build communities of their own, they’d be working towards forming a real alternative. That answers the reasonable objection that communes are just privileged escape pods, which gets double dipped with the objection that psychedelics are also privileged escape pods (or, alternatively, as some say, "a vacation for the poor").⟦/author⟧

[P6] How accurate are the fears about the future? I don't know exactly. Maybe AI will improve the lot of humanity enormously. Maybe millions of people will lose their jobs to AI. Maybe AI will become the administrative layer between us and nearly everything. Maybe all of these things will happen before Chantress Seba finishes going through all the applications to her commune. 😂

[P7] Ok, so on a personal level, it makes more sense to me to focus on cultivating more human independence from the technosphere, all the machines and systems modern life runs on, than to wait for certainty. ⟦author⟧It's better to have community and find out I didn't need a way out of the technosphere than to need one after spending ten years optimizing my social media feed, right?⟦/author⟧

[P8] What connections to the technosphere do you keep, and which do you limit, when you're designing a community? ⟦author⟧This is also one area where many modern folk will balk. Let the community decide if I can use my social media, or ChatGPT? Are you kidding?⟦/author⟧ I get it. ⟦author⟧I really do. You're not a child anymore. But on the other hand, these technologies don't just affect you, they affect how you relate to the community as well.⟦/author⟧

[P9] ⟦author⟧[caption] New friends are silver, but old friends are gold⟦/author⟧

[P10] Medical care, banking, phones, vehicles, schools, supply chains, government ID, internet and energy are each a separate decision. If you opt out of them completely, it can cause needless suffering. If you depend on them completely, the outside system can reach directly into every essential function.

[P11] The Amish, over generations, have made community decisions about where and if they want to use a piece of technology based on how it affects their community. Different Amish communities make different decisions. Sometimes they use it at work but restrict it at home. Sometimes they share it instead of everyone owning one. Sometimes they hold off on using it until they know more about its social effects. Whatever else you think of the Amish, they at least understand that adopting a technology is a decision for the group to make, rather than something that gets downloaded onto your device while you sleep.

[P12] ⟦author⟧Medical care is one of the toughest dependencies to work out.⟦/author⟧ A community may prefer natural care, but what happens when someone in the community needs trauma surgery, antibiotics, dentistry, emergency obstetric care, or any number of specialist services? ⟦author⟧The community should think through how members are going to have access to care.⟦/author⟧ What kind of care is going to be guaranteed to community members by the community using common resources? What things might require outside dollars or some form of insurance? ⟦author⟧And how will the community deal with people who choose to accept (or not accept) different levels of medical care?⟦/author⟧

[P13] ⟦author⟧No member should have to prove their ideological loyalty when they're scared or sick. There should be pre-existing agreements in place so that their choices will be protected, both for catastrophic illness and end of life care. Medical privacy should be discussed, but may not be guaranteed, since some medical conditions are contagious.⟦/author⟧

[P14] At the same time, having social connections is measurably good for a person's health. The Surgeon General's advisory notes an increased risk for a range of health outcomes among people who are socially isolated. Community's not a panacea, but it can make a difference, day to day, in the conditions people get sick, heal, age, and die in.
