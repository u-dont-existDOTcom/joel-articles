Dissolution and Exit

It’s something the founders of any institution probably don’t want to talk about while they’re naming it and looking forward to its harvests, but it needs to be talked about.

If the commune/intentional community/co-op dissolves, what happens to the land, any outside debts, any other property (buildings, equipment, stores, funds, etc.)? What are the rights of each person at this time? Who is responsible for ensuring that everyone has a place to live and food to eat? Who takes responsibility for making sure people get their meds and/or other care they need? Who makes sure the kids are taken care of and continue to go to school? Who takes responsibility for bridging gaps in food, housing, care, external income/benefits, etc., if applicable? Who takes responsibility for transportation, for record-keeping, for helping people stay in touch with family, for the animals, for ensuring obligations to the land are met, for other claims on the group’s time/attention/etc., if applicable?

Think of it like a living will, and assign a bridge/successor for each function in advance. The institution may not be salvageable, but people’s lives need to go on.

It’s also a way for a group to end without destroying all the friendships involved, or without people losing their dignity.

Institutions and the relationships that make them up don’t have to die together. American Commune recently ran an article about two sisters who grew up on The Farm. One of them recently became ill, and even though the economic institution they were born into had changed dramatically over the years, former members who hadn’t seen her in years still rallied around her.

The Bruderhof still impacts my father’s life, the foundation he started, the movie he made, and the places he’s brought me to, and me, in ways I’m only starting to comprehend.

Even if the legal entity dies, the rest of it doesn’t have to.
