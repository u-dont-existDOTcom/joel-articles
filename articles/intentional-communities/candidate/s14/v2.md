# Dissolution and Exit

[image 18](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!9Q3c!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fddd2a051-9ac5-4630-868b-5a37226707f9_1536x1024.png)

Dissolution is something founders probably don’t want to talk about while they’re picking a name for their community and looking forward to its first harvest. Who would? But that’s exactly when it needs to be talked about.

If the commune dissolves, what happens to the land and any outside debts? What about its buildings, equipment, common stores or funds? What happens to each member’s agreed rights? More immediately, who is responsible for ensuring that everyone has a place to live and food to eat? Who takes responsibility for making sure people get their medications and the other care they need? Who keeps childcare going and makes sure the kids continue to go to school? Who takes responsibility for bridging gaps in food, housing, care, external income/benefits, etc., if applicable? Who takes responsibility for transportation, for the records, for family contact, for the animals, for ensuring obligations to the land are met, and for any unresolved claims? That’s a lot of loose ends!

Think of it like a living will, and assign a bridge/successor for each critical function in advance, so basic life doesn’t depend on reviving the commune. The institution may not be salvageable, but who’s making breakfast tomorrow?

Dissolution terms are also a way for a group to end without turning every friendship into evidence. Who wants that? People may leave hurt, but they keep the transition support they agreed on, and their dignity. And they can tell a coherent story of what happened.

Institutions and the relationships that make them up don’t have to die at the same time. What a relief! American Commune follows two sisters who grew up on The Farm. When one of them later became ill, former members who hadn’t seen her in years reached out with support. The communal economy they were born into had changed dramatically, but the bonds hadn’t vanished.

My father carried the Bruderhof with him long after he left it. It shaped the foundation he started, the film he made, the places he took me, and the questions I’m still asking.

Even if the legal entity dies, some of what people learned to give one another can go on. That gives me hope.
