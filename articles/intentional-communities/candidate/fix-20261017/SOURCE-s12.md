# Source check s12: the "new study" paragraph against its write-up

I checked the paragraph against the write-up its link points to: "Social freedom shapes whose version of reality you adopt, new research suggests" on PsyPost, dated October 15, 2026. It covers a paper in Social Psychological and Personality Science titled "Choosing Our Reality: Relational Mobility Shapes When and With Whom People Share Reality" (Elnakouri, Finkel, Scholer). I read only the write-up, not the paper, and no other file. The web-fetch tool returns a processed version of the page, so I ran a second pass that confirmed every quote below appears word for word in the article (curly apostrophes and italics aside). Every quote is under 15 words.

## 1. The claims, one by one

"A new study found": SUPPORTED. The write-up is dated two days before today (October 17, 2026) and opens "New research provides evidence that..." Its verbs are a notch softer than "found" (the headline says "suggests"), but "found" is ordinary usage for a study's result.

"the same goes for how we see the world" (what the study is about): SUPPORTED. The write-up says the environment "influences whose version of the world we adopt as our own", and the researcher says cultures "shape how we see the world, but also who we look to". Whether "the same" fits the career-market sentence is section 2.

"Where relationships are easy to make and break": SUPPORTED. The write-up's own wording is "relationships are easily formed and broken", and it describes high relational mobility as a setting where "people can easily choose new ties or walk away from current ones". In the student studies and the experiment this was how mobile people felt their social world was. The write-up says a measure of how free participants themselves felt to leave relationships "showed no such pattern". The paragraph says "where", not "who", so it fits.

"people line up their view of reality with whoever helps them reach their goals": SUPPORTED. It is close to the write-up's first sentence: "align their worldviews more selectively with people who help them achieve their goals". This is the weaker of the paragraph's two halves (section 3).

"and feel less on the same page with whoever doesn't": SUPPORTED. "partly by feeling less in sync with those who don't". In the experiment, high-mobility participants "reported less shared reality with their less helpful career contacts" (3.69 against 4.04 on a 7-point scale).

"Kind of a 'no, duh' study, but still": this is the author's own reaction, not a claim about what was found, so I did not rate it. The write-up never calls the result obvious or surprising. The researcher does call one part "interesting": that people "seem to primarily distance themselves from those who do not support their goal".

Nothing in the paragraph is missing from the write-up, and nothing is overstated outright.

## 2. Does "found the same goes for how we see the world" misread the study?

No. It is the author's bridge from the career-market sentence. The study does not build that bridge itself, but nothing it reports contradicts it.

The study tests relational mobility, not the career market. The write-up names careers outright in the 590-person experiment, which asked each person to name "one who was helpful for their career goals" and one who was not. Elsewhere the helpful people are "friends, family members, or colleagues who help them achieve specific personal or professional goals", so the finding is about goals in general, with career as one case.

"How we see the world" is the write-up's own phrase, but what was measured is how much people felt shared reality with particular people (in the 39-country data, "they rated how much they felt the person truly understood them"), not whether anyone's beliefs changed. The paragraph's own words "feel less on the same page" match the measure.

"The same" fits the researcher's summary that mobility "pushes people to invest more in some relationships, yes, but also less in others", which is close to "all the little energy we've got".

A reader could take the transition to mean the study showed the career market bends people's worldviews. It did not test that. The next sentence heads this off by naming the real condition, so I would leave the transition as it is.

## 3. Is anything missing that makes the paragraph wrong?

No. One thing is worth knowing, but its absence leaves the paragraph shorter, not wrong: the two halves are not equally strong.

The page's description line says the shift happens "mostly by pulling people's minds away from contacts who don't support their goals". In the experiment, "Shared reality with helpful contacts did not differ between conditions". In the student network studies, "students who saw their social world as highly mobile showed a bigger gap" in three of four analyses, but in only one did the gap come from lower shared reality with less helpful people, and in the others "the shifts with helpful and unhelpful people were not statistically significant on their own". The closer-to-helpers half shows up on its own in one analysis of the 39-country data: "the gap arose because people reported more shared reality with supportive partners".

The paragraph still describes the gap between helpers and non-helpers correctly. That gap is what the write-up reports in both analyses of the 39-country data, in three of four network analyses, and in the experiment's drop for unhelpful contacts, and the paragraph follows the write-up's own first sentence.

The other limits the write-up lists (an 86 percent women Facebook sample in study 1, student samples at a single university, a one-acquaintance experiment, typical social-psychology effect sizes, three correlational datasets) bear on how far to generalize "people". They do not make the paragraph wrong.

If a tightening is ever wanted, the smallest one is "and" becoming "mostly by": "...line up their view of reality with whoever helps them reach their goals, mostly by feeling less on the same page with whoever doesn't." It is not needed for accuracy.

VERDICT: OK
