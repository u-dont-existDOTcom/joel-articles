# Founderism: The Four Questions

Every community starts because somebody took the initiative. Somebody found the land, wrote the first documents, taught the method, brought in the first members, or kept the whole thing going after everybody else lost interest.

That person ends up with real influence, and pretending they don't just makes that influence harder to look at.

I know this tension firsthand, because a lot of the methods in this article are mine. I wrote the materials. So it would be easy for me to become the guy who decides whether everyone else “understood” them correctly. Writing in anti-authoritarian language doesn't keep an author from enjoying being the author.

A few things help. One is to judge the method by results people can actually see. Are the members flourishing, and are they safe? Can conflicts get resolved? Can people disagree, leave, and later tell a different story about it? Are the kids doing well once they grow up? Is the community still materially viable? Can it correct a prediction that turned out wrong? Can it replace the founder? And if it's meant to spread, do new projects actually start up and last? How long a community has lasted, how full it is, whether it reaches consensus and how many members stay aren't enough on their own. A group can keep its members because people love it, or because leaving costs too much. And don't judge it by how closely people stick to the founder's own interpretation.

Let people run parallel versions. Fork the method. If a community changes Hearthwork and gets better results, they've taught me something. If every change still has to be approved by the founder, those versions aren't really parallel.

Here are the four questions:

Can the community respectfully override the founder? Can it replace the founder’s role? Can it revise the founder’s vision? Can it survive the founder’s absence?

Then follow the practical power. Whose name is on the deed? Who controls the housing, the food stores, the vehicles and tools, and any bank account outside the community? Who keeps the records, decides admissions, picks the reviewers, decides who gets access to the children or to medicine, and who can make a complaint disappear? The org chart can call itself horizontal, and meanwhile the keys, the passwords, the titles and the reputation all lead back to one person.

An override is only real if it can actually work against the person being challenged. If the founder picks the reviewer, holds the records, owns the land, and can kick out whoever complained, the community can't really override the founder, no matter how many circles it holds.

Ask these questions about any community, teacher or method. And ask them about me, if people end up living on land I buy.

Also watch for the day-to-day signs: the founder getting more and more central over time, disagreement being treated as failure, every bad result getting blamed on poor implementation, insider knowledge growing while public accountability shrinks, nobody ever measuring outcomes, and former members turning morally contaminated the moment they leave.

None of these proves abuse by itself. When several show up together, though, they deserve attention, before the founder explains why this situation is spiritually unique.

## How My Own Model Could Become a Cult

[image 16]

I could totally start a cult. Trust me.

Every practice in this article can be turned against the people using it.

“Nothing suppressed, everything processed” can turn into compulsory disclosure. I’ve seen that risk in communities that use ZEGG Forum. A group can start out inviting honesty and end up treating privacy as resistance.

Peer counseling can turn into surveillance that happens in a warm tone. Medicine prerequisites can create gatekeepers who have power over people when they're at their most open. And “willing to be seen” can become a demand that everybody reveal whatever the group wants to know.

The line is simple. These principles are commitments I choose for my own practice, and they don't give the community any power over me.

“Nothing suppressed” is about my relationship with my own material. It doesn't give anyone the right to pull that material out of me. Members can decline to share, and they don't have to come up with a therapeutic explanation for saying no.

Medicine is optional. Choosing not to take it can't reduce anyone's membership rights. No single person or permanent clique controls who gets admitted. And a disagreement gets answered like a disagreement.

“Your resistance is your wound talking” is one of the most efficient sentences ever invented for getting out of an argument. When a community starts diagnosing dissent instead of answering it, leave before they schedule a group process about your leaving.

There's also a floor that the majority can't vote away: bodily safety, enough food and sleep, independent medical care, private thought, contact with people outside the community, a way to report harm without having to ask the accused person's friends for permission, and a way to leave that actually works. Taking these away from someone doesn't become consensual just because the majority voted for it.
