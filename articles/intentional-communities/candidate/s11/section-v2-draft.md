# Founderism: The Four Questions

Every community got started because somebody took the initiative. Somebody had to find the land, write the first documents, teach the method, recruit the first members, or keep things going when everybody else lost interest.

That person ends up with real influence, and pretending they don't just makes that influence harder to look at.

I know this tension firsthand, because a lot of the methods in this article are mine. I wrote the materials. So it would be easy for me to become the guy who decides whether everyone else “understood” them correctly. Writing in anti-authoritarian language doesn't keep an author from enjoying being the author.

A few things help. One is to judge the method by results people can actually see. Are the members flourishing, and are they safe? Can conflicts get resolved? Can people disagree, leave, and later tell a different story about it? Are the kids doing well once they grow up? Is the community still materially viable? Can it correct a prediction that turned out wrong? Can it replace the founder? And if it's meant to spread, do new projects actually start up and last? How long a community has lasted, how full it is, whether it reaches consensus and how many members stay aren't enough on their own. A group can keep its members because people love it, or because leaving costs too much. And don't judge it by how closely people stick to the founder's own interpretation.

Let people run their own parallel versions, and fork the method. If a community changes Hearthwork and gets better results, they've taught me something. But if every change still needs the founder's approval, it's not really parallel, is it?

Here are the four questions:

Can the community override the founder, respectfully? Can it replace the founder in their role? Can it change the founder’s vision? And can it survive without the founder?

Then look at who controls the practical stuff. Whose name is on the deed? Who's in charge of the housing and the food stores, the vehicles and tools, and any bank account outside the community? Who keeps the records? Who decides who gets admitted, and who picks the reviewers? Who decides who gets access to the children, or to medicine? And who can make a complaint disappear? The org chart can say it's horizontal while the keys, the passwords, the titles and the reputation all lead back to one person.

An override is only real if it can actually work against the person being challenged. If the founder picks the reviewer, holds the records, owns the land, and can kick out whoever complained, the community can't really override the founder, no matter how many circles it holds.

Ask these questions about any community, teacher or method. And ask them about me, if people end up living on land I buy.

Also watch for the day-to-day signs. Is the founder getting more central over time? Does disagreeing get treated as failing? Does every bad result get blamed on poor implementation? Is insider knowledge growing while public accountability shrinks? Does anybody measure outcomes? And do former members become morally contaminated the moment they leave?

None of these proves abuse by itself. But if several show up together, pay attention, before the founder gets around to explaining why this situation is spiritually unique.

## How My Own Model Could Become a Cult

[image 16]

I could totally start a cult. Trust me.

Every practice in this article can be turned against the people using it.

“Nothing suppressed, everything processed” can turn into compulsory disclosure. I’ve seen that risk in communities that use ZEGG Forum. A group can start out inviting honesty and end up treating privacy as resistance.

Peer counseling can turn into surveillance, just with a warm tone of voice. Prerequisites for medicine can put gatekeepers in charge of people right when they're at their most open. And being “willing to be seen” can turn into a demand that everybody reveal whatever the group wants to know.

Here's where I draw the line. These principles are commitments I choose for my own practice, and they don't give the community any power over me.

“Nothing suppressed” is about me and my own material. It doesn't give anyone the right to pull anything out of me. Any member can choose not to share, and they don't need to come up with a therapeutic reason for it.

Medicine is optional, and not taking it can't cost anyone their rights as a member. No single person or permanent clique gets to control who's admitted. And if someone disagrees, you answer what they're actually saying.

“Your resistance is your wound talking” is about the most efficient sentence ever invented for getting out of an argument. When a community starts diagnosing dissent instead of answering it, leave. Better yet, leave before it gets to the point where they schedule a group process about your leaving.

There's also a floor the majority can't vote away. Everybody gets bodily safety, enough food and sleep, independent medical care, and their own private thoughts. They get to stay in contact with people outside the community. They need a way to report harm without asking the accused person's friends for permission, and a way to leave that actually works. If the majority votes to take any of that away, it still isn't consensual.
