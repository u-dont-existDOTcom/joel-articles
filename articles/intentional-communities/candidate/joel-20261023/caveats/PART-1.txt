## The New Age May Dawn Suddenly
I just put a movie my father made in 1988 up on the Internet for the first time: Where’s Utopia? It was the result of years of work, traveling to all kinds of communes and communal experiments around the country, trying to answer a question that for him was primary: How do we live together without recreating the same loneliness, domination, and stupidity we were supposedly escaping?
He had already spent 17 years in the Bruderhof, a Christian community that doesn’t use money internally. So when I talk later on about evolving past the use of internal money, I’m not proposing something nobody has ever lived. They also accepted my dad as a Jewish atheist, making him a remarkably long-running doctrinal exception. In meditation there, he came to his own understanding that “God is the Universe, and the Universe is Love.”
[an image]
He might have stayed, but the U.S. antiwar movement during Vietnam needed somebody who knew how to set up printing presses. He eventually founded the Aquarian Research Foundation—not a commune, but a nonprofit devoted to researching how the planet could have a positive future and publishing what it found. His conclusion was simple and enormous: human beings need small tribal communities again, because Civilization Is A Hoax, as I blogged about at the beginning of the Covid plandemic.
That’s where the escuelita in my title comes in. Escuelita means “little school.” I’m borrowing the word from the Zapatistas in Mexico, whose Escuelita brought outsiders into their communities for a while to learn autonomy by living it. In the same way, people would live in the healing communities I’m proposing, to heal and to learn the relational and practical methods, so that some of them could eventually leave and set up another one somewhere else. Otherwise, we’ve built a lovely refuge for whoever got there first.

I spent much of my childhood being carried through Twin Oaks, Acorn, East Wind, Padanaram, Zendik, and many other communities. I was usually just a visitor’s kid, close enough to see how people lived but never quite part of the tribe. At the time that meant a lot of boredom, with some adventures mixed in, but those years gave me a great sample to compare against.
From 1999 to 2001, my family actually lived with the Love Israel family in Washington while escaping Y2K. In case you aren’t familiar with Y2K, it was the Year 2000 (represented as year 00) computer bug which some folks like my dad especially, felt might be the end of civilization We lived in a yurt. At Love Israel, I had my dream of picking all kinds of wild berries in nature come true. I was also lonely, bored, irritated by the cult programming, and missing my friends in Philadelphia.
I wanted to befriend the children there, but they treated me as an outsider. My mom never liked the place. The leader was also named Love Israel, and to me he seemed like the fakest person I’d ever met, complete with the expected God complex.
We spent a lot of time on our knees in the community garden growing food they could sell in town. The money was opaque and seemed to travel mostly upward toward Love. Meanwhile everybody sang, “Here in the garden, there’s nothing left to do-ooo-oo, but eat, sleep, and keep clean!” Again and again. 🌱
My father eventually gave the community his airplane. They sold it instead of using it. He felt tricked, began exposing what was happening, and then we were asked to leave.
The East Wind commune was almost the opposite way. There were maybe eighty people when I was there, enough to have a variety of personalities, small enough to actually make friends. I was happy there. I especially liked showering in a communal shower with both sexes present. I wonder if that still goes on at East Wind? That’s the sort of thing that doesn’t make it into the history books, and which takes some balls (no pun intended), considering how the wider society counts this as potentially damaging to children’s psyches, despite it being the way we’ve lived for thousands of years prior.
Even at East Wind, though, there was some political power usurpation under the surface. They had a nut butter factory that brought in a lot of their income, which seemed to make the woman running it very hard to question. Otherwise, I thought the community had a great social life, a nice way of distributing labor, and no obvious guru.
There were like eight people at Acorn when I was there, and no kids. I couldn’t tell whether it was a commune I might join or a group of people who were already friends, and who happened to live on some really beautiful land and be good at building cool stuff.
Twin Oaks was much bigger, like two or three hundred people at the time, and almost institutional. It was friendly and interesting, but I didn’t feel like I was in a tribe there. It was more like a tiny hippie town, but with a highly structured political system that didn’t seem intuitive for hippies.
Looking back, almost none of these communities had a communal therapy practice. People brought the usual human pain and suffering with them, and it all got translated into politics.
Which meant weekly meetings, run according to Robert’s Rules of Order, with enormous agendas, where most of the things you needed to talk about couldn’t be talked about, and you generally felt like you’d rather be sleeping. (Some people, including me, solved this problem by just going to sleep in the meeting and openly snoring.)
Zendik went in the other direction. They held a brutal community meeting that I’m still recovering from 35 years later. 🤣 Five-year-old me was put in the center and told I was bad because I hadn’t volunteered to help adults with whatever jobs they were doing. Apparently kindergarten had already failed to teach me labor consciousness.
But what I gathered from a bird’s eye view is that the secular communities often thought they could make everyone feel loved and respected by adopting optimal democratic procedures. The spiritual communities went deeper in search of real purpose, belonging, and divine connection, but often put one person in charge of everybody’s inner life. Most of the my big little article here is me attempting to describe the missing bridge between those.

## Communes Are Hot Again 🔥
Suddenly, the longing to escape loneliness which inspired my father’s movie, is everywhere again. Back in 2023, ironically, just the year after Covid lockdowns ended, the U.S. Surgeon General even put out an advisory, saying roughly half of American adults experience loneliness, and it treated social connection as a public-health issue.
Whether you want to call that loneliness an official “epidemic” or not, it’s obvious that a lot of people can’t tolerate modern life in isolation anymore (the UN’s rules for prisoners literally ban more than 15 days of it). Folks may disagree about politics, food, God, sex, or whether shoes are oppressive, but they agree that something about the current arrangement is starving them.
What’s starving them is anomie. We evolved to live in small groups where everybody knows everybody. In a large society the shared norms and close ties that held those groups together fall apart, and that’s how large societies end up run by psychopaths. (Yes, I’m always going on about this.)
Check out the Google Trends for “ecovillage” and “intentional community”. After years of decline, interest climbed sharply in the mid-2020s.
[an image]
Apart from the Covid lockdowns just prior, AI became serious around this time, and it started changing people’s jobs and relationships, and how they see the future. Suddenly, forming a village sounded like a good backup plan, not just a hippie utopian whim.
It’s poppin’ on Instagram too! 🔥 Chantress Seba—my favorite light-language love influencer—posted an application for people interested in starting a commune.  I applied, although I don’t think she’s coming to Africa, where I’m at. When I saw the post, many thousands of people appeared to be interested. That was encouraging, but imagine Chantress Seba the next morning opening her inbox  to a zillion excited applications to co-found her dream village. 😅
Later, when another influencer with a big following floated the commune idea, the comment section started to become a recruitment board, where people were apparently forming joint operations together right in there.
It was chaotic, but sincere, and it revealed to me how people are so ready for this that they’ll literally jump into it from one video.
## The Freeloader Problem
Some of the commenters were skeptics tho. The oldest objection showed up immediately, restating the collective action problem: anarcho-communalism sounds great on paper, but when property becomes “owned by everyone, cared for by nobody,” the practicality comes into question.
[an image]
I’d say the comment above is more true of the big socialism experiments than small communes, but I still take it seriously, because some people really would rather let somebody else take responsibility. Sometimes you see the same three people whoi end up doing all the maintenance, while everybody else talks about freedom while lounging around in their hammocks. And then unofficial leaders start gaining power just based on their indispensibility.
Communal ownership entails folks taking on visible and named stewardship duties. It requires knowing what to do if someone is leeching off the group and how to deal with it. It requires developing healthier forms of relationships. No, taking away the idea of a boss does not make everyone less passive, selfish, thieving, nor suddenly unlearning learned helplessness.

## Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways
Most communes of my acquaintance traded inner freedom of the heart (spirituality) for outer freedom (secular, nondogmatic, political). The two pendulum ends of this trade-off mirror each other. I believe we can combine the best of both, but first, let’s see how each fails individually.
## Outer-freedom-only models
The secular communes (and all the manuals I’ve seen on building them) had a solution for how to distribute property or labor in a way folks could agree on, but when the deep waters of jealousy or childhood hurt or humiliation or attraction or resentment or grief came up, the group was without a common method for dealing with it.
Eventually, when people are together long enough, politics will become a way of carrying emotional material that people can’t name. There will be a fight about a schedule or committee or noise or food or some policy or other, but it won’t be about that. There’s no shared way to talk about the hurt itself, but the meeting agenda is the one place the group lets you push for something and win, so the angry communard goes there, trying to get some justice.
## Spiritual dogma models attempting inner freedom
Many people have already realized that they are more interested in spiritual (or emotional) liberation than political liberation, so they tend to congregate in communities built for that. But because these communes focus so much on the inner work that the founder believes in, it can often look like some kind of prison-monastery cult, where you feel free inside as long as you don’t question anything.
The Farm, in Tennessee, at its largest about 1,500 people, had many beautiful values. But in the early days, the community’s spirituality and social life followed the founder, Stephen Gaskin. From what former members have shared, the community’s doctrine ran deep into members’ intimate decisions. This is how it often goes: folks talk about oneness with the universe, while one man defines what the universe is.
## We Want Freedom Inside & Out!
Let’s face it: food, housing, and logistics are not enough reason to bind whole lives together. That’s more like a situationship. A community needs a shared sacred inner practice that gives the difficulty of our life together a deeper meaning. We help each other heal and choose love over fear. And we turn outward with a purpose, using the escuelita model to bring our healing to the world.
I dream of a way to work in depth that isn’t run by a guru, and a way of being in a community with distributed authority. A place where people who’ve done the healing first can join without pretending they’re fully finished, and that also has peer practice and enough play that it doesn’t become a permanent repair shop.
The motto would be: “Nothing suppressed, everything processed.” Each of us guides our own compatible practice, and our practices weave the community into the quilt that keeps us all warm and safe. Whatever answers we find stay fluid and organic.
Speaking of dynamically weaving ourselves together, though, a community does need some stable agreements that they’re not tweaking and revising every time somebody has a revelation in breathwork. But these agreements need to stay open to lived experience.
When agreements get frozen, religious communities tend to call the frozen version doctrine. Secular communities, on the other hand, often call it “the process,” which can become just as sacred.
To give an example of striking the balance: My contemplative practice is grounded in the early Buddhist suttas, with a critical reading. Most are treasure, while some are obviously late additions (sometimes to the point of real absurdity). A living community should relate to its own teachings in that same critical manner while staying devoted to following what they’ve decided on so far.
## The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together
[an image]
Communities tend to focus on the aspect of their project that was most interesting to the original founders, for example political types getting super into writing by-laws, or spiritual types just meditating all the time, or therapists processing each other, or people who like psychedelics discovering they’re all God together and never getting to the point of deciding who will put their name on the deed.
I’ve broken the model I propose down into the 4 parts shown in the above diagram. Note, Pl/ork means play + work. Calling therapy “inner work” forgets the inner child, who also wants to play. (Forgive the neologism. I hope pl/ork isn’t too corny. 🌽): 1) inner pl/ork (reparenting), 2) relational pl/ork (peer counseling especially), 3) medicine pl/ork (like loveyhuasca or iboga), and 4) the practical logistical foundation which is most of this article, since I have guides separately on the other parts. Obviously the details are complicated, and jealousy, children, money, land, God, and iboga don’t fit neatly into four boxes, but let’s go with it.
## 1. Inner pl/ork
Probably the most obvious type of pl/ork is reparenting. Here’s an example: you teach your inner child geography, while s/he teaches you the world is a cat!
[an image]
Inner-child reparenting means learning to identify as the best holistic inner adult, which is comprised of the three inner adult roles: Nurturer, Protector, and Guide. The newly developed adult reparents their newly recognized inner child. The child receives all that love, protection, and guidance that you give it, and as a result, your little one gains more freedom to remain alive inside you, which brings back the joy of life that many adults have forgotten about.
And this reparenting journey is built on somatic regulation first. I.e., listening to the body’s feelings before making a decision with the mind. And that actually symbolizes the adult/child relationship to me in some way as well.
