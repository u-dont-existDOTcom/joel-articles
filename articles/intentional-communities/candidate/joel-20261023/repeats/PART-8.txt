## Jealousy Can Capture Monogamy or Free Love
Any community that lasts will eventually be reorganized by attraction and pair-bonding. When two exes live in the same city, they can move to different sides of the city. When they’re part of a thirty-person commune, they may still be in the same barn the next morning, working in the same team, attending the same meetings, sharing childcare and the same friends. A couple’s jealousy or breakup becomes a problem for the entire community, because it will change the loyalties, the sleeping arrangements, the labor, the kids, the alliances, and sometimes who’s leaving.
The discussion on this topic generally goes straight to “should people be monogamous or poly?” But as I discuss in my Romance Guide, this is one level too late in the conversation. The community problem is that, in close quarters, jealousy gets magnified, and jealousy can capture either system.
Rigid monogamy can turn fear and possession into a moral law. If people are doing free love, but aren’t doing the inner pl/ork, they can just end up with a larger spreadsheet to distribute their fear over. It’s not like calling something by a certain name dissolves the childhood panic, the comparison, the terror of abandonment, or the desire to control another person.
I don’t think it should be up to the community to decide for everyone what form relationships will take. People can make different agreements with each other, in pairs, threes, fours, whatever. They can change those agreements as they wish. And they can ask for support when those agreements bring up old material.
Whatever forms people choose, I do think that housing, security for kids, membership and access to common necessities should not be contingent on a person staying in some agreement with the people they’re romantically involved with.
ZEGG, a community near Berlin, and Tamera, a ZEGG offshoot in Portugal, have spent decades experimenting with relationships inside community. Their answers are not a set of commandments that everyone must follow. But they are an example of how a community can acknowledge the erotic aspects of life.
The non-negotiable for me is that jealousy not be allowed to become the hidden governor. This doesn’t mean it can’t come up. It needs to be able to be talked about and felt in the body. It needs to be explored without humiliation. And it needs to be let go of gradually, and not allowed to turn into punishment, or into community rules.
There are some ways the group can help, and basic boundaries the group can enforce, without deciding what type of relationships people have. Even so, there will still be disagreements about this. That’s more realistic than saying it’s all been resolved.
## The Grown Children Get the Final Review
Couples who live together, share the same culture and even the same bed, still disagree about screens, vaccines, beans (vs meat), schooling, religion, discipline and medical care. And I mean, that’s just two people... Now think of twenty adults, all with different childhoods and value systems.
The community has to share enough principles about raising kids to offer them coherence, and yet still leave parents enough room to contribute their individual humanity, insights, gifts, and so on.
My own childhood broke many mainstream rules about what children should see and do. There’s no space to get into all of it here, but I’m grateful that my mother nursed me until I was five and let me sleep in their bed until 11. I’m grateful my parents didn’t vaccinate me, and grateful for nearly all of the unusual world they gave me, which has given me endless stories to tell.

Echo chambers aren’t ideal either, but we need enough agreement to make life together work.

## Communal Parenting Adds On
One of the biggest reasons I think community matters for kids is Jean Liedloff’s The Continuum Concept. Liedloff was writing about what she learned while living with the Ye’kuana tribe in the Venezuelan rainforest. Some say she idealized what she saw, but I still find it powerful: the babies are carried by the adults (and not just by one exhausted parent) all the time, so they participate in everything adults do from the start. Instead of age-matched cohorts like we have in our schools, kids are with others of all ages. Some modern parents try to adopt some of the Ye’kuana child-rearing practices, but the full stack requires the actual communal support they had.
Experiments where kids sleep and live apart from their parents deserve special caution. Research on Israeli kibbutzim found that the practice of kids sleeping communally eventually ended due to developmental findings, parents’ preferences and social change. Kids can benefit from high-quality group care and multiple loving adults in their lives, but replacing a secure attachment to their parents is a different matter.
Tamera’s Children’s Place has semi-repeated some of this old Kibbutz philosophy, taking children away from biological parents, because their founders’ ideology said that children are traumatized by the nuclear family and its unstable romantic underpinnings. On the other hand, their actual practice did allow parents (including fathers) to sometimes stay with their children in Children’s Place, so they weren’t as dogmatic as I originally thought.
## Children Need Freedom Inside Real Protection
[an image]
My own bias is still toward a system where kids are not solely taken care of by their parents, while still honoring the parent-child bond. If the father is unknown, the whole community can do the fathering, but the biological mom’s role should always be honored, since that attachment is formed already from years of nursing. Kids do need their own territory, places where they can create their own society, invent games and work out ordinary disputes, without always being under the management of adults.
I got a taste of that at a Unitarian youth camp, and it’s still one of the freest memories I have.
This could be badly misunderstood, so the safeguards need to be explicit. The adults still have to pay attention to what goes on in the children’s territory. Older kids can’t be allowed to boss the younger ones around there. And keeping secrets shouldn’t be part of how independence manifests.
I feel very much saddened by the way that adults are seen as (and actually often are) so dangerous for children in the West, especially when they are fond of each other. It’s good for kids to have a whole bunch of grownups who know them and enjoy them, who would notice when something changes, and who would act when something’s wrong. When adults panic like that, kids can lose all those grownups without the panic actually preventing abuse. It’s amazing when you travel the world and you see how sick US culture is especially… all over Africa and parts of Asia, I’ve seen parents let their kids run around naked in public with no qualms or concerns. That’s real freedom!
So here’s how I see this protection being ideally structured:
- Give children real agency. When kids say no to an adult who wants to be alone with them or touch them, that’s a hard line that can’t be crossed. Apart from that, obedience should not be demanded from kids outside of legitimate safety needs.
- Create a no-secrets rule. No grownup has a right to ask a kid to keep a secret from their mom or another trusted adult, whether it’s about a gift, a game, a touch, a conversation or a relationship. (A surprise has an end date. But secrets meant to isolate a kid aren’t allowed.)
- Teach Honesty. Protecting children is the duty of adults, but it requires children to help also. “The Boy Who Cried Wolf” is a didactic children’s story which continues to be useful generation after generation. The Buddha even taught his son Rahula honesty as the most important first lesson. I agree. We must teach children truthfulness before there’s a crisis. “Make believe” must be labeled as such.
When adults lie to children (like “Santa is real!” etc), that teaches them to misunderstand the difference between reality and make-believe. Make sure kids understand what they say will be taken seriously if they are known to be honest. Those who are known to lie a lot are especially targeted by abusers who know their accusations won’t be believed. Don’t let the teaching turn into interrogation or punishment when they’re not sure. A scared or confused kid may get mixed up telling what happened and still need protection.
- Hear the child even when the accused is someone people love. Communities fail here, because the adult may be charismatic, useful, rich, spiritually important, or everybody’s friend. Decide on the response process ahead of time, before anyone’s face is attached to it. Keep everyone at risk safe first, right away, then make sure the accusation is investigated competently and fairly. Nobody’s status buys silence.
- Make sure kids have relationships and a reporting route outside the community. Grandparents, cousins, local friends, teachers and other trusted adults give them perspective on their life, and someone to talk to. State intervention can fail spectacularly, and enclosing kids completely in a community can fail too. Kids need more than one world. In 1984, Vermont authorities raided a religious community in Island Pond and took 112 kids into custody over suspected abuse. A judge turned down the state’s blanket request to hold them, because it couldn’t name any specific case, and the kids went home that same day. There was apparently some physical abuse going on there as a disciplinary practice, but that’s something which varies a lot in every society also.
