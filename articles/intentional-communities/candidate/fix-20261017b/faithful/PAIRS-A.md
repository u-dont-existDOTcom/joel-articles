SECTION: The New Age May Dawn Suddenly

PUBLISHED:

I recently put my father’s 1988 film, Where’s Utopia?, online for the first time. He made it in 1988 after spending years visiting communal experiments around the United States and asking a question that never really left him: how could human beings live together without reproducing the same loneliness, domination, and stupidity we were supposedly escaping?

He had already spent 17 years in the Bruderhof, a Christian community that doesn’t use money internally. He had no personal money there: income went into the common purse and the community provided what members needed. So when I talk later about moving beyond internal money, I’m not proposing something nobody has ever lived. My father spent most of two decades doing it. They also accepted him as a Jewish atheist, making him a remarkably long-running doctrinal exception. In meditation there, he came to his own understanding that “God is the Universe, and the Universe is Love.”

[caption] This was the banner my mom painted that hung in our living room for decades. I will try to locate the actual photo.

He might have stayed, but the U.S. antiwar movement during Vietnam needed somebody who knew how to set up printing presses. He eventually founded the Aquarian Research Foundation—not a commune, but a nonprofit devoted to researching how the planet could have a positive future and publishing what it found. His conclusion was simple and enormous: human beings need small tribal communities again, because Civilization Is A Hoax, as I blogged about at the beginning of the Covid plandemic.

That’s where the **escuelita** in my title comes in. Escuelita means “little school.” I’m borrowing the word from the Zapatistas, whose Escuelita brought outsiders into their communities to learn autonomy by living it. The healing communities I’m proposing should do the same: people live there, heal, learn the relational and practical methods, and some eventually leave to start the next one. Otherwise, we’ve built a lovely refuge for whoever got there first.

That’s how I spent much of my childhood being carried through Twin Oaks, Acorn, East Wind, Padanaram, Zendik, and many other experiments. I was usually the visitor’s kid, close enough to see how people lived but never quite part of the tribe. At the time this mostly meant boredom. In retrospect, it gave me a strangely useful comparison sample.

From 1999 to 2001, my family actually lived with the Love Israel family in Washington while escaping Y2K, that brief historical period when otherwise reasonable adults prepared for civilization to be defeated by a two-digit date. We lived in a yurt. There were wild berries of every kind, and I loved being in nature. I was also lonely, bored, irritated by the cult programming, and missing my friends in Philadelphia.

I wanted to befriend the children there, but they treated me as an outsider. My mom never liked the place. The leader was also named Love Israel, and to me he seemed like the fakest person I’d ever met, complete with the expected God complex.

We spent a lot of time on our knees in the community garden growing food they could sell in town. The money was opaque and seemed to travel mostly upward toward Love. Meanwhile everybody sang, “Here in the garden, there’s nothing left to do but eat sleep and keep clean.” Again and again. 🌱

My father eventually gave the community his airplane. They sold it instead of using it. He felt tricked, began exposing what was happening, and then we were asked to leave.

East Wind felt almost like the opposite social experience. It had roughly eighty people when I visited: large enough to have real variety, small enough that friendship still felt possible. I was happy there. I also loved the mixed-sex communal showers, and I still wonder whether those survived. These are the questions history books neglect.

East Wind still had a quieter power problem. The woman directing the nut-butter factory brought in much of the community’s income, which seemed to make her unusually difficult to question. The group had a warm social life, distributed labor, and no obvious guru, but the factory’s importance still affected who could be challenged.

Acorn had something like eight people when I visited. There were no children there. I couldn’t tell whether it was a commune I might join or a group of friends who happened to own beautiful land and build lovely things together. Joining an established group of eight friends felt less like applying to a community than asking whether their group chat had an opening.

Twin Oaks was much larger—perhaps two or three hundred people at the time—and felt almost institutional. It was friendly and interesting, but I didn’t feel a tribe. It felt more like a tiny hippie town with an unusually complicated labor-credit system.

Across nearly all of these places, the same absence kept appearing: no communal therapy practice. Human pain still entered the group, of course. It simply got translated into politics.

I remember weekly meetings run under Robert’s Rules of Order, with enormous agendas, little room for what anyone actually needed to discuss, and the persistent thought, “I’d rather be sleeping.” Sometimes people solved that conflict by snoring through the meeting.

Zendik went in the other direction. They held a brutal community meeting that I’m still recovering from 35 years later. 🤣 Five-year-old me was put in the center and told I was bad because I hadn’t volunteered to help adults with whatever jobs they were doing. Apparently kindergarten had already failed to teach me labor consciousness.

The secular communities often buried hurt under procedure. The spiritual communities often put one person in charge of everybody’s inner life. Most of the rest of this article is my attempt to describe the missing third option.

SECTION: Communes Are Hot Again 🔥

PUBLISHED:

The longing that shaped my father’s film is suddenly everywhere again. The U.S. Surgeon General’s 2023 advisory reported that roughly half of American adults experience loneliness and treated social connection as a public-health issue rather than a sentimental extra.

I’m less interested in declaring an official “epidemic” than in the obvious fact underneath it: a lot of people can’t tolerate isolated modern life anymore. They may disagree about politics, food, God, sex, and whether shoes are oppressive, but they agree that something about the current arrangement is starving them.

Look at Google Trends for “ecovillage” and “intentional community.” Interest had been drifting downward for years, then bent upward sharply in the mid-2020s.

I can’t prove AI caused that turn. The timing is still hard to ignore. Around the same period, AI stopped being a tech-news curiosity and began reorganizing people’s jobs, feeds, relationships, and expectations of the future. Suddenly “maybe we should form a village” sounded less like a 1972 leftover and more like a backup plan.

It’s poppin’ on Instagram too! 🔥 Chantress Seba—my favorite light-language love influencer—posted an application for people interested in starting a commune. I applied, although I don’t think she’s coming to Africa. When I saw the post, many thousands of people appeared to be interested. Imagine opening your inbox after casually inviting a million followers to co-found a village.

Another large influencer later floated the commune idea, and the comments began turning into a recruitment board. People were finding prospective members under a post the way they might normally find a used sofa. It was chaotic, sincere, and revealing.

### The Freeloader Problem

Not everybody in the comments was trying to join. The oldest objection arrived almost immediately: maybe most people don’t want the responsibility anarchism requires, and communal property becomes “owned by everyone, cared for by nobody.”

The commune comments became a miniature political-philosophy seminar, as Instagram comments sometimes do.

Although that comment applies more to large socialism experiments than small communes, that objection deserves a serious answer. Some people really would rather let somebody else take responsibility. Shared ownership can blur responsibility until the same three people maintain everything while everybody else discusses freedom. Informal leaders then gain power partly because everyone keeps handing it to them.

Communal ownership therefore needs named stewardship, visible duties, consequences for chronic freeloading, and enough relational capacity to confront the problem before resentment becomes the real government. A community cannot run on the assumption that removing bosses removes passivity, selfishness, theft, or learned helplessness.

The desire to return to a natural way of living together has outrun the knowledge. People know they’re lonely and exhausted. They usually don’t know why so many earlier communities failed, why the same conflicts keep returning, or how quickly beautiful land becomes irrelevant once money, children, jealousy, and ownership enter the picture.

I’m not currently recruiting anyone or founding a community. That makes this easier to write because I’m not trying to get you onto my land. I do plan to buy land, and people may naturally end up living around me. Every warning in this article applies to me too.

SECTION: Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways

PUBLISHED:

The communes I knew tended to fail inwardly in one of two directions.

## The secular failure

This failure is caused when the community distributes ownership and labor but has no shared way to address jealousy, childhood wounds, humiliation, attraction, resentment, or grief. Those things don’t disappear. They acquire agenda items.

The Federation of Egalitarian Communities and other income-sharing groups developed sophisticated systems for labor, money, and decision-making. Yet a meeting procedure can’t make two people stop hating each other. It can only determine whose turn it is to speak about the hatred and whether the motion requires a second.

Eventually politics begins carrying emotional material that nobody knows how to name. People argue about schedules, committees, noise, food, or policy while the real subject sits nearby with its arms crossed.

## The spiritual failure

This is caused when the community recognizes that inner transformation matters, then routes it through a founder, teacher, channel, or special interpreter of reality.

The Farm in Tennessee grew to around 1,500 people and held many beautiful values. Its early spiritual and social life was also deeply shaped by Stephen Gaskin. Accounts from former members describe community doctrine reaching into intimate decisions. A group can talk about universal consciousness while one man’s opinions become unusually difficult to escape.

The two failures mirror each other. Secular groups distribute power but leave the inner life largely private. Spiritual groups address the inner life but concentrate power around whoever defines the path.

The deeper failure is that authority earned in one role can be spent in every other role. A founder may be a gifted teacher. A therapist may genuinely help people. A farmer or business manager may carry much of the economy. The danger begins when the same person can define the problem, control the evidence, choose the remedy, and make disagreement or exit costly.

I want depth without a guru owning it, and distributed authority without pretending that people arrive emotionally finished. That requires practice between peers, plus enough play that the community doesn’t become a permanent repair shop.

SECTION: The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together

PUBLISHED:

Communities tend to solve whichever part most interests the founders. The political people write bylaws. The spiritual people meditate. The therapists process. The psychedelic people discover that everyone is God and forget to decide whose name goes on the deed.

I divide the model into four parts. Yes, four sounds suspiciously tidy for something involving jealousy, children, land, money, God, and iboga. Bear with me. I’ve published detailed guides for the first three—free, with no funnel and no retreat to buy. Most of this article is about the fourth.

Food, housing, and logistics are not enough reason to bind whole lives together. Shared inner practice gives the difficulty meaning: we are not merely maintaining property together, but helping one another heal and become less governed by fear. An outward escuelita and federation purpose adds another reason to keep going—the community exists not only to protect the people already inside it, but to help other people build.

## One principle runs through all of them

Nothing suppressed, everything processed; authority stays with the practitioner; the community provides the container; and the answers remain fluid and organic instead of freezing into doctrine.

These categories leak into one another almost immediately. Jealousy can become governance; control of shared resources can become psychology; medicine can create status; child-rearing can expose values the founders never discussed. I use the boxes to notice what a community has neglected, not because communal life stays inside boxes.

A community does need stable agreements. Nobody wants to reconsider the membership process every Tuesday because one person had a revelation during breathwork. But agreements have to remain open to lived experience.

Religious communities may call the frozen version doctrine. Secular communities often call it “the process,” which can become equally sacred—just with longer meetings and fewer robes.

### 1. Inner pl/ork

Pl/ork means play + work. Calling therapy “inner work” forgets the inner child, who also wants to play.

Forgive the neologism. I hope pl/ork isn’t too corny. 🌽

Here’s an example: you teach your inner child geography, while s/he teaches you the world is a cat!

Inner-child reparenting means identifying as the inner adult—or the three inner adults: Nurturer, Protector, and Guru/Leader—and caring for the inner child from there. The adult reparents. The child receives love, protection, guidance, and enough freedom to remain alive inside you.

Then there’s somatic regulation, because the mind can make a magnificent decision while the nervous system quietly files an appeal.

My contemplative practice is grounded in the early Buddhist suttas, taken critically. Most are treasure. Some seem like late additions. I consider the Abhidhamma a scholastic wrong turn. A living community should relate to its own teachings the same way: devoted enough to practice them and secure enough to disagree.

### 2. Relational pl/ork

Hearthwork distributes counseling among peers: equal time, attention without interpretation or fixing, and then you switch.

The community’s capacity to help people therefore lives throughout the group instead of inside one professional, guru, or emotionally indispensable founder.

But care is not jurisdiction. The person listening to me should not automatically gain power over my membership, work, housing, children, medicine, or the evidence in a dispute about them. Peer care becomes another hierarchy the moment emotional access turns into administrative authority.

Some things also remain outside peer competence. Knowing when to refer to outside or expert assistance is important.

Jealousy and conflict should be addressed while they still belong to the people involved—before they acquire minutes, procedural objections, and a place near the bottom of an agenda nobody will finish.

My Romance Guide covers the dyads that will form whether the founding document acknowledges romance or pretends everybody has transcended it.

### 3. Medicine pl/ork

Careful, peer-guided use of serious plant medicines, with no special shaman placed above everybody else.

Peer-led doesn’t mean casual. Screening, sober sitters, emergency plans, integration, and clear boundaries all have to exist before the first ceremony. Figuring out the rules while several members are altered would be a very pure form of collective learning, but I don’t recommend it.

### 4. The container

Membership, land, shared resources, governance, children, and exit.

My own long-term direction is toward communities that use less and less money. I think the path has three stages.

First, basic life inside the community—food, housing, care, tools, education, and ordinary transport—is not bought from one another. Members may still keep income from outside work, but anyone earning outside contributes an agreed share to the common. This is probably the realistic starting point.

Later, outside wages and income from community businesses go into a common purse. Members do not accumulate private wealth while they belong. The Bruderhof has lived a version of this for generations; my father spent seventeen years inside it. Twin Oaks and other income-sharing communities have built related versions. A common purse is not an untested theory.

The furthest goal is for the community to stop using money outside too, meeting needs directly and exchanging through goods, gifts, shared work, or reciprocal obligation. I did not find a well-documented modern community that has fully reached that point. Governments may make it literally impossible through land taxes and other required payments. The practical endpoint may therefore be a nearly moneyless community with one small collective cash boundary.

None of this removes scarcity or economic power. Food stores, housing, tools, land, medicine, transport, and access to whatever outside currency the group still needs can all be controlled. A money-free community still has to answer who allocates scarce things, what obligations people have to one another, and what happens when somebody leaves.

These subjects receive less spiritual enthusiasm because nobody has ever returned from an ecstatic vision announcing that the group needs a better provisional-membership policy. Unfortunately, the vision can’t determine a departing member’s share, prevent one founder from controlling the land, or decide what happens when a child reports something serious.

Beautiful land can’t compensate for ownership that gives one person the final say. Peer counseling can’t rescue a membership process that admits people faster than the group can support them. Medicine can’t repair an exit agreement that was never written.

The first three parts shape the community’s inner life. The container gives that work a stable place to happen.

Skip one, and the group eventually has to deal with it under pressure—usually after land, labor, or shared resources are already entangled and everyone has developed opinions.

⟦author⟧And this reparenting journey is built on somatic regulation first. I.e., listening to the body's feelings before making a decision with the mind. And that actually symbolizes the adult/child relationship to me in some way as well.⟦/author⟧

### 2. ⟦author⟧Relational pl/ork⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧Hearthwork distributes counseling among peers: equal time, attention without interpretation or fixing, and then you switch.⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧The idea is that the capacity to be there for people when they need help isn’t located in one professional or guru, or the founder of the group that everyone emotionally depends on, but in the community as a whole.⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧But since we're merging peer therapy with community, we also need to make sure that the person caring about you and listening to you shouldn't translate that information into administration over your life, the way a professional therapist might diagnose you as this or that and then suddenly you're ineligible for certain careers. Although obviously that information can come out regardless when people are in close quarters.⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧Another thing is knowing which problems are beyond what peers can handle and need outside or expert help. And another is to deal with jealousy and conflict while it's still between the people involved, before it gets put on the agenda (generally at the bottom of the agenda). Speaking of jealousy, check out my Romance Guide, since that's a topic each person & each couple first handles by themselves.⟦/author⟧

### 3. ⟦author⟧Medicine pl/ork⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧Community can reach new heights by carefully incorporating serious plant medicines with peer guidance rather than exalting any one person as a special shaman.⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧This doesn’t mean that a peer-led ceremony should be done without structure. Things like screening participants, making sure the sitters are sober, having a plan for dealing with emergencies, planning for integration, and other rules and boundaries need to be dealt with beforehand. While there may be some pure learning that could come from having several altered members figure out what rules and boundaries are needed on the spot, it’s not something I recommend.⟦/author⟧

### 4. ⟦author⟧The container⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧This is the part about membership, land, resources, governance, children, leaving, etc.⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧Like my father, I’m aiming for communities with less and less money involved, since money is antagonistic to the spirit of community. If I have money, I can get whatever I want, regardless of whether anyone cares about my wellbeing. That's the hegemonic philosophy of life we are trying to overturn. I think the path there has three stages.⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧First there will be no money exchanged between members for basic community functions like food, housing, care, tools, education, transport etc. Members might have outside jobs, and they bring an agreed share of that income in. This is probably a good place to start if someone wants to create a community now and have it be realistic.⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧Later all wages and other income earned by the members from work outside the community, or from business inside the community, will be paid into the common purse of the community. No member accumulates private wealth while a member of the community. Some version of this is how things have been done in the Bruderhof community for generations, and my father lived there for 17 years. It is also done, in a somewhat different way, at Twin Oaks, and a number of other income-sharing communities. So a common purse isn’t just a theory.⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧Eventually, the community would also stop using money outside. People would meet their needs directly, or through exchange (be it trade, gift, work, obligation, or whatever). I couldn't find a well-documented modern community that has gone this far, and in fact, it may be impractical in some locations because of government requirements like taxes on land. So perhaps the practical end point here is a community that is nearly entirely moneyless, but has a small collective cash boundary on the outside.⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧None of this does away with scarcity or economic power. There are lots of areas where such power can be wielded: access to food storage, housing, tools, land, medicine, transportation, and the outside world’s currency, to name a few. Even in a moneyless community, someone can control what's scarce. Who decides how these things are distributed? What do members owe one another? What happens when someone wants to leave?⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧These are less exciting topics to discuss spiritually. It's rare to see someone come back from an ecstatic vision with news of how to revamp the community’s policies on provisional members, or how to divvy up assets with a departing member, or how to prevent one founder from having control over the land, or what to do when a child reports something serious. And if they do, it's still something that needs a sober thinking-through.⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧But no amount of gorgeous land will make up for the mess that is created when one person owns the land or has the final say about what will happen on it. And when members come on board faster than the group can support them, all the peer counseling in the world won't make up for it, any more than medicine makes up for the lack of a written exit agreement.⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧The first three parts are the inside of the container, and this container is what gives those a stable place to manifest. It's the practical logistical foundation that gets built once the architects can work well together.⟦/author⟧

⟦author⟧If one of the four gets skipped, it will need to be dealt with eventually, at high stress, usually after the group has invested land, labor, or other resources and everyone has an opinion on how things should be.⟦/author⟧
