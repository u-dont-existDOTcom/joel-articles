## 1. The spine

The published argument: communes fail in two mirror-image ways, secular groups burying hurt under procedure and spiritual groups putting one person in charge of everyone's inner life; the missing third option is peer-held inner, relational and medicine practice inside a deliberately built container (money, children, membership, land, exit), guarded against founder capture and overload, built practice-first and spread by teaching. Section 1 sets up the father, the escuelita and both failures; 2 says why now and answers the freeloader objection; 3 diagnoses; 4 lays out the model; 5 to 8 work through medicine, technology, jealousy and children; 9 and 10 give the best real model and how it spreads; 11 and 12 turn the warnings on the founder and on capacity; 13 and 14 give the practical sequence through exit and dissolution; 15 tests it against Sénégal; 16 says what to do now.

The rewrite makes the same argument in the same order: all 17 sections, the thesis lines word for word ("The secular communities often buried hurt under procedure... the missing third option"), the closing lines nearly so. Several of Joel's changes make jobs clearer: pl/ork is defined at first use, founderism is defined, and section 8 now opens on couples disagreeing about children, which bridges from section 7.

[1] One job changed, in Joel's own rewrite: the second half of section 11, "How My Own Model Could Become a Cult". The published met its risks with hard lines: "The boundary is simple: the principles are commitments I choose for my own practice, never powers the community gains over me"; "Members can decline to share without producing a therapeutic explanation for the refusal"; a floor "the majority cannot vote away"; "leave before they schedule a group process about your leaving". The rewrite keeps the firm lines on medicine ("nobody has to take medicine to have equal respect in the community"), exit ("a usable avenue for exit") and admission (below), and meets the rest with balances: "privacy also can be resistance to the communal spirit"; "if a member is avoiding the therapy process and becoming hard to deal with, they will naturally not belong at a certain point"; rights that can't be voted away "unless it's a question of resource availability as a whole"; "Sometimes it really is their wounded ego projection"; "you can vote with your feet". The position is his. The effect on the whole: the answer to the opening charge, that spiritual communities "put one person in charge of everybody’s inner life", no longer says a member may keep their inner material to themselves, while his section 3 defines the spiritual failure as "the inner world being shaped by the outer as a condition of membership". His answer is probably that nobody holds the gate alone ("No single person or permanent group of people should handle grievances and have the power to decide who gets into or stays in the community"). Question for Joel: say that difference outright in section 11, or bring back the non-disclosure line?
