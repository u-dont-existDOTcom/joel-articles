SECTION: The Medicine Part, Without Pretending It Isn’t There

PUBLISHED:

Psychedelics have moved from taboo toward regulated use much faster than most community literature has acknowledged. Oregon’s licensed psilocybin service centers began opening in 2023. Colorado created a regulated natural-medicine system and began licensing in 2025. New Mexico enacted a Medical Psilocybin Act in 2025. In Australia, authorized psychiatrists have had limited access to psilocybin and MDMA for specified conditions since 2023.

The movement is neither smooth nor inevitable. In 2024, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration issued a Complete Response Letter declining to approve the submitted MDMA-assisted therapy application and asking for further evidence. Portugal is often described lazily as having “legalized everything”; it actually decriminalized possession of small amounts for personal use while trafficking remains criminal.

Communities therefore face real legal differences by country, state, substance, religious status, and activity. Anyone building around medicine needs actual local legal advice, not a friend who once read a thread.

Most intentional communities that use psychedelics still avoid saying so publicly. Even where the law permits some use, parents may worry about children, neighbors may imagine chaos, insurers may disappear, and a local official may understand none of the distinctions.

I’m discussing it because leaving medicine out would falsify my model. Many readers already know my psychedelic writing, including my loveyhuasca/Haoma material. The honest version of this article has to address both the value and the risk.

The case against building medicine into community is serious. The Global Ayahuasca Survey found physical and mental adverse effects were common, although severe outcomes were much less common and context mattered. These medicines can open material faster than a person or group can support it.

They also attract people who love revelation and hate follow-through. A person can collect twelve ceremonies, six origin stories, and a new sacred name while remaining unable to apologize to a housemate. Any community organized mainly around the peak experience will eventually discover that insight does not wash dishes, revise agreements, or calm a frightened child.

The case for communal use is also real in my experience. Psychedelics can loosen defenses that otherwise dominate every serious conversation. They can create powerful bonds when people sit through difficult nights together. They can also produce confusion, grandiosity, dependency, and interpersonal mess. The question is whether the surrounding community has enough maturity to tell those apart.

## Community Is What Makes Psychedelics Pl/ork Best!

Insights need somewhere to land. You see your life with startling clarity one night and return to the same environment, where nobody knows what happened and every old cue is waiting. In community, the people who held you through the experience can also notice what happens during the following months.

Depth can increase alienation. After my original ayahuasca ego deaths at 26, I had trouble finding anyone in the physical world who seemed to have experienced anything similar. I couldn’t even find them at Buddhist monasteries despite all the preaching about enlightenment. That helped produce a God complex, a Savior complex, or both—and then attracted exactly the right people to demonstrate my limits. 😂

Trusted support is different from purchased support. The commercial alternative is often paying strangers for one night of care. Sometimes those strangers are excellent. Sometimes the person with the feather has known you for four hours and already has a cosmology for everything you say.

In community, a sober sitter may be somebody who has known you for years. Being watched and being held feel different. This matters especially with long, medically risky experiences such as iboga, where screening and continuous observation are essential.

Peers can also reality-check the downloads. Psychedelics produce genuine insight and convincing nonsense with identical lighting. Friends who know your history may notice which parts resemble a new understanding and which parts resemble your mother.

Community creates rhythm. Many traditional medicine systems place ceremonies inside communal timing rather than individual appetite. A shared calendar can reduce compulsive repetition and give integration time to happen.

The logistics become easier. Trusted childcare can be arranged collectively. People don’t need to drive away from the ceremony. Somebody can bring water, stay nearby, or sit quietly while a person returns to ordinary consciousness.

There are people to play with. Raves often create sudden intimacy among strangers before trust exists. In community, ecstatic music and movement can happen among people who already know one another’s names, histories, and inconvenient habits. 💃🕺

I won’t idealize psychedelics. Some people should avoid them, some communities shouldn’t use them, and enthusiasm will create casualties wherever competence lags behind access. A community choosing this path needs enough humility to say no, pause, refer out, and admit when an experience caused harm.

## My Ceremony Principles

No special shamans. Traditional lineages usually disagree with me here. Many ayahuasca and Bwiti communities believe medicine should remain under trained lineage authority. I respect what those traditions have preserved and have learned from them.

My objection is to placing one charismatic person above everyone else’s direct experience. A retreat-circuit facilitator may be wise, or may be unvetted, unaccountable, sexually predatory, financially dependent on repeat customers, or simply wrong. Deep openness magnifies the cost of that uncertainty.

I don’t yet know how a peer-led medicine culture prevents an informal shaman from emerging anyway—the person everybody consults, trusts, and gradually stops questioning even though nobody gave them the title. Rotating roles and public accountability may help. I don’t think either solves it completely.

I want people to learn self-guidance and reciprocal care while respecting the limits of their competence. I’ve written more about peer-held ceremonies and about the best psychopath shaman I ever met.

Peer-led never means casual. Screen for medical and psychiatric contraindications. Review medications and combinations. Keep an appropriate sober sitter. Decide what requires professional or emergency care before anybody takes anything. Distributed authority raises the competence requirement; it doesn’t lower it.

I’m especially cautious about medicines and protocols I consider more destabilizing or medically burdensome, including some uses of ketamine and MDMA and bufo. Those judgments are explained in the linked articles rather than smuggled into one sentence here.

The medicine stays behind the other pl/ork. Reparenting, somatic capacity, and peer counseling come first. Medicine can deepen a practice that exists. It cannot create months of honest relationship by pharmacological decree.

This prerequisite also filters out experience collectors. Somebody unwilling to spend six months learning to listen without fixing is unlikely to become more relationally mature because the visions were especially geometric.

Integration is ordinary life. Ceremony opens something. The following weeks reveal whether anything changed—in relationships, daily behavior, sleep, decisions, and the person’s ability to tolerate frustration without declaring a new spiritual emergency.

Supports matter. Preparation, nutrition, interaction screening, dose discipline, and emergency planning matter more than aesthetic ceremony details. I keep my current safety material in Altered States Triage.

Legality needs a written answer. The law varies widely and sometimes ambiguously. A community should document what activities are allowed, prohibited, private, insured, or legally reviewed. “Nobody around here has heard of it” is information, not a legal opinion.

## After the Psychedelic Calling

Many people come out of deep psychedelic or mystical experience with the same urge: find the tribe, build the village, and help other people reach whatever just opened.

I know that call. I also know the inflation that can ride beside it. You return believing love can heal anyone. Then you meet people who don’t want to change, people who sincerely want to change but keep choosing the opposite, and people whose crisis exceeds anything your enthusiasm can hold.

The call may be real. Omnipotence is a terrible founding document.

A group formed during collective spiritual euphoria can recruit too quickly, promise too much, and discover arithmetic only after several people are already dependent on it. The capacity and membership sections later in this article exist partly to protect the original generosity from that first rush.

Once the call sobers up, it can become useful. A community can serve as a training ground where people learn the practices, care for one another, and sometimes leave to begin another experiment. That answers the reasonable objection that communes are merely privileged escape pods.

Medical care, banking, phones, vehicles, schools, supply chains, government identification, internet access, and energy are separate decisions. Full rejection can create needless suffering. Full dependence means the outside system can reach directly into every essential function.

The Amish have spent generations evaluating technologies according to their effects on community life. Different groups reach different answers. It should decide in advance how members access care, what the community guarantees from common resources, what may require outside money or insurance, and how to treat somebody who chooses a different level of medical involvement.

No member should have to prove ideological loyalty while frightened or sick. A catastrophic-illness agreement should protect choice, access to common resources or outside funds when needed, transportation, privacy, and the possibility that the community’s preferred approach won’t be enough.

At the same time, social connection itself has measurable health value. The Surgeon General’s advisory links isolation with higher risks across several outcomes. Community won’t cure every illness. It can change the daily conditions under which people become ill, recover, age, and die.

SECTION: Jealousy Can Capture Monogamy or Free Love

PUBLISHED:

Any community that lasts will be reorganized by attraction and pair-bonding. In a city, two exes can move across town. In a thirty-person commune, they may still share a kitchen, work team, meeting, childcare, and friends the next morning. A couple’s jealousy or breakup becomes the community’s problem because it changes loyalties, sleeping arrangements, labor, children, alliances, and sometimes who leaves.

The usual argument—monogamy versus free love—starts one level too late. My Romance Guide gives the longer version. Here I’ll state the community problem plainly: close quarters magnify jealousy, and jealousy can capture either system.

Rigid monogamy can turn fear and possession into moral law. Free love without inner pl/ork can distribute the same fear across a larger spreadsheet. A relationship label doesn’t release childhood panic, comparison, abandonment terror, or the wish to control another person.

I don’t think a community should choose the form for everyone. Couples and other relationship configurations can make different agreements, revise them, and ask for support when those agreements expose old material.

Whatever forms people choose, housing, children’s security, membership, and access to common necessities should not depend on remaining inside one romantic agreement.

ZEGG and Tamera have spent decades experimenting with relationships inside community. Their answers aren’t commandments for everybody, but they demonstrate that a group can acknowledge erotic life rather than pretending twenty adults have become neutral colleagues because the bylaws say so.

The non-negotiable for me is that jealousy cannot become the hidden governor. It has to be speakable, felt in the body, explored without humiliation, and gradually released rather than converted into punishment or policy.

The group can offer support and enforce basic boundaries without choosing everyone’s relationship structure. People will still disagree, which is more realistic than declaring the matter solved.

## The Mother Is Primary. The Village Is Real. Neither Replaces the Other.

My position is that mothers raise their own children inside a community where care is genuinely shared. The village can carry meals, supervision, teaching, protection, play, and much of fathering. It should never take a child away from the mother in the name of liberating her.

Jean Liedloff’s The Continuum Concept is one of the biggest reasons I think community matters for children. Her Ye’kuana account is one outsider’s interpretation and may idealize what she saw, but the picture remains powerful: babies carried inside ordinary adult life, children surrounded by several ages, learning through participation, and one exhausted parent not expected to become the child’s whole social world. A nuclear household can imitate pieces of this. A real village can make it normal.

Experiments with children sleeping and living separately from parents deserve special caution. Research on Israeli kibbutzim found that communal sleeping eventually ended through a mixture of developmental findings, parental preferences, and social change. High-quality group care and multiple loving adults can benefit children; replacing secure parental attachment is a different proposition.

Tamera’s Children’s Place deserves direct study rather than caricature. My own bias remains with systems that expand a child’s circle without making the parent-child bond ideologically inconvenient.

## Children Need Freedom Inside Real Protection

[caption] African kids enjoying their childhood!

Children also need territory that belongs to them—places where they can develop a society of children, create games, resolve ordinary disputes, and experience some freedom from constant adult management.

I tasted that at a Unitarian youth camp, and it remains one of the freest memories I have. ZEGG’s Kinderhaus works from a similar intuition. Adults hold the outer safety. Children still get a world.

This can be badly misunderstood, so the safeguards need to be explicit. A children’s territory cannot become a place where adults stop paying attention, where older children quietly dominate younger ones, or where secrecy is treated as independence.

I also reject the suburban panic that treats every warm relationship between an unrelated adult and child as suspicious. Children benefit from many adults who know them, enjoy them, notice changes, and will act when something is wrong. Fear can destroy that gift without actually preventing abuse.

The protection has several parts:

Give children real agency. Their “no” matters. Adults aren’t entitled to affection, physical contact, obedience outside legitimate safety needs, or private access.

Create a no-secrets rule. No adult asks a child to hide a gift, game, touch, conversation, or relationship from the child’s mother or another trusted adult. Surprises have an ending date. Secrets designed to isolate the child are forbidden.

Teach truthfulness before a crisis. Children should understand that their words carry power and that adults will listen seriously. This education must never become interrogation, punishment for uncertainty, or a reason to dismiss a report. A frightened or confused child may tell a story imperfectly and still need protection.

Hear the child even when the accused person is beloved. Communities fail here because the adult may be charismatic, useful, wealthy, spiritually important, or everybody’s friend. Decide the response process before anyone’s face is attached to it. Immediate safety comes first; investigation must be competent and fair; nobody’s status buys silence.

Keep relationships and a reporting route outside the community. Grandparents, cousins, local friends, teachers, and other trusted adults give children perspective and somewhere to speak. At least one trusted adult, advocate, or reporting route should be reachable without going through community leadership. In 1984, Vermont authorities seized 112 children during the Island Pond raid, and the court returned them after rejecting the state’s blanket request. State intervention can fail spectacularly. Total community enclosure can also fail. Children need more than one world.

I also searched the research corpus for a well-documented intentional-community case in which a child remained seriously dangerous to other people and the record followed the whole sequence: conduct, assessment, intervention, review, and what happened later. I did not find one. That may be because records are private or cases were routed elsewhere, but it means I cannot claim communities already know how to handle the hardest version internally. ## The Door Must Open Outward at Adulthood

Amish youth make an adult choice about whether to join the church, and roughly 85 percent or more eventually do. The popular caricature of Rumspringa as a universal teenage free-for-all is exaggerated, but adult baptism still matters: belonging becomes a commitment rather than a fact imposed at birth.

A community whose children never leave cannot know whether they would freely return. Let them study, travel, make friends, encounter other ways of life, and disagree with the founders without being treated as traitors.

Every childhood narrows the future. Most ordinary families do not hand an eighteen-year-old a pile of money to go explore the world; they try to prepare them for some viable next step. A poor or money-free community may have even less cash to offer, and food, medical care, and basic survival may rightly come first.

The realistic standard is not unlimited freedom. It is that leaving should not be made artificially impossible. If children grow up without using money, they especially need enough knowledge of the outside money world not to be helpless in it: jobs, rent, contracts, banking, scams, identification, transportation, and how to ask for help. They need portable skills and education, relationships beyond the community, access to their records and documents, and at least one plausible next path—school, apprenticeship, work, family, or another community. If the community can offer temporary housing, travel help, tools, or a small transition reserve, great. If it cannot, it can still avoid punishing departure or withholding the knowledge and records a young adult needs to function elsewhere. No upbringing offers perfect freedom. The point is not to erase path dependence; it is not to turn belonging into a trap.

Some may leave permanently; others may start communities with different values rather than waiting decades to inherit the original version. Parents may experience that as loss even when the movement is evolving.
