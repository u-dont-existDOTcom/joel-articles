It's kind of important to be able to eject people from the commune if they become a serious, generalized danger or if they repeatedly and seriously violate important rules and boundaries. Yes, sometimes there are useful restorative things that can be done, but only if they can be done in a reasonable amount of time. If someone is being seriously dangerous, the commune does not have to become a treatment program for them.

The problem is that this power is super easy to abuse, so it's really important to set up a process ahead of time for when this power is used. What counts as dangerous behavior serious enough to warrant immediate protective actions? How is evidence gathered and preserved? Who recuses themselves from the process? Who gets to decide what immediate temporary actions to take to prevent harm while preserving the person's access to adequate housing, common resources, outside funds (if any), etc? How can they respond to the charges/actions against them? Is there a process by which their case can be reviewed and appealed without risking harm to others? How are their records and contact with family preserved if they're separated? When is it appropriate to involve outside authorities?

That last one is trickier than it seems, and in fact this whole research thing starts off much too nationstatist, but stay with it. What counts as an outside authority doesn't have to be the nationstate; it could be another community, a larger federation, outside professionals, an autonomous regional authority, or existing courts, regulatory agencies or emergency services. Just because a region is autonomous, like the Zapatistas, doesn't mean that its constituents give up all their non-waivable individual rights.

It can also be useful to set up a mediator or ombud ahead of time, so that in an emergency the person in question will trust the "neutral stranger" who they've already met. However mediation is not a replacement for safeguarding and adjudication; sometimes people just have to separate.
