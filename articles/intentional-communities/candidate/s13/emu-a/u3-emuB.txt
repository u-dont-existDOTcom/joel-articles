This is a great point because whoever has the land has all the power.

If the land is in the name of the founder, then you have a landlord who may be running the community with a philosophy. If the land is in the names of three members out of twelve, then you have two classes of members, regardless of the language in the welcome packet.

Some form of legal entity needs to be decided on before the land is bought: cooperative, trust, association, company etc. Then you need to decide voting rights, access and use rights to common resources, whether or not contributions will be counted, what happens when someone dies, outside debt, improvements, leaving, etc.

Even in a moneyfree community you'll want some kind of system that makes visible all the things that people would otherwise fight over. All members should have access to information about what the community "owns" or shares, what is reserved for certain uses, what outside "obligations" the group has, who is making allocations of key resources, potential conflicts of interest, etc. Also, procedures for large transfers of resources outside the community (land, major equipment, stores, money) and procedures for members to take resources with them when they leave (whether that's money or some other form) are really important.

Even good people have reasons to leave a community someday. It's coercive to not allow them to leave with what they brought.

It's like a prenup at a wedding, but it's a good idea to have while you're still planning the wedding.

For an ordering of community steps, CLT suggests vision, members, major resources, and then land.
