The problem is that The Farm grew so rapidly that there were a lot of people who really needed this kind of thing and didn’t have very good filters for how to do it. Like, it was a spiritual thing to be open to everybody, etc.

So, yeah, you do need a process by which people come in, just for the benefit of everyone involved.

a) Exchange a bunch of correspondence, make sure they’ve read the right things, groked the basic ideas, started doing the practice, etc.

b) Have them come out for a short visit, like a week or two. Note that most people can be at least reasonably charming for two weeks. In fact, if they weren’t charming for two weeks, congrats, you just picked up a new member! 😅

c) Have them come out for a longer visit. Some even professionally competent fakers can pull it off for a few months.

d) Grant them some kind of provisional membership.

e) Grant them full membership, with rights to land and shared resources, etc., subject to whatever agreements the founding members made amongst themselves.

At each stage, you should be looking for evidence about whether the person will behave the way you need them to, not whether they have good vibes. It’s very easy to be fooled by good vibes, even if you spend half a year with someone. Especially if you’re relying on some kind of spiritual or therapeutic training to help you evaluate people. You want evidence specific to the behavior you’re looking for, especially if you’re giving them access to something risky, like children, or large shared resources, or the ability to prescribe medicine, or drive a car, or access private information about other members.

Before even granting provisional membership, you should make sure to do at least one group decision-making process with the person, at least one work/shared resource activity with the person, and at least one post-processing of a conflict that involved the person. This will give you a rough simulation of what it’ll be like after people have moved in, and it will prevent people from moving in before they’re ready.

Finally, keep in mind that even people who aren’t participating in your polity (visitors, volunteers, workers, renters, interns, etc.) need something like this, if you’re making decisions that affect their compensation or access to shared resources. It’s not enough to just have an egalitarian polity among the members of one commune and then treat the people in another commune like servants.
