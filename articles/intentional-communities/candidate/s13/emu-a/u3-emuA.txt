Land ownership confers a tremendous amount of power.

If it’s just in the founder’s name, he or she is effectively the landlord and gets to impose his or her philosophy on the community. If it’s in the names of, say, three out of twelve members, then there are effectively two classes of membership, regardless of how “egalitarian” the community is on paper.

So it’s important to make some decisions before buying land: What legal entity will purchase the land – co-op, trust, association, company, etc.? Who will have what voting rights and access/use rights to the land and other pooled resources? Will member contributions be accounted for, and if so, how? What happens when a member dies? Can members have outside debt? Who’s allowed to make improvements? What happens when people want to leave?

Even in a “money-free” community there will be scarce resources and bottlenecks that need to be navigated. Community members ought to know what resources are pooled, what’s set aside for specific purposes, what external obligations the community has, what power people have to redistribute those resources, and where conflicts of interest are. It shouldn’t be possible for someone to take a big chunk of community resources (land, equipment, food stores, outside cash, etc.) without meeting some criteria. And people ought to be able to leave with something – whether it’s cash or some amount of community resources (tools, transportation, temporary housing, food, use rights, etc.).

Even good people will sometimes need to leave for good reasons, and it’s not very loving to hold them there with threats to confiscate everything they bring with them.

Like a pre-nup, it’s best to sort this stuff out while you’re still planning your wedding.

Of course, as Diana Leafe Christian says in Creating a Life Together , you need to sort out your vision before you even bring in new members, and you need to sort out membership before pooling major resources, and you need to sort out major resources before buying land.
