# Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community

You don’t need land yet. The small group that’s still deciding whether to build a community already is the first community.

Don’t combine ownership and major resources without going through at least one real conflict together, start to finish.

Not writing a vision statement and shaking hands on it, or settling for a truce nobody believes. Actually going through the conflict together, emotionally. Somebody names what happened, and each person gets time. You find out what old material is getting touched for each of them and where the jealousy/humiliation/fear is, and you stay with it until the emotional pressure has changed enough for an agreement to mean something.

Until that’s happened, the group is still a circle of promising strangers, and I wouldn’t ask that circle to take on a mortgage yet.

In the meantime there’s plenty to do. Meet in rented or borrowed space, or at someone’s house, and find out how the group handles conflict long before anyone needs a surveyor.

Also in the meantime, work out a budget for the practical side. Water, housing, food stores, health care. Tools, seasonal labor, transport, schooling, getting to markets. Outside debts and how long the cash will last, if the community uses money. And the few skills that would stop everything if nobody had them. However good everyone is at dealing with each other emotionally, a group can still die if the well dries up, or if the one exhausted person who knows where everything is and how it works collapses.

## Please Choose the Values Before Falling in Love With the View

People often pick a community the way they’d pick a vacation rental: the land is gorgeous, the sunset shows up in the pond, and everybody seemed really nice when you visited.

The pond wins you over on the first date, and then two years later the vaccination policy comes up.

When you go to visit a community, have a list of your values ready to go. If you’re unsure where you stand on something, note that too. Some important areas are children, money, property ownership, romance, medicine, food, spiritual practice, technology, privacy, conflict and leaving.

Add sexuality and gender to the list. Could an LGBTQ+ member live openly, have relationships and stay fully protected by the community? Should children be taught to question their sexual identities at an early age? What will the community do if one of its kids grows up gay or trans? If a group hasn’t answered these yet, it hasn’t finished working out its values on freedom or on raising children, either.

Being willing to experiment is a real value, but you have to declare it, because the person next to you may think the experiment ended in 1978 and the results are doctrine now.

Then compare your values with the group’s behavior, which doesn’t always match its public language. Ask how they handled a recent conflict and who can block a decision. It’s also worth finding out what happened to the last person who left, and who owns the land. Those can be uncomfortable conversations, but the view will still be there afterward.

## Resources, Land, and Exit Terms

Whoever owns the land has a lot of power over the community. If the deed is in the founder’s name, the founder is basically a landlord with a philosophy. If it’s in the names of three members out of twelve, you have two classes of members, whatever the welcome packet says about everyone being equal.

Some kind of legal form has to be chosen before the land is bought, whether that’s a cooperative, trust, association, company, or whatever fits your jurisdiction. Then you need to decide voting rights and access/use rights for shared resources, and whether contributions get counted. Work out inheritance and outside debt, how improvements get handled, and what happens when somebody leaves.

Even a money-free community has scarce things and bottlenecks, so the material side has to be visible to everyone. Members ought to be able to see what the community owns or holds in common and what’s been set aside, and what it owes to anyone outside. They should know who can hand out major resources, and where the conflicts of interest are. Big transfers of land, equipment, stored supplies or outside cash shouldn’t depend on one person’s memory or say-so.

People also need to be able to leave with something, in whatever form makes sense. That might be cash, if the community uses it, or it might be tools, transport, temporary housing, stored goods or the right to keep using something. Otherwise the right to leave is a paper right that only works when there happens to be spare money.

Good people leave for good reasons, like a dying parent, love or work. Health and exhaustion count too, and so does a change of mind. If leaving means giving up everything they put in, staying becomes coercive.

That’s why exit terms are best written while nobody is leaving and it’s all still hypothetical. It’s the community version of a prenup that you work out while planning the wedding. Nobody finds that romantic, but it protects people. And it’s much easier to do before anybody is furious.

If you want the whole sequence, Diana Leafe Christian’s [*Creating a Life Together*](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Ecovillages_and_Low_Impact_Development/Creating_a_Life_Together-Practical_Tools_to_Grow_Ecovillages_and_Intentional_Communities.pdf) has it: the vision first, then the members, then pooling major resources, and the land last.

## From Visitor to Member—Slowly

When [The Farm](https://thefarmcommunity.com/) grew fast, a lot of the people who came were in need, and there weren’t many filters that worked. Being open to everybody felt spiritual, but the practical burden piled up anyway.

So a membership process is there to protect both the people who are already there and the newcomers. It’s no good for anyone to join a group that can’t deliver what it promised.

Correspond first. Have they read the materials? Do they understand why the community exists, and have they started the practices? Writing back and forth costs a lot less than six months of misunderstanding.

Short visit: a week or two of daily life with the group. Most people can be charming for two weeks, so congrats, you’ve found out they can be charming for two weeks. 😅

Long visit: stay long enough for the inconvenience and the conflicts to show up. Watch how they react when they’re tired or corrected, attracted or disappointed, or just bored. Even [a professional faker](http://sidney.u-dont-exist.com/) can sometimes keep it up for months, so time gets you more evidence, not certainty.

Provisional membership: decide how long it lasts, what rights and responsibilities come with it, how the review works and how they’d leave. The decision shouldn’t be up to the person who recruited them.

Full membership: rights to the land and shared resources, a voice, and long-term obligations, whatever your founding agreements say.

At each stage, judge people by their conduct, however spiritual or therapeutic they sound. It’s easy to go by vibes, and just as easy to mistake conformity for safety. Six months gives you a bigger sample, but it won’t tell you who’s a good person.

If someone’s going to have a role with real consequences, around children, shared resources, medicine, vehicles or private records, you want evidence of how they handle that specific thing. That means references where they make sense and access given in stages, plus an independent way to challenge the decision.

Before anyone becomes a provisional member, do more than hang out together. Make one real group decision, do one piece of work or one shared-resource task together, and process one small conflict afterward. A trial run like that won’t show you anyone’s soul, but it will show you whether your way of coordinating works before anybody sells a house.

Visitors, volunteers, workers, renters and interns don’t need full political membership to have rights. If a decision really affects their pay or their access to shared resources, they need clear terms and a way to be heard, and the decision should be open to review. The same goes for decisions about their schedule, safety or housing, and their records or discipline. Equality that stops at the membership line can bring back a servant class between communes.

## When Somebody Has to Go

A safe community can’t promise to keep everyone. Sometimes somebody is a serious or general danger, keeps violating important boundaries, or has become impossible to live with, and then ejecting them can be the right answer. Restorative work is useful if it can work in a reasonable amount of time. If someone is endangering everyone else, though, nobody has a duty to turn the commune into their treatment program.

But the power to remove somebody is also one of the easiest powers to abuse. So work out the process before it's about anyone in particular. It should say what kind of conduct calls for protective action right away, and who preserves the evidence. Anyone who’s too close to the case steps aside, and it should be clear who decides on temporary restrictions. The person gets a way to reply, and some kind of review or appeal that doesn’t put others in danger.

While they’re separated, their housing, records and contact with family need protecting, and so does their access to common resources, or to outside funds if there are any. The process should also say when to call for outside help.

That last one is trickier than it looks. “Outside” doesn’t always mean the nation-state, and [my research](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/) leaned too far that way at first. What matters is a competent layer that isn’t reviewing its own conduct. That could be another community, a federation or a professional. It could be an autonomous regional institution, or a court, a regulator or an emergency service. The Zapatistas showed me that autonomous institutions can review local power without becoming the state. But autonomy doesn’t give anyone permission to erase individual rights that can’t be waived, and it doesn’t make outside jurisdiction disappear.

It also helps to pick a mediator or ombud before there’s a crisis, because people who meet the “neutral stranger” for the first time on the day things explode often don’t trust them. Mediation is there to repair things, but it doesn’t replace evidence, safeguarding or a ruling, and once consent is gone it can’t stand in for a final separation.
