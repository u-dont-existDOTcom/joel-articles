Don't worry about getting land yet. If you've got a small handful of people who want to make a community but aren't sure yet, you are already a community, even if it's still an untested one. It may sound cheesy, but it's true all the same.

Also, don't even think about combining ownership (or any other big resource, for that matter) until you've been through at least one real conflict together and REALLY dealt with it. Not dealt with it by writing out a vision statement and shaking hands, or any other fake way of "resolving" things, like a truce nobody believes ("let's just leave each other alone"). But really, truly dealt with it. This includes the relational pl/ork of each of you naming what really went on, giving each of you time, figuring out what came up for each of you (jealousy, humiliation, fear, old material, whatever), and dealing with it until the emotional pressure changes enough that whatever agreement you come up with means anything. Until then, what's it worth?

Until that’s happened, you’re all still promising strangers, and you don’t want to take out a mortgage together yet. That’s okay! There’s lots you can do! You can all meet in a rented or borrowed space, or at each other’s houses, and find out how much conflict the group can handle before you need a surveyor.

You will also want a budget of what you have in the way of resources. This is the boring stuff! But you will need to know what the group can offer in the way of water, housing, food stores, health care, tools, seasonal labor, transportation, access to markets, schooling, etc. Do you have outside debts to pay? How long will your cash reserves last? What critical skills will you need? Being great at the emotional stuff won’t stop you from being dead in the water if your well fails, or if the one person who knows where everything is and how it works gets too exhausted to keep going.

## Please Choose the Values Before Falling in Love With the View

Have you noticed people often choose their community in the same way they would choose a vacation rental? “Wow! Look at this beautiful land! And a pond, with the sunset in it!! Who needs a beach?! And the people seem really nice, let’s get in here!” Fast forward two years. “Wait, there’s a vaccination policy?? And you need paperwork for it?!?!”

Whenever you go to visit a community, have a list ready of what you really believe about children, money, property ownership, romance, medicine, food, spiritual practice, technology, privacy, how to deal with conflict, leaving the community, etc. Long list, huh? Put some question marks next to the things you’re not sure about yet.

This list should also include your thoughts on sexuality and gender, and whether or not LGBTQ+ people should be able to live openly in your community, to date or be in relationships, and be fully protected by the community. You should also include whether or not children should be taught to question their sexual identities from a young age, and what should happen if one of the community’s children comes out as gay or transgender. You don’t have your community’s values on freedom and raising children complete until you answer these questions. Both of them!

Are you willing to experiment? That’s a real value too! Make sure your list of values declares whether or not you are willing to experiment. The people next to you may feel that all experimenting ended in 1978, and everything that came out of it has been doctrine ever since.

When you have a good idea of what’s important to you, you can look at how a group actually functions, not just its public face. Find out how it handled its last conflict, and who can block a decision. How did leaving go for the last person who left? Who owns the land? Then make a choice whether or not it functions in a way that is important to you. It may be an uncomfortable conversation, but the view will still be there after you’ve had it.

## Resources, Land, and Exit Terms

Who will have title to the land? Very important! Whoever has it will have enormous power over the rest of the community. If the deed’s in the name of the “founder,” then he or she is really your landlord, with a philosophy. If three out of twelve members own everything, then despite what your literature says about everyone being equal, there are two classes of members.

What kind of legal entity (cooperative, trust, association, company, whatever fits your jurisdiction) will hold the land? This needs to be decided before it's bought. Then you'll need to decide who has what voting rights, who has what access and use rights to common resources, how you will account for people's contributions (if at all), how inheritance works, what about outside debt, how improvements get handled, and what happens when someone wants to leave the community. Lots of decisions to make!

And how will you handle the VISIBILITY of what the community has? Even if you're a money-free community, you'll still have scarce resources and bottlenecks. Sorry! It will be important for people to know what's owned or held in common, what you've set aside, what obligations you have to outsiders, who has authority to hand out the major resources, where conflicts of interest are, etc. It won't do to have the transfer of major resources (land, equipment, stores, outside cash, etc.) dependent on one person's memory or permission. It will be important to have it specified ahead of time what people's rights are to the common resources when they leave the community. Whether or not you use money, you'll need to have a way of providing people with resources when they leave (cash, if you use it, but also tools, transportation, temporary housing, stored up goods, the right to keep using something, or whatever). Otherwise your "right" to leave is only on paper -- it can't be exercised unless the community has spare money!

Good people leave for good reasons -- dying parent, love, work, health, exhaustion, change of mind, etc. You don't want to make them leave everything they've put in when they leave! That's coercive -- they HAVE to stay if they can't afford to go. Write up the exit terms when it's only theoretical -- like a pre-nup when you're planning the wedding. Not romantic, but protective! Much easier than doing it once everybody's furious.

For the full sequence -- vision, then members, then pooling major resources, then land -- see Diana Leafe Christian's [*Creating a Life Together*](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Ecovillages_and_Low_Impact_Development/Creating_a_Life_Together-Practical_Tools_to_Grow_Ecovillages_and_Intentional_Communities.pdf) (on the reading list at the end). It's the first book I'd hand to any group starting out.
