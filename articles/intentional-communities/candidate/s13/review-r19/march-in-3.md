Don't worry about getting land yet. If you've got a small handful of people who want to make a community but aren't sure yet, you are already a community, even if it's still an untested one. It may sound cheesy, but it's true all the same.

Also, don't even think about combining ownership (or any other big resource, for that matter) until you've been through at least one real conflict together and REALLY dealt with it. Not dealt with it by writing out a vision statement and shaking hands, or any other fake way of "resolving" things, like a truce nobody believes ("let's just leave each other alone"). But really, truly dealt with it. This includes the relational pl/ork of each of you naming what really went on, giving each of you time, figuring out what came up for each of you (jealousy, humiliation, fear, old material, whatever), and dealing with it until the emotional pressure changes enough that whatever agreement you come up with means anything. Until then, what's it worth?

Until that’s happened, it’s still just a circle of promising strangers, and you don’t want to take out a mortgage with them yet. That’s okay! There’s lots you can do! You can all meet in a rented or borrowed space, or at each other’s houses, and find out how much conflict the group can handle before you need a surveyor.

You will also want a budget of what you have in the way of resources. This is the boring stuff! But you will need to know what the group can offer in the way of water, housing, food stores, health care, tools, seasonal labor, transportation, access to markets, schooling, etc. Do you have outside debts to pay? How long will your cash reserves last? What critical skills will you need? Being great at the emotional stuff won’t stop you from being dead in the water if your well fails, or if the one person who knows where everything is and how it works gets too exhausted to keep going.

## Please Choose the Values Before Falling in Love With the View

Have you noticed people often choose their community in the same way they would choose a vacation rental? “Wow! Look at this beautiful land! And a pond, with the sunset in it!! Who needs a beach?! And the people seem really nice, let’s get in here!” Fast forward two years. “Wait, there’s a vaccination policy?? And you need paperwork for it?!?!”

Whenever you go to visit a community, have a list ready of what you really believe about children, money, property ownership, romance, medicine, food, spiritual practice, technology, privacy, how to deal with conflict, leaving the community, etc. Long list, huh? Put some question marks next to the things you’re not sure about yet.

You better also include sexuality and gender on that list. Can LGBTQ+ people live openly in the community? Can they date or be in relationships? Will they be fully protected? Is it okay for children to be taught to question their sexuality at an early age? What will you do if one of the community’s children turns out to be gay or transgender? Until a group has answered those questions, its stance on freedom and raising children is incomplete. Both of them!

Are you willing to experiment? That’s a real value too! Make sure your list of values declares whether or not you are willing to experiment. The people next to you may feel that all experimenting ended in 1978, and everything that came out of it has been doctrine ever since.

When you have this list well in hand, compare it to how the group you’re joining actually functions, not just its public face. How was a recent conflict handled? Who can block group decisions? How did leaving go for the last person who left? Who owns the land? It will be uncomfortable to talk about these things, but the view isn’t going anywhere.

## When Somebody Has to Go

A safe community can’t promise to keep everyone. There’s going to be times when someone has to go. Yeah, it’s tough. It’s also true. If someone is a serious or generalized danger to others, or keeps violating essential boundaries, or for whatever reason just makes living together impossible for everyone else, it can be the right call to eject them. If restorative work can help in a reasonable amount of time, all the better. But it’s not the community’s job to be a treatment program for someone who’s endangering everyone else.

This power to remove people is so easily abused, though. So work out the process before it's about anyone in particular. What counts as cause for immediate protective action? Who decides to put temporary restrictions on someone? Who will make sure all the evidence is preserved? Who will recuse themselves? How will the person be able to reply to the concerns, and have the decision reviewed or appealed, without anyone being put in danger? What protections will there be for the person’s interests (housing, records, family contact, access to common resources, outside funds if any) while the separation is being worked out? When is it time to bring in outside help? (More on that next.)

Note that "outside" doesn't have to mean the nation-state. [My research](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/) initially leaned too far that way. The basic need here is for a competent level of authority that is not reviewing its own conduct. That could mean another community, a federation of communities, professionals, autonomous regional institutions, courts, regulatory agencies, emergency services, etc. The key is that it's not the same people! An important correction that came from the Zapatistas: that authority can be an autonomous regional institution, without it turning into the state. But when power started creeping upward in their own system, they pushed it back down again. And no, autonomy still doesn't mean anyone gets to decide that individual people don't have certain non-waivable rights, or that outside jurisdiction just goes away.

Also decide on a mediator or ombud in advance. Why bother? People often won't trust someone they haven't already met, who comes in during a crisis as a "neutral stranger." This does not obviate the need for evidence, safeguarding, rulings, etc. Mediation is an attempt to restore good relations between people. When people can't or won't consent to being in community any more, it's time for a separation.
