Don't worry about getting land yet. If you've got a small handful of people who want to make a community but aren't sure yet, you are already a community, even if it's still an untested one. It may sound cheesy, but it's true all the same.

Also, don't even think about combining ownership (or any other big resource, for that matter) until you've been through at least one real conflict together and REALLY dealt with it. Not dealt with it by writing out a vision statement and shaking hands, or any other fake way of "resolving" things, like a truce nobody believes ("let's just leave each other alone"). But really, truly dealt with it. This includes the relational pl/ork of each of you naming what really went on, giving each of you time, figuring out what came up for each of you (jealousy, humiliation, fear, old material, whatever), and dealing with it until the emotional pressure changes enough that whatever agreement you come up with means anything. Until then, what's it worth?

Until that’s happened, you’re all still promising strangers, and you don’t want to take out a mortgage together yet. That’s okay! There’s lots you can do! You can all meet in a rented or borrowed space, or at each other’s houses, and find out how much conflict the group can handle before you need a surveyor.

You will also want a budget of what you have in the way of resources. This is the boring stuff! But you will need to know what the group can offer in the way of water, housing, food stores, health care, tools, seasonal labor, transportation, access to markets, schooling, etc. Do you have outside debts to pay? How long will your cash reserves last, if you have any? What critical skills will you need? Being great at the emotional stuff won’t stop you from being dead in the water if your well fails, or if the one person who knows where everything is and how it works gets exhausted.

## Please Choose the Values Before Falling in Love With the View

Have you noticed people often choose their community in the same way they would choose a vacation rental? “Wow! Look at this beautiful land! And a pond, with the sunset in it!! Who needs a beach?! And the people seem really nice, let’s get in here!” Fast forward two years. “Wait, there’s a vaccination policy?? And you need paperwork for it?!?!”

Whenever you go to visit a community, have a list ready of what you really believe about children, money, property ownership, romance, medicine, food, spiritual practice, technology, privacy, how to deal with conflict, leaving the community, etc. Long list, huh? Put some question marks next to the things you’re not sure about yet.

This list should also include your thoughts on sexuality and gender, and whether or not LGBTQ+ people would be able to live openly in your community, to date or be in relationships, and stay fully protected by the community. You should also include whether or not children should be taught to question their sexual identities from a young age, and what happens if one of the community’s children comes out as gay or transgender. Until you answer these questions, you don’t have your community’s values on freedom and raising children complete. Both of them!

Are you willing to experiment? That’s a real value too! Make sure your list of values declares whether or not you are willing to experiment, because the people next to you may feel that the experiment ended in 1978, and everything that came out of it has been doctrine ever since.

When you have a good idea of what’s important to you, you can look at how a group actually functions, not just its public face. Find out how it handled its last conflict, and who can block a decision. How did leaving go for the last person who left? Who owns the land? Then make a choice whether or not it functions in a way that is important to you. It’ll be an uncomfortable conversation, but the view will still be there after you’ve had it.

## Resources, Land, and Exit Terms

Who will have title to the land? Very important! Whoever has it will have enormous power over the rest of the community. If the deed is in the name of the founder, then he or she is really a landlord with a philosophy. If three out of twelve members own everything, then whether or not your literature says everyone is equal, there are two classes of members.

What kind of legal entity (cooperative, trust, association, company, whatever fits your jurisdiction) will hold the land? This needs to be decided before it's bought. Then you'll need to decide who has what voting rights, who has what access and use rights to common resources, how you will account for people's contributions (if at all), how inheritance works, what about outside debt, how improvements get handled, and what happens when someone wants to leave the community. Lots of decisions to make!

And how will you handle the VISIBILITY of what the community has? Even if you're a money-free community, you'll still have scarce resources and bottlenecks. Sorry! It will be important for people to know what's owned or held in common, what you've set aside, what obligations you have to outsiders, who has authority to hand out the major resources, where conflicts of interest are, etc. It won't do to have the transfer of major resources (land, equipment, stores, outside cash, etc.) dependent on one person's memory or permission. It will be important to have it specified ahead of time what people's rights are to the common resources when they leave the community. Whether or not you use money, you'll need to have a way of providing people with resources when they leave (cash, if you use it, but also tools, transportation, temporary housing, stored up goods, the right to keep using something, or whatever). Otherwise your "right" to leave is only on paper -- it can't be exercised unless the community has spare money!

Good people leave for good reasons -- dying parent, love, work, health, exhaustion, change of mind, etc. You don't want to make them leave everything they've put in when they leave! That's coercive -- they HAVE to stay if they can't afford to go. Write up the exit terms when it's only theoretical -- like a pre-nup when you're planning the wedding. Not romantic, but protective! Much easier than doing it once everybody's furious.

For the full sequence -- vision, then members, then pooling major resources, then land -- see Diana Leafe Christian's [*Creating a Life Together*](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Ecovillages_and_Low_Impact_Development/Creating_a_Life_Together-Practical_Tools_to_Grow_Ecovillages_and_Intentional_Communities.pdf) (on the reading list at the end). It's where I'd start for getting that order right.

## When Somebody Has to Go

A safe community can’t promise to keep everyone. There may be times when someone has to go. Yeah, it’s tough. It’s also true. If someone is a serious or generalized danger to others, or keeps violating essential boundaries, or for whatever reason just makes everyday life together impossible for everyone else, it can be the right call to eject them. If restorative work can help in a reasonable amount of time, all the better. But it’s not the community’s job to be a treatment program for someone who’s endangering everyone else.

This power to remove people is so easily abused, though. So work out the process before it's about anyone in particular. What counts as cause for immediate protective action? Who decides to put temporary restrictions on someone? Who will make sure all the evidence is preserved? Who will recuse themselves? How will the person be able to reply to the concerns, and have the decision reviewed or appealed without putting others in danger? What protections will there be for the person’s interests (housing, records, family contact, access to common resources, outside funds if any) during the separation? When is it time to bring in outside help? (More on that next.)

Note that "outside" doesn't have to mean the nation-state. [My research](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/) initially leaned too far that way. The basic need here is for a competent level of authority that is not reviewing its own conduct. That could mean another community, a federation of communities, professionals, autonomous regional institutions, courts, regulatory agencies, emergency services, etc. The key is that it's not the same people! An important correction that came from the Zapatistas: that authority can be an autonomous regional institution, without it turning into the state. But when power had crept up in their own system over the years, it took them a long review to push it back down. And no, autonomy still doesn't mean anyone gets to decide that individual people don't have certain non-waivable rights, or that outside jurisdiction just goes away.

Also decide on a mediator or ombud in advance. Why bother? People often won't trust someone they haven't already met, who comes in during a crisis as a "neutral stranger." A mediator doesn't obviate the need for evidence, safeguarding, rulings, etc. Mediation is an attempt to restore good relations between people. When people can't or won't consent to being in community any more, they still need a final separation.
