# Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community

You don’t need land yet. The small group that’s still deciding whether to build a community already is the first community. Even if it’s just three people around somebody’s kitchen table, that counts.

Don’t combine ownership and major resources without going through at least one real conflict together, start to finish. Not writing a vision statement and shaking hands on it, or settling for a truce nobody believes. I mean actually going through the conflict together, emotionally. That’s relational pl/ork. Somebody names what happened, and each person gets time. You find out which jealousy, humiliation, fear or old material is involved, and you only reach an agreement once the emotional pressure has changed enough for it to mean something.

Until that’s happened, the group is still a circle of promising strangers, and I wouldn’t ask that circle to take on a mortgage yet. In the meantime there’s plenty to do! Meet in rented or borrowed space, or at someone’s house, and find out how much conflict the group can handle before anyone hires a surveyor.

Also in the meantime, budget what the group can provide on the practical side. Water, housing, food stores, health care. Tools, seasonal labor, transport, schooling, getting to markets. Outside debts, or how long the cash will last, if there are any. And the few skills that could stop everything if nobody had them. It’s the boring part, I know. But however good everyone is at dealing with each other emotionally, a group can still die because the well fails, or because one exhausted person is the only one who knows where everything is and how it works.

## Please Choose the Values Before Falling in Love With the View

People often pick a community the way they’d pick a vacation rental: the land is gorgeous, the sunset shows up in the pond, and everybody seemed really nice when you visited. The pond wins you over on the first date, and then two years later the vaccination policy shows up, with paperwork.

When you go to visit a community, have a list ready of what you really believe about children, money, property ownership, romance, medicine, food, spiritual practice, technology, privacy, conflict and leaving. Where are you still unsure? Mark those places too.

Add sexuality and gender to the list. Yes, it keeps getting longer. Could an LGBTQ+ member live openly, have relationships and stay fully protected by the community? Should children be taught to question their sexual identities at an early age? What will the community do if one of its kids grows up gay or trans? If a group hasn’t answered these yet, it hasn’t finished working out its values on freedom or on raising children, either. Your list should also say whether you’re willing to experiment. That’s a real value too, and it has to be declared, because the person next to you may think the experimenting ended in 1978 and whatever came out of it is doctrine now.

Then compare your values with the group’s real behavior, as well as with its public language. Ask how they handled a recent conflict, who can block a decision, what happened to the last person who left, and who owns the land. Those are uncomfortable conversations, but the view will still be there afterward.

## Resources, Land, and Exit Terms

Whoever owns the land has enormous power over the community. If the deed is in the founder’s name, the founder is basically a landlord with a philosophy. And if three members out of twelve own everything? Then you have two classes of members, whatever the welcome packet says about everyone being equal.

What legal form will hold the land: a cooperative, trust, association, company, or whatever fits your jurisdiction? Decide that before the land is bought. Then you need to decide voting rights and access/use rights for shared resources, and contribution accounting if you use it. Work out inheritance and outside debt, how improvements get handled, and what happens when somebody leaves. That’s a lot of deciding, I realize.

Even a money-free community has scarce things and bottlenecks, so the material side has to be visible to everyone. Members ought to be able to see what the community owns or holds in common and what’s been set aside, and what it owes to anyone outside. They should be able to find out who can hand out major resources, and where the conflicts of interest are. Big transfers of land, equipment, stored supplies or outside cash shouldn’t depend on one person’s memory or say-so. Define exit rights, too, so people can leave with something, in whatever form makes sense: cash if the community uses it, and also tools, transport, temporary housing, stored goods or the right to keep using something. Otherwise the right to leave is a paper right that only works when the community happens to have spare money. That’s not much of a right.

Good people leave for good reasons, like a dying parent, love or work. Health and exhaustion count too, and so does a changed mind. If leaving means giving up everything they put in, staying becomes coercive. Exit terms are best written while nobody is leaving and it’s all still hypothetical. It’s the community version of a prenup that you work out while planning the wedding. Nobody finds that romantic, but it protects people. And it’s much easier to do before anybody is furious. If you want the whole sequence, Diana Leafe Christian’s [*Creating a Life Together*](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Ecovillages_and_Low_Impact_Development/Creating_a_Life_Together-Practical_Tools_to_Grow_Ecovillages_and_Intentional_Communities.pdf) has it: the vision first, then the members, then pooling major resources, and the land last.

## From Visitor to Member—Slowly

When [The Farm](https://thefarmcommunity.com/) grew fast, a lot of the people who came were in need, and there weren’t many filters that worked. Being open to everybody felt so spiritual! The practical burden piled up anyway. A membership process is there to protect both the people who are already there and the newcomers. It’s no good for anyone to join a group that can’t deliver what it promised.

Correspond first. Have they read the materials? Do they understand why the community exists, and have they started the relevant practices? A thoughtful back-and-forth costs so much less than six months of misunderstanding!

Short visit: a week or two of daily life with the group. Most people can be charming for two weeks, so congrats, you’ve found out they can be charming for two weeks. 😅

Long visit: stay long enough for the inconvenience and the conflicts to show up. Watch how the person reacts when they’re tired or corrected, attracted or disappointed, or just bored. Even [a professional faker](http://sidney.u-dont-exist.com/) can sometimes keep it up for months! So time gets you more evidence, not certainty.

Provisional membership: decide how long it lasts and what rights and responsibilities come with it, plus how the review works and how they’d leave. Who decides whether they move on to full membership? Not the person who recruited them. Then full membership: rights to the land and shared resources, a voice, and long-term obligations, as your founding agreements set them out.

At each stage, judge people by their conduct, however spiritual or therapeutic they sound. Going by vibes is so easy! So is mistaking conformity for safety. Six months gives you a bigger sample, but it won’t tell you who’s a good person.

If someone’s going to have a role with real consequences, around children, shared resources, medicine, vehicles or private records, you want evidence of how they handle that specific thing. Have you seen them do it? Use references where they make sense and give access in stages. There should also be an independent way to challenge the decision.

Before anyone becomes a provisional member, do more than hang out together. Can the group decide things and work together, or does it only get along? Make one real group decision, do one ordinary work or shared-resource task, and process one small conflict afterward. A trial run like that won’t show you anyone’s soul, but it can show you whether your way of coordinating works before anybody sells a house.

Visitors, volunteers, workers, renters and interns don’t need full political membership to have rights. If a decision really affects their pay or their access to shared resources, they need clear terms and a way to be heard, and the decision should be open to review. The same goes for decisions that really affect their schedule, safety, housing, records or discipline. Equality that stops at the membership line? That can bring back a servant class of non-members moving between communes.

## When Somebody Has to Go

A safe community can’t promise to keep everyone. And if somebody is a serious or general danger, keeps violating essential boundaries, or has become impossible to live with? Then ejecting them can be the right answer. Restorative work is useful if it can work in a reasonable amount of time. If someone is endangering everyone else, though, nobody has a duty to turn the commune into their treatment program.

But the power to remove somebody is also one of the easiest powers to abuse! So work out the process before it's about anyone in particular. The process should say what kind of conduct calls for protective action right away, and who preserves the evidence. Anyone who’s too close to the case steps aside, and it should be clear who decides on temporary restrictions. The person gets a way to reply, and some kind of review or appeal that doesn’t put others in danger. While they’re separated, their housing, records and contact with family need protecting, and so does their access to common resources, or to outside funds if there are any. The process should also say when to call for outside help.

“Outside” doesn’t always mean the nation-state. I’ll admit [my research](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/) leaned too far that way at first. What matters is a competent layer that isn’t reviewing its own conduct. That could be another community, a federation or a professional. It could be an autonomous regional institution, or a court, a regulator or an emergency service. The Zapatistas are an important correction here: autonomous institutions can review local power without becoming the state. But autonomy doesn’t give anyone permission to erase individual rights that can’t be waived, and it doesn’t make outside jurisdiction disappear.

Also pick a mediator or ombud before there’s a crisis. Why ahead of time? Because people who meet the “neutral stranger” for the first time on the day things explode often don’t trust them. Mediation is there to repair things, but it doesn’t replace evidence, safeguarding or a ruling, and once someone no longer consents to living together, it can’t stand in for a final separation.
