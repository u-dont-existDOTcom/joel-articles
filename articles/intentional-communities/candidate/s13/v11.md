# Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community

Don't worry about getting land yet. If you've got a small handful of people who want to make a community but aren't sure yet, you are already a community, even if it's still an untested one. It sounds cheesy but it's true.

Don't even think about combining ownership or other big resources until you have gone through at least one real conflict together and really dealt with it. Not 'dealt with it' by writing a vision statement and shaking hands, or by calling a truce nobody believes, like "Let's all just agree to leave each other alone". But really, truly dealt with it. That's the real relational pl/ork of each of you naming what happened, giving each of you time, finding whatever is there (jealousy, humiliation, fear, old material, whatever), and dealing with it until the emotional pressure has changed enough that an agreement actually means something. Until then, what's it worth?

Until that’s happened, the group is still a circle of promising strangers, and I wouldn’t ask that circle to take on a mortgage yet. In the meantime there’s plenty to do! Meet in rented or borrowed space, or at someone’s house, and find out how much conflict the group can handle before anyone hires a surveyor.

Along with that, you'll also need to budget for what the group can provide on the practical side. That is, the things they can offer in the way of water, housing, food stores, health care, tools, seasonal labor, transportation, schooling, market access, etc. If there are outside debts to pay or cash reserves, how long will the cash last? What are the few critical skills that could stop everything if nobody had them? It's boring stuff, I know. But in the end, a group could be great on an emotional level but be dead in the water because the well fails, or because one exhausted person is the only one who knows where everything is and how it works.

## Please Choose the Values Before Falling in Love With the View

Have you noticed people often choose their community in the same way they would choose a vacation rental? “Wow! Look at this beautiful land! And a pond, with the sunset in it!! Who needs a beach?! And the people seem really nice, let’s get in here!” Fast forward two years. “Wait, there’s a vaccination policy?? And you need paperwork for it?!?!”

When you go to visit a community, have a list ready of what you really believe about children, money, property ownership, romance, medicine, food, spiritual practice, technology, privacy, conflict and leaving. Where are you still unsure? Mark those places too.

Throw sexuality and gender onto that list. Yes, it keeps getting longer. Could an LGBTQ+ member live openly there? Date and/or be in a relationship? Would the community keep them fully protected? Should children be taught to question their sexual identities at an early age? Should one of the children grow up to be gay or transgender, what happens then? Until a group has answered these questions, its values on freedom and child-rearing are incomplete.

Willing to experiment? That's a real value too! Make sure that your list declares whether or not you value experimenting, because the folks next to you may think experimenting ended in 1978 and anything that came from it is doctrine now.

Once you’ve got a good sense of what is important to you, compare this to how the group actually functions (not just how they talk about it in public). How was a recent conflict handled? Who can block decisions? How did it go for the last person who left the group? Who owns the land? It’ll be uncomfortable to have these conversations, but the view won’t go away after you’ve had them.

## Resources, Land, and Exit Terms

Who has the title to the land? It matters so much! Whoever has it has a massive amount of power over the community. If the deed is in the founder's name, then they're really a landlord just with a philosophy. If three members out of twelve own everything, then there's effectively two classes of members even if the literature says everyone's equal.

What legal form will hold the land: a cooperative, trust, association, company, or whatever fits your jurisdiction? Decide that before the land is bought. Then you need to decide voting rights and access/use rights for shared resources, and contribution accounting if you use it. Work out inheritance and outside debt, how improvements get handled, and what happens when somebody leaves. That’s a lot of deciding, I realize.

Visibility is important even in a money-free community. There are always scarce things and bottlenecks. It is important that people be able to know what the community owns or holds in common, what is set aside, what it owes to outsiders. Who has authority over distribution of major resources? Where are the conflicts of interest? Large transfers (land, equipment, stores or outside cash) need to be recorded somewhere, and can't depend on one person's permission. It is also important to specify what rights people have to resources when they leave the community. Whether or not the community uses money, it still needs to be able to provide people with resources when they leave, in whatever form makes sense (cash if it uses money, or tools, transportation, temporary housing, stored goods, the right to keep using something). Otherwise the "right" to leave is just something on paper that can only be exercised if the community has spare money. That's not much of a right.

Good people leave for good reasons, like a dying parent, love, work, health, exhaustion, or just a simple change of mind. Leaving should not require someone to leave everything they’ve put in, as that’s effectively coercing someone to stay. You should write down the exit terms while no one is leaving yet, and it’s still just a theoretical discussion. Think of it as a pre-nup while you’re planning the wedding. It’s not that romantic, but it’s protective, and a lot easier than doing it when people are furious.

For the order to do all this in, see Diana Leafe Christian’s [*Creating a Life Together*](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Ecovillages_and_Low_Impact_Development/Creating_a_Life_Together-Practical_Tools_to_Grow_Ecovillages_and_Intentional_Communities.pdf): First you get the vision, then you get the members, then you pool the major resources, and lastly you get the land. So yes, the land comes last!

## From Visitor to Member—Slowly

When [The Farm](https://thefarmcommunity.com/) grew fast, a lot of the people who came were in need, and there weren’t many filters that worked. Being open to everybody felt so spiritual! The practical burden piled up anyway. A membership process is there to protect both the people who are already there and the newcomers. It’s no good for anyone to join a group that can’t deliver what it promised.

There’s some correspondence first. Did they read the materials? (Not everyone does!) Do they get the basic idea of why this community is here? Are they at least starting to do the relevant practices? It’s a lot cheaper to find out through a thoughtful exchange than after six months of misunderstanding, right?

The short visit: If they come for a week or two and just participate in normal life, well, most people can be charming for two weeks. So if that's all they do, congratulations! You've established that they can be charming for two weeks. 😅

The long visit: If you're not around long enough to actually experience inconvenience and conflict with the person in question, then you're not going to learn very much about them. Watch how they react when tired, corrected, attracted, disappointed, bored, etc. (Hopefully not all at once!) Even [a professional faker](http://sidney.u-dont-exist.com/) may be able to pull off an act for months, so time gets you more evidence, not certainty.

Have you thought about these questions: How long will a provisional membership be? What will a provisional member be responsible for and what are their rights? How will a provisional member's membership be reviewed? How might a person leave provisional membership? Who will make the decision about a person becoming a full member? (Hint: it shouldn't be the person who recruited them.) What rights will a full member have in terms of the land, other shared resources, and their voice within the group? What will a member be responsible for over the long term? (A full member's rights and responsibilities should be addressed in the agreements you draw up when you are first starting your group.) Yes, it's a lot of homework!

How do you know? At each stage, always judge people based on their conduct. No matter how good it sounds when they talk about their spirituality or their therapy, don’t go by that. It’s too easy to go by vibes. It’s also easy to mistake people just doing what everyone else is doing for safety. Even giving it six months will give you more data, but it won’t tell you if someone is a good person.

If someone will have a role in your group that has serious consequences, involving kids, shared resources, medicine, vehicles or private records, you want to know how they handle that specific thing. I wouldn’t hand someone the van keys just because they’re great with kids! You can find out by asking if you’ve seen them do this thing before, and asking for references if appropriate. Give people access to these things in stages and ensure there’s always some way to appeal decisions about these roles to a separate authority if needed.

Do stuff together prior to making them provisional members. (Hanging out doesn't count, I'm afraid!) Can you actually make a decision as a group? Can you actually work together to get something done (an ordinary work or shared-resource task will do)? Can you even process a small conflict afterwards? Do a trial run like that before anybody sells a house. It won't show you anyone's soul, but it can show you whether your way of coordinating works.

Do visitors, volunteers, workers, renters and interns at a commune need some rights even if they're not fully "in" the commune politically? (Yes, even the interns!) When decisions really affect their pay or access to shared resources, they need clear terms and a way to be heard, and those decisions have to be open to review. Also when decisions really affect their schedule, safety, housing, records or discipline. Equality isn't just for members, or else there's a risk of recreating a servant class that moves from commune to commune.

## When Somebody Has to Go

A safe community can’t promise to keep everyone. Yes, really. If someone is a serious or general danger, keeps violating essential boundaries, or is just impossible to live with, ejecting them can be the right call. If restorative work with them can work in a reasonable amount of time, that’s cool! But if they are a danger to the community, the community doesn’t have to turn into their treatment program.

That said, the power to remove somebody is also one of the easiest powers to abuse. So work out the process before it's about anyone in particular. What conduct constitutes a reason for immediate protective action, and who decides on temporary restrictions? Who preserves the evidence, and who has to step aside from the decisions? How does the person reply, and what review or appeal is possible without endangering others? How do people who are separated get housed, have their records and family connections protected, and still be able to use common resources or have access to outside funds, if any? When do people reach for outside help? (More on that one next.)

'Outside' doesn't have to be a nation-state. (It's true that at first [my research](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/) kinda leaned too far toward the nation-state.) What's really needed is that there be a competent layer that isn't reviewing its own conduct. That can be another community, a federation, a professional, an autonomous regional institution, a court, a regulatory agency, an emergency service, or anything else. The Zapatistas are an important correction here: autonomous institutions can review local power without becoming the state, and when power crept up in theirs, they pushed it back down. But autonomy doesn't mean that anyone can erase non-waivable individual rights, or that outside jurisdiction disappears.

Also pick a mediator or ombud beforehand. Why bother? Because if they are chosen in a crisis, and nobody has met this “neutral stranger” before, people often do not trust them. And remember that although mediation is for repairing relations, it does not replace the need for evidence, safeguarding or a ruling. And it does not replace the finality of separation once someone no longer consents to the shared life.
