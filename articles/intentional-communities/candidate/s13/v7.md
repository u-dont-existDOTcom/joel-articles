# Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community

Don't worry about getting land yet. If you've got a small handful of people who want to make a community but aren't sure yet, you are already a community. It sounds cheesy but it's true.

Don't combine ownership/major resources until the group has had at least one real conflict and dealt with it all the way through. Not "dealt with it" by writing a vision statement and shaking hands, or declaring a truce nobody believes, but really dealing with it. In other words, you need to do some real relational pl/ork where someone names what happened and each person gets time. What comes up in conflicts is always jealousy, humiliation, fear, or other old material, so find out which. Deal with it until the emotional pressure has changed enough for an agreement to mean something, and then you can make one.

Until that’s happened, the group is still a circle of promising strangers, and I wouldn’t ask that circle to take on a mortgage yet. In the meantime there’s plenty to do! Meet in rented or borrowed space, or at someone’s house, and find out how much conflict the group can handle before anyone hires a surveyor.

Along with that, you'll also need to budget for the group's physical needs. That is, the things they can offer in the way of water, housing, food stores, health care, tools, seasonal labor, transportation, schooling, market access, etc. If there are outside debts to pay or cash reserves, how long will the cash last? What critical skills will the group need that they don't currently have? It's boring stuff but in the end, a group could be great on an emotional level but be dead in the water because the well fails, or because one exhausted person is the only one who knows where everything is and how it works.

## Please Choose the Values Before Falling in Love With the View

Have you noticed people choose their community in the same way they would choose a vacation rental? “Wow! Look at this beautiful land! And a pond, with the sunset in it!! Who needs a beach?! And the people seem really nice, let’s get in here!” Fast forward two years. “Wait, there’s a vaccination policy?? And you need paperwork for it?!?!”

When you go to visit a community, have a list ready of what you really believe about children, money, property ownership, romance, medicine, food, spiritual practice, technology, privacy, conflict and leaving. Where are you still unsure? Mark those places too.

Throw sexuality and gender onto that list. Is it ok to be openly part of the LGBTQ+ community? Is it okay to date and/or be in a relationship with whomever you choose? Will your community keep you fully protected if you are a member of the aforementioned community? Will your children be encouraged or discouraged to question their sexual identities at an early age? Should your children grow up to be gay or transgender, what happens then? Until your prospective community has answered these questions, its values on freedom and child-rearing are incomplete. Also make sure that your list declares whether or not you value experimenting, the folks next to you may think experimenting ended in 1978 and anything that came from it is doctrine now.

Once you’ve got a good sense of what is important to you, compare this to how the group actually functions (not just how they talk about functioning). How was a recent conflict handled? Who can block decisions, and how? How did it go for the last person who left the group? Who owns the land? It might be uncomfortable to have some of these conversations, but the view won’t go away after you’ve had them.

## Resources, Land, and Exit Terms

People tend to not take this into account enough. Whoever has the title to the land has a massive amount of power over the community. If the deed is in the founder's name, then they're really a landlord just with a philosophy. If it's in the name of three members out of twelve, then there's effectively two classes of members even if the literature says everyone's equal.

What legal form will hold the land: a cooperative, trust, association, company, or whatever fits your jurisdiction? Decide that before the land is bought. Then you need to decide voting rights and access/use rights for shared resources, and contribution accounting if you use it. Work out inheritance and outside debt, how improvements get handled, and what happens when somebody leaves. That’s a lot of deciding, I realize.

Visibility is important even in a money-free community. There are always scarce things and bottlenecks. It is important that people be able to know what the community owns or holds in common, what is set aside, what it owes to outsiders. Who has authority over distribution of major resources? Where are the conflicts of interest? Transfers of major resources (land, equipment, stores, outside cash, etc.) need to be recorded somewhere, and can't depend on one person's permission. It is also important to specify what rights people have to resources when they leave the community. If the community does not use money, it still needs to be able to provide people with resources when they leave (tools, transportation, temporary housing, stored goods, the right to keep using something, etc). Otherwise the "right" to leave is just something on paper that can only be exercised if there is money around.

Good people leave for good reasons, often due to a dying parent, love, work, health, exhaustion, or just a simple change of mind. Leaving should not require someone to leave everything they’ve put in, as that’s effectively coercing someone to stay. You should set the terms of letting someone leave when no one is leaving, and it’s just a theoretical discussion. Think of it as a pre-nup while you’re planning the wedding. It’s not that romantic, but it’s protective, and a lot easier than doing it when people are furious. For the whole sequence, see Diana Leafe Christian’s [*Creating a Life Together*](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Ecovillages_and_Low_Impact_Development/Creating_a_Life_Together-Practical_Tools_to_Grow_Ecovillages_and_Intentional_Communities.pdf): First you get the vision, then you get the members, then you pool the major resources, and lastly you get the land.

## From Visitor to Member—Slowly

When [The Farm](https://thefarmcommunity.com/) grew fast, a lot of the people who came were in need, and there weren’t many filters that worked. Being open to everybody felt so spiritual! The practical burden piled up anyway. A membership process is there to protect both the people who are already there and the newcomers. It’s no good for anyone to join a group that can’t deliver what it promised.

There’s some correspondence first. Did they read the materials? Do they get the basic idea of why this community is here? Are they at least starting to do the practices? It’s better to just find out that way than after you spend six months together, right?

The short visit: If you go for a week or two and just participate in normal life, well, most people can be charming for two weeks, so if that's all you do, congratulations, you've established that they can be charming for two weeks. 😅

The long visit: If you're not around long enough to actually experience any inconvenience or conflict with the person in question, then you're not going to learn very much about them. Watch how they react when tired, corrected, attracted, disappointed, bored, etc. Even [a professional faker](http://sidney.u-dont-exist.com/) may be able to pull off an act for a few months if they're really motivated, so time gets you more evidence, not certainty.

Have you thought about these questions: How long will a provisional membership be? What will a provisional member be responsible for and what are their rights? How will a provisional member's membership be reviewed? How might a person leave provisional membership? Who will make the decision about a person becoming a full member? (It shouldn't be the person who recruited them.) What rights will a full member have in terms of the land, other shared resources, and their voice within the group? What will a member be responsible for over the long term? (These should be addressed in the agreements you draw up when you are first starting your group.)

How do you know? At each stage, always judge people based on their conduct. No matter how good it sounds when they talk about their spirituality or their therapy, don’t go by that. It’s too easy to go by vibes. It’s also easy to mistake people just doing what everyone else is doing for safety. Even giving it six months will not give you enough data to know if someone is a good person.

If someone will have a role in your group that has serious consequences, for example with kids, shared resources, medicine, vehicles or private records, you want to know how they handle that specific thing. You can do this by asking if you’ve seen them do this thing before, and asking for references if appropriate. Give people access to these things in stages and ensure there’s always some way for someone to appeal to a separate authority if needed.

Do stuff together prior to making them provisional members. Can you actually make a decision as a group? Can you actually work together to get something done (an ordinary work or shared-resource task will do)? Can you even process a small conflict afterwards? Do a trial run like that before people have to sell their houses to join. It won't show you anyone's soul, but it can show you whether your way of coordinating works.

Do visitors, volunteers, workers, renters and interns at a commune need some rights even if they're not "in" the commune politically? When decisions really affect their pay or use of resources, they need clear terms and a way to be heard, and those decisions have to be open to review. Also when decisions really affect their schedule, safety, housing, records, discipline, etc. Equality isn't just for members, or else there's a risk of creating servant classes from commune to commune.

## When Somebody Has to Go

Even in a safe community it’s ok to kick people out if they’re a serious or general danger. If someone won’t follow basic rules and boundaries, and won’t stop, and/or is just not someone people can live with, it’s okay to eject them. If the community feels like they can do restorative work with them in a reasonable amount of time that’s cool, but if they are a danger to the community, the community doesn’t have to provide them treatment.

That said, the power to remove somebody is also one of the easiest powers to abuse. So work out the process before it's about anyone in particular. What constitutes a reason for immediate protective action, and who decides on temporary restrictions? How do you preserve the evidence and make sure that people who are involved in it aren’t participating in the decision? How does the person reply, and appeal the decision, without endangering others? How do people who are separated get housed, have their records and family connections protected, and still be able to use common resources or have access to outside funds? When do people reach for outside help?

'Outside' doesn't have to be a nation-state. (It's true that I've been kinda assuming that in [my research](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/).) All that's really needed is that there be a competent layer that isn't reviewing its own conduct. That can be another community, a federation, a professional, a court, a regulatory agency, an emergency service, or anything else. The Zapatistas have been very important in opening my eyes to the possibilities of autonomous regional institutions, which can review local power without becoming the state. But that doesn't mean that they can violate non-waivable individual rights, or that there can't be jurisdiction above them.

Why should a mediator or ombud be chosen beforehand? Because if they are chosen in a crisis, and if the parties have not met this “neutral stranger” before, they are unlikely to trust them. And remember that although mediation can help restore good relations, it does not replace the need for evidence and safeguarding and, if necessary, a ruling. And it does not replace the finality of separation if one party no longer consents to staying.
