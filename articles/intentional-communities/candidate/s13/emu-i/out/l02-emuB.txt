What about people who aren't really in the commune yet, politically speaking? Visitors, volunteers, workers, renters, even interns! (Yes, interns.) As long as commune decisions may really affect them (regarding pay, access to common stuff, etc.), they should get to know the terms involved and have a say that can be reviewed. They also should have some say in anything else that really affects them (schedule, safety, housing, records, discipline, etc.) Communes should follow the equality guideline even for these people, to avoid creating a class of servants who float from commune to commune.

Preparing for When Somebody Has to Go

Although no community can promise it will be safe for everybody, sometimes it's right to throw somebody out -- either because they're generally or specifically a bad risk, or because they won't stop violating some essential limitation, or for any of a number of reasons that make it impossible to live with them. (If some restorative work with them could resolve the situation in a reasonable amount of time, great! But if they're dangerous, it's not the community's job to be their treatment program.)

Since this power can easily be abused, though, it's a good idea to prepare ahead for dealing with it. What might cause the community to have to act immediately to protect people from somebody? Who will decide whether to impose temporary restrictions on the person? Who will collect and preserve evidence? Who should stay out of these decisions? How will the person be able to reply to the charges against them? How can the decision be reviewed or appealed later, without creating danger? How will the person's interests be protected regarding housing, records, family, access to common stuff, outside money, etc., during the separation? When will the community seek outside help, to be discussed next?
