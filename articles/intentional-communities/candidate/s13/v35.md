# Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community

Don't worry about getting land yet. If you've got a small handful of people who want to make a community but aren't sure yet, you are already a community, even if it's still an untested one. It may sound cheesy, but it's true all the same.

Also, don't even think about combining ownership (or any other big resource, for that matter) until you've been through at least one real conflict together and REALLY dealt with it. Not dealt with it by writing out a vision statement and shaking hands, or any other fake way of "resolving" things, like a truce nobody believes ("let's just leave each other alone"). But really, truly dealt with it. This includes the relational pl/ork of each of you naming what really went on, giving each of you time, figuring out what came up for each of you (jealousy, humiliation, fear, old material, whatever), and dealing with it until the emotional pressure changes enough that whatever agreement you come up with means anything. Until then, what's it worth?

Until that’s happened, you’re all still promising strangers, and you don’t want to take out a mortgage together yet. That’s okay! You can all meet in a rented or borrowed space, or at each other’s houses, long before anyone needs a land surveyor.

You will also want a budget of what you have in the way of resources. This is the boring stuff. But you will need to know what the group can offer in the way of water, housing, food stores, health care, tools, seasonal labor, transportation, access to markets, schooling, etc. Do you have outside debts to pay? How long will your cash reserves last, if the community uses them? What critical skills will you need? Being great at the emotional stuff won’t stop you from being dead in the water if the one person who knows where everything is and how it works gets too exhausted to keep going.

## Please Choose the Values Before Falling in Love With the View

Have you noticed people often choose their community in the same way they would choose a vacation rental? “Wow! Look at this beautiful land! And a pond, with the sunset in it!! Who needs a beach?! And the people seem really nice, let’s get in here!” Fast forward two years. “Wait, there’s a vaccination policy?? And you need paperwork for it?!?!”

Bring your values with you when you go to visit a community. Make a list of what you really believe about children, money, property ownership, romance, medicine, food, spiritual practice, technology, privacy, dealing with conflict, leaving the community, etc. It’s a long list, I know. Some of them will just get a question mark, if you’re not sure yet.

Sexuality and gender belong on there too. Would LGBTQ+ people be able to live openly in your community, date, be in relationships, and stay fully protected by the community? Should children be taught to question their sexual identities from a young age, and what happens if one of the community’s children comes out as gay or transgender? Until you answer these, your community’s values on freedom and on raising children aren’t complete. Yep. Both of them.

Are you willing to experiment? That’s a real value too. Put it on your list, because the people you live next to might think the experiment ended in 1978, and everything that came out of it is doctrine now.

Then don’t just listen to what the group says. Look at how they really operate. A recent conflict is a good place to start: how did they handle it? There’s also who can block decisions, and what happened to the last person who left. (And who owns the land!) Does that match your values? It’s uncomfortable to talk about these things, I know, but the view isn’t going anywhere.

## Resources, Land, and Exit Terms

Who will have title to the land? Very important! Whoever has it will have enormous power over the rest of the community. If the deed is in the name of the founder, then he or she is really a landlord with a philosophy. If three out of twelve members own everything, then whether or not your literature says everyone is equal, there are two classes of members.

What kind of legal entity (cooperative, trust, association, company, whatever fits your jurisdiction) will hold the land? This needs to be decided before it's bought. Then you'll need to decide who has what voting rights, who has what access and use rights to common resources, how you will account for people's contributions (if at all), how inheritance works, what about outside debt, how improvements get handled, and what happens when someone wants to leave the community. Lots of decisions to make!

And how will you handle the VISIBILITY of what the community has? There will be bottlenecks, sorry. It will be important for people to know what's owned or held in common, what you've set aside, what obligations you have to outsiders, who has authority to hand out the major resources, where conflicts of interest are, etc. It won't do to have the transfer of major resources (land, equipment, stores, outside cash, etc.) dependent on one person's memory or permission.

Good people leave for good reasons -- dying parent, love, work, health, exhaustion, change of mind, etc. You don't want them to lose everything they've put in. That's coercive -- they HAVE to stay if they can't afford to go. Whether or not you use money, you'll need to have a way of providing people with resources when they leave (cash, if you use it, but also tools, transportation, temporary housing, stored up goods, the right to keep using something, or whatever). Otherwise their "right" to leave is only on paper -- it only works when the community happens to have spare money. Write up the exit terms while leaving is still only theoretical -- like a pre-nup when you're planning the wedding. Not romantic, but protective. Much easier than doing it once anybody's furious.

Honestly? For the nuts and bolts of all this (members, resources, land, agreements, etc.), I'd go to Diana Leafe Christian's [*Creating a Life Together*](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Ecovillages_and_Low_Impact_Development/Creating_a_Life_Together-Practical_Tools_to_Grow_Ecovillages_and_Intentional_Communities.pdf) (on the reading list at the end).

## From Visitor to Member—Slowly

When [The Farm](https://thefarmcommunity.com/) grew fast, a lot of the people who came were in need, and there weren’t many filters that worked. Being open to everybody felt so spiritual! The practical burden piled up anyway. A membership process is there to protect both the people who are already there and the newcomers.

First, correspond with them. Did they read the materials? (Not everyone does.) Do they get the basic idea of why this community is here? Are they at least starting to do the relevant practices? It’s a lot cheaper to find out through a thoughtful exchange than after six months of misunderstanding, right?

The short visit: If they come for a week or two and just participate in normal life, well, most people can be charming for two weeks. Not exactly a high bar. 😅 So that's about all you'll find out.

The long visit: If you're not around long enough to actually experience inconvenience and conflict with the person in question, then you're not going to learn very much about them. Watch how they react when tired, corrected, attracted, disappointed, bored, etc. (Hopefully not all at once!) Even [a professional faker](http://sidney.u-dont-exist.com/) may be able to pull off an act for months.

Write down your answers to these questions: How long will a provisional membership be? What will a provisional member be responsible for and what are their rights? How will a provisional member's membership be reviewed? How might a person leave provisional membership? Who will make the decision about a person becoming a full member? (Hint: not the person who recruited them! 😜) What rights will a full member have in terms of the land, other shared resources, and their voice within the group? What will a member be responsible for over the long term? (A full member's rights and responsibilities should be addressed in your founding agreements.)

At each stage, always judge people based on their conduct. No matter how good it sounds when they talk about their spirituality or their therapy, don’t go by that. What do they actually do? It’s too easy to go by vibes. It’s also easy to think someone’s safe just because they’re going along with everyone else. Six months with someone will give you more data, but there’s still no “good person” detector.

If someone will have a role in your group that has serious consequences, involving kids, shared resources, medicine, vehicles or private records, you want to know how they handle that specific thing. I wouldn’t hand someone the van keys just because they’re great with kids. You can find out by checking whether you’ve seen them do this thing before, and asking for references if appropriate. Give people access to these things in stages and ensure there’s always some way to appeal decisions about these roles to a separate authority if needed.

Do stuff together prior to making them provisional members. (Sorry, hanging out doesn't count. I know, it's the fun part.) Can you actually make a decision as a group? Can you actually work together to get something done (an ordinary work or shared-resource task will do)? Can you even process a small conflict afterwards? Do a trial run like that before anybody sells their house to move in. It can show you whether your way of coordinating works.

Then there are the people who aren't fully "in" the commune politically. Visitors, volunteers, workers, renters and interns at the commune (yes, even interns) are entitled to some rights when decisions really affect their pay or access to common resources. Why wouldn't they be? All of them should have clear terms, and the chance to be heard and to have such decisions reviewed. The same is true for decisions really affecting their schedule, safety, housing, records or discipline. Equality that stops at the membership line can end up recreating a servant class that moves from commune to commune.

## When Somebody Has to Go

A safe community can’t promise to keep everyone. There may be times when someone has to go. Yeah, it’s tough. It’s also true. If someone is a serious or generalized danger to others, or keeps violating essential boundaries, or for whatever reason just makes everyday life together impossible for everyone else, it can be the right call to eject them. If restorative work can help in a reasonable amount of time, all the better. But it’s not the community’s job to be a treatment program for someone who’s endangering everyone else.

This power to remove people is so easily abused, though. So work out the process before it's about anyone in particular. What counts as cause for immediate protective action? Who decides to put temporary restrictions on someone? Who will make sure all the evidence is preserved? Who will recuse themselves? How will the person be able to reply to the concerns, and have the decision reviewed or appealed without putting others in danger? What protections will there be for the person’s interests (housing, records, family contact, access to common resources, outside funds if any) during the separation? When is it time to bring in outside help? (More on that next.)

Note that "outside" doesn't have to mean the nation-state. [My research](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/) initially leaned too far that way. The basic need here is for a competent level of authority that is not reviewing its own conduct. That could mean another community, a federation of communities, professionals, autonomous regional institutions, courts, regulatory agencies, emergency services, etc. The key is that it's not the same people. An important correction that came from the Zapatistas: that authority can be an autonomous regional institution, without it turning into the state. But when power had crept up in their own system over the years, it took them a long review to push it back down. And no, autonomy still doesn't mean anyone gets to decide that individual people don't have certain non-waivable rights, or that outside jurisdiction just goes away.

Also decide on a mediator or ombudsperson in advance. Why bother? People often won't trust someone they haven't already met, who comes in during a crisis as a "neutral stranger." A mediator doesn't obviate the need for evidence, safeguarding, rulings, etc. Mediation is an attempt to restore good relations between people. When people can't or won't consent to being in community any more, they still need a final separation.
