# Your Little Forming Group Already Is the Community

Don’t get land until there’s a community. The small group that’s still deciding whether to build one already is the first community.

Don’t combine ownership and major resources without going through at least one real conflict together, start to finish.

No writing a vision statement and shaking hands on it. No truce that nobody believes, either. Actually go through the conflict, emotionally, together. Name what happened and give each person time. Find out what old material is getting touched for each of them, and where the jealousy, humiliation or fear is. Keep at it until the emotional pressure has changed enough for an agreement to mean something.

Until that’s happened, the group is still a circle of promising strangers, and I wouldn’t ask that circle to take on a mortgage yet.

In the meantime there’s plenty to do. Meet in a rented space, a borrowed room or someone’s house, and find out how the group handles conflict. Hiring a surveyor comes after that.

Also budget for the practical side: water, housing, food stores and health care. Then there are tools, seasonal labor, transport and schooling, and access to markets. If the community uses money, add outside debts and how long the cash will last. And figure out the few skills that would stop everything if nobody had them. A group can be great at dealing with each other emotionally and still fall apart if the well dries up, or if the one exhausted person who knows where everything is and how it works collapses.

## Please Choose the Values Before Falling in Love With the View

People often pick a community the way they’d pick a vacation rental: the land is gorgeous, the sunset shows up in the pond, and everybody seemed really nice when you visited.

The pond is what you fall for on the first date. The vaccination policy shows up two years later.

Before you visit a community, have a list of your values ready to go. If you’re unsure where you stand on something, note that too. Some important areas are children, money, property ownership, romance, medicine, food, spiritual practice, technology, privacy, conflict and leaving.

Add sexuality and gender to the list. Could an LGBTQ+ member live openly, have relationships and stay fully protected by the community? Should kids be taught to question their sexual identities at an early age? What will the community do if one of its kids grows up gay or trans? If a group hasn’t answered these yet, it hasn’t finished working out its values on freedom or on raising kids, either.

Being willing to experiment is a real value, but you have to declare it, because the person next to you may think the experiment ended in 1978 and the results are doctrine now.

Then compare your values with what the group actually does, not just its public language. Ask how they handled a recent conflict, and who can block a decision. Ask what happened to the last person who left, and who owns the land. The view will still be there after you’ve asked.

## Resources, Land, and Exit Terms

Whoever owns the land has a lot of power over the community.

If the deed is in the founder’s name, the founder is basically a landlord with a philosophy. If it’s in the names of three members out of twelve, you have two classes of members, whatever the welcome packet says about everyone being equal.

Decide on a legal form before the land is bought: a cooperative, trust, association, company, or whatever fits your jurisdiction. Then decide voting rights, and the access and use rights to shared resources. Decide whether contributions get counted, what happens when someone dies, and how you handle outside debt and improvements. And decide what happens when somebody leaves.

Even a money-free community has scarce things and bottlenecks, so make the material side visible. Members ought to be able to see what the community owns or holds in common, what’s set aside, and what it owes outside. They should know who can hand out major resources and where the conflicts of interest are. Big transfers of land, equipment, food stores or outside cash shouldn’t depend on one person’s memory or say-so. And people ought to be able to leave with something, in whatever form makes sense: cash if the community uses it, but also tools, transport, temporary housing, stored food or the right to keep using something. Otherwise the right to leave only works on paper, when there’s money to spare.

Good people leave for good reasons. A parent is dying, or they’ve fallen in love, or it’s work, their health, exhaustion, or they’ve simply changed their mind. If leaving means giving up everything they put in, staying becomes coercive.

So write the exit terms while leaving is still hypothetical. It’s the community version of a prenup, worked out while you’re planning the wedding. That’s unromantic, but it protects people, and it’s much easier before anybody is furious.

Diana Leafe Christian’s [*Creating a Life Together*](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Ecovillages_and_Low_Impact_Development/Creating_a_Life_Together-Practical_Tools_to_Grow_Ecovillages_and_Intentional_Communities.pdf) has the whole sequence: sort out your vision before you bring in members, membership before you pool major resources, and major resources before you buy land.

## From Visitor to Member—Slowly

[The Farm](https://thefarmcommunity.com/) grew fast. A lot of the people who came were in need, and there weren’t many filters that worked. Being open to everybody felt spiritual, but the practical burden piled up anyway.

A membership process protects the people who are already there and the newcomers too. Nobody benefits from joining a group that can’t deliver what it promised.

Correspond first. Have they read the materials? Do they understand why the community exists, and have they started the practices? Writing back and forth costs a lot less than six months of misunderstanding.

Short visit: a week or two of ordinary life with the group. Most people can be charming for two weeks, so congrats, you’ve found out they can be charming for two weeks. 😅

Long visit: stay long enough for the inconvenience and the conflicts to show up. Watch how they react when they’re tired or corrected, attracted or disappointed, or just bored. Even [a professional faker](http://sidney.u-dont-exist.com/) can sometimes keep it up for months, so time gets you more evidence, not certainty.

Provisional membership: decide how long it lasts, what rights and responsibilities come with it, how the review works and how they’d leave. The person who recruited them shouldn’t get to decide.

Full membership: rights to the land and shared resources, a voice, and long-term obligations, whatever your founding agreements say.

At each stage, judge people on what they actually do, not on how spiritual or therapeutic they sound. Vibes are easy to read wrong, and so is conformity, which can pass for safety. Six months gives you a bigger sample, but it won’t tell you who’s a good person. If someone’s going to have a role with real consequences, with children, shared resources, medicine, vehicles or private records, you want evidence of how they handle that specific thing. Get references where it makes sense, give access in stages, and make sure there’s an independent way to challenge the decision.

Before anyone becomes a provisional member, do more than hang out. Make one real group decision together, do one ordinary work or shared-resource task, and process one small conflict afterward. A trial run like that won’t show you anyone’s soul, but it shows whether your way of coordinating actually works, before anybody sells a house.

Visitors, volunteers, workers, renters and interns don’t need full political membership to have rights. If a decision really affects their pay or their access to shared resources, or their schedule, safety, housing, records or discipline, they need clear terms, a way to be heard, and a review. Equality that stops at the membership line can bring back a servant class between communes.

## When Somebody Has to Go

A safe community can’t promise to keep everyone. Sometimes somebody is a serious or general danger, keeps violating important boundaries, or makes ordinary life together impossible, and then ejecting them can be the right answer. Restorative work is useful if it can work in a reasonable amount of time. But if someone is endangering everyone else, nobody has a duty to turn the commune into their treatment program.

The power to remove somebody is also one of the easiest powers to abuse. So work out the process before it's about anyone in particular. Decide what behavior calls for protective action right away, and who preserves the evidence. Decide who steps aside because they’re too close, and who decides on temporary restrictions. The person needs a way to reply, and there should be a review or appeal that doesn’t put others in danger. While they’re separated, protect their housing, their records and their contact with family, and their access to common resources or outside funds if there are any. And decide when to call for outside help.

That last one is trickier than it looks. “Outside” doesn’t always mean the nation-state, and [my research](https://innerself.185-233-106-15.sslip.io/blog/commune-article-research/) leaned too far that way at first. What matters is a competent layer that isn’t reviewing its own conduct. That could be another community, a federation or a professional. It could be an autonomous regional institution, or a court, a regulator or an emergency service. The Zapatistas showed me that autonomous institutions can review local power without becoming the state. But autonomy doesn’t give anyone permission to erase individual rights that can’t be waived, and it doesn’t make outside jurisdiction disappear.

Also pick a mediator or ombud ahead of time. People who meet the “neutral stranger” for the first time on the day things explode often don’t trust them. Mediation is for repair. It doesn’t replace evidence, safeguarding or a ruling, and it can’t stand in for a final separation once consent is gone.
