# Sections, published and rewritten

====================
SECTION: The Math of Absorption, and Who This Isn’t For

PUBLISHED:

This article asks people to discuss jealousy, God, death, childhood, money, sex, psychedelics, and what happens when somebody wants to leave. Many perfectly intelligent adults would rather discuss solar panels for six hours.

I understand why. Modern life trains us to perform competence while hiding whatever might complicate the performance. Screens let us disappear from discomfort instantly. Institutions reward narrow skills and then act surprised when nobody can sit through a conflict without appointing a subcommittee or blocking somebody.

So the question isn’t whether a person is “good enough” for community. The practical question is how much attention they currently have available for other people, how willing they are to examine their own reactions, and whether the group has enough capacity to support them without being consumed.

Some people have years of contemplative or therapeutic practice. Some got unusually lucky in childhood. Some are actively surviving an emergency and need care more than responsibility. Most of us move between those positions over a lifetime.

In this model, the community shares some functions that modern society assigns to therapists. [Peer counseling](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/hearthwork-an-integrated-peer-counseling) distributes the capacity to listen, accompany, and help people process ordinary emotional material.

That does not make the community infinitely absorbent.

I borrow the phrase [*free resource*](https://www.rc.org/) from Re-evaluation Counseling. It means the attention, steadiness, skill, and practical capacity a person has available after dealing with enough of their own immediate material. *Need* is what currently requires support.

Everyone carries both, and the ratio changes.

My own need has exceeded my free resource at many points. Good Lord. During one stretch, my first wife tried to kill me while psychotic because she believed I was her sociopathic father. Then [she took my son](https://youtu.be/0eOInpVYnsk?si=dbxjP76Rbk5FmjDs). Later she tried to kill my mother, while her pimp stalked my sister’s family until they moved to Canada.

I could continue, but the arithmetic is already visible. I had very little left for anybody else. When I say a person may need to stabilize and do inner pl/ork before helping found a community, I’m not speaking from a mountaintop.

A group can absorb only as much need as its members have free resource to meet. Eight people with two in sustained crisis may spend most of their attention on emergency care. Eighty people can often distribute the same need across many relationships and practical roles.

The ability to welcome somebody is therefore partly a resource question rather than a measure of compassion. Saying yes beyond capacity can injure the existing members and the person who was promised support.

The difficult paradox is that small communities need members most and can absorb disruption least. Eight people still need food grown, equipment repaired, accounts kept, children cared for, and the water system working. The well does not become more understanding because the new member has a beautiful heart.

So early members usually need more available resource than acute need, plus practical skills and a willingness to learn.

I still don’t know how to keep this language of capacity from becoming a respectable way to exclude people who are disabled, poor, less articulate, unfamiliar with therapeutic language, or simply inconvenient. Pretending capacity is unlimited won’t solve that. Any real process would need concrete criteria, outside review, and a way to revisit a “not now” decision. Even then, the power remains dangerous.

This community may not be right for you *right now* if:

You are fleeing an active crisis and mainly need safety, treatment, housing, or legal help. A founding group should not pretend it can replace those.

You want the feeling of village life without responsibility for governance, maintenance, conflict, and care.

You are unwilling to examine your behavior when jealousy or conflict appears.

Your current need greatly exceeds your available resource. That says nothing final about your worth. It describes timing. The first step may be building support and capacity where you already are.

A humane membership process should be able to say “later,” “in another role,” or “with more support” instead of converting every limit into rejection.

REWRITE:

Many perfectly intelligent grownups would rather sit around for six hours talking about solar panels than talk about jealousy, God, death, childhood, money, sex, psychedelics, or somebody wanting to leave, which is what I ask people to do in this article.

This does not surprise me, since we are taught to put on a show of competence in modern society. We have a lot of incentives to hide ourselves in our lives and disappear behind our screens the second things get uncomfortable. We have been encouraged by our institutions to be narrow in our skills, and then they act surprised when nobody can sit through a conflict without appointing a subcommittee or blocking somebody.

So the question isn’t whether a person is “good enough” for community. The practical question is how much attention they currently have available for other people, how willing they are to examine their own reactions, and whether the group has enough capacity to support them without being consumed.

Some people have engaged in long practices of contemplation or therapy, or have had very good luck in childhood; other people are just trying to survive some emergency in their life and need care more than responsibility. Most of us are all of these at different times in our lives.

In the model I’m imagining, some of the functions that are outsourced to therapists in modern society are distributed throughout the community. In other words, there’s a lot of [peer counseling](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/hearthwork-an-integrated-peer-counseling) going on, people listening to each other, being there for each other and helping each other process ordinary emotional stuff.

That does not make the community infinitely absorbent.

I’m going to use the phrase “[free resource](https://www.rc.org/),” which comes from Re-evaluation Counseling, to refer to the attention, steadiness, skill and practical capacity people have left over for others once they’ve dealt with enough of their own immediate stuff, and “need” to refer to the amount of help people need at a given time.

Everyone has a certain amount of both, and the ratio varies.

My own need has exceeded my free resource at many points. Good Lord. During one stretch, my first wife tried to kill me while psychotic because she believed I was her sociopathic father. Then [she took my son](https://youtu.be/0eOInpVYnsk?si=dbxjP76Rbk5FmjDs). Later she tried to kill my mother, while her pimp stalked my sister’s family until they moved to Canada.

I could go on and on, but the math is pretty clear by now. There wasn’t much of me left over for anybody else. So when I say someone might need to stabilize and do inner pl/ork before helping found a community, I’m not saying it from a mountaintop.

The problem is that, in a group of eight people, if two are in a sustained crisis mode, the group may end up spending most of its attention caring for those in crisis. However, in a group of eighty people, the same need, or even more of it, can often be spread across lots of relationships and roles. A group can only absorb as much need as its members have free resource to meet.

The ability to welcome somebody is partly a problem of resources, then, not compassion -- how many people can a group take in without hurting the existing members or the people they are taking in?

There is a difficult paradox to consider. The need for new members in a small community is greater, but the ability of that community to tolerate the disruption of new members is less. With only eight people in the community, someone still has to grow the food, fix the cars, balance the books, watch the kids, and maintain the water system. The water well doesn’t care how beautiful someone’s heart is.

So early on, members usually need to have more available resource than acute need, and some practical skills to contribute, and a willingness to learn.

I still don’t know how to keep this language of capacity from becoming a respectable way to exclude people who are disabled, poor, less articulate, unfamiliar with therapeutic language, or simply inconvenient. Pretending capacity is unlimited won’t solve that. Any real process would need concrete criteria, review by someone who wasn’t part of the decision, and a way to revisit a “not now” decision. Even then, the power to veto membership applications based on capacity remains dangerous.

This community may not be a good fit for you *right now* if:

You’re running from some crisis right now and mainly need a place to land, get treatment, housing, legal aid, etc. (A founding group shouldn’t pretend it can give you those.)

You want the feeling of village life but don’t want to take on governance, maintenance, conflict, care, etc.

You’re not willing to look at yourself when jealousy or conflict comes up.

You have a need that far outstrips your free resource right now. (This doesn’t mean you’re bad or doomed, it’s about timing; it may mean you need to take a first step of finding support where you are and building up your resources.)

Any humane membership process should have ways of not flat-out rejecting people who fall into these categories, like saying “later,” or “in another role,” or “with more support.”
