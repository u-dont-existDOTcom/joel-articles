# Communes Are Hot Again 🔥

The longing that shaped my father’s film is suddenly everywhere again. The U.S. Surgeon General’s 2023 advisory reported that roughly half of American adults experience loneliness and treated social connection as a public-health issue rather than a sentimental extra.

I’m less interested in declaring an official “epidemic” than in the obvious fact underneath it: a lot of people can’t tolerate isolated modern life anymore. They may disagree about politics, food, God, sex, and whether shoes are oppressive, but they agree that something about the current arrangement is starving them.

Look at Google Trends for “ecovillage” and “intentional community.” Interest had been drifting downward for years, then bent upward sharply in the mid-2020s.

image 4

I can’t prove AI caused that turn. The timing is still hard to ignore. Around the same period, AI stopped being a tech-news curiosity and began reorganizing people’s jobs, feeds, relationships, and expectations of the future. Suddenly “maybe we should form a village” sounded less like a 1972 leftover and more like a backup plan.

It’s poppin’ on Instagram too! 🔥 Chantress Seba—my favorite light-language love influencer—posted an application for people interested in starting a commune. I applied, although I don’t think she’s coming to Africa. When I saw the post, many thousands of people appeared to be interested. Imagine opening your inbox after casually inviting a million followers to co-found a village.

Another large influencer later floated the commune idea, and the comments began turning into a recruitment board. People were finding prospective members under a post the way they might normally find a used sofa. It was chaotic, sincere, and revealing.

### The Freeloader Problem

Not everybody in the comments was trying to join. The oldest objection arrived almost immediately: maybe most people don’t want the responsibility anarchism requires, and communal property becomes “owned by everyone, cared for by nobody.”

image 5

The commune comments became a miniature political-philosophy seminar, as Instagram comments sometimes do.

Although that comment applies more to large socialism experiments than small communes, that objection deserves a serious answer. Some people really would rather let somebody else take responsibility. Shared ownership can blur responsibility until the same three people maintain everything while everybody else discusses freedom. Informal leaders then gain power partly because everyone keeps handing it to them.

Communal ownership therefore needs named stewardship, visible duties, consequences for chronic freeloading, and enough relational capacity to confront the problem before resentment becomes the real government. A community cannot run on the assumption that removing bosses removes passivity, selfishness, theft, or learned helplessness.

The desire to return to a natural way of living together has outrun the knowledge. People know they’re lonely and exhausted. They usually don’t know why so many earlier communities failed, why the same conflicts keep returning, or how quickly beautiful land becomes irrelevant once money, children, jealousy, and ownership enter the picture.

I’m not currently recruiting anyone or founding a community. That makes this easier to write because I’m not trying to get you onto my land. I do plan to buy land, and people may naturally end up living around me. Every warning in this article applies to me too.

# Why Communities Keep Dying in the Same Two Ways

Most communes of my acquaintance traded inner freedom of the heart (spirituality) for outer freedom (secular, nondogmatic, political). The two pendulum ends of this trade-off mirror each other. I believe we can combine the best of both, but first, let’s see how each fails individually.

## Outer-freedom-only models

The secular communes (and all the manuals I’ve seen on building them) had a solution for how to distribute property or labor in a way folks could agree on, but when the deep waters of jealousy or childhood hurt or humiliation or attraction or resentment or grief came up, the group was without a common method for dealing with it.

Eventually, when people are together long enough, politics will become a way of carrying emotional material that people can’t name. There will be a fight about a schedule or committee or noise or food or some policy or other, but it won’t be about that. There’s no shared way to talk about the hurt itself, but the meeting agenda is the one place the group lets you push for something and win, so the angry communard goes there, trying to get some justice.

## Spiritual dogma models attempting inner freedom

Many people have already realized that they are more interested in spiritual (or emotional) liberation than political liberation, so they tend to congregate in communities built for that. But because these communes focus so much on the inner work that the founder believes in, it can often look like some kind of prison-monastery cult, where you feel free inside as long as you don’t question anything.

The Farm, in Tennessee, at its largest about 1,500 people, had many beautiful values. But in the early days, the community’s spirituality and social life followed the founder, Stephen Gaskin. From what former members have shared, the community’s doctrine ran deep into members’ intimate decisions. This is how it often goes: folks talk about oneness with the universe, while one man defines what the universe is.

## We Want Freedom Inside & Out!

Let’s face it: food, housing, and logistics are not enough reason to bind whole lives together. That’s more like a situationship. A community needs a shared sacred inner practice that gives the difficulty of our life together a deeper meaning. We help each other heal and choose love over fear. And we turn outward with a purpose, using the escuelita model to bring our healing to the world.

I dream of a way to work in depth that isn’t run by a guru, and a way of being in a community with distributed authority. A place where people who’ve done the healing first can join without pretending they’re fully finished, and that also has peer practice and enough play that it doesn’t become a permanent repair shop.

The motto would be: “Nothing suppressed, everything processed.” Each of us guides our own compatible practice, and our practices weave the community into the quilt that keeps us all warm and safe. Whatever answers we find stay fluid and organic, and don’t freeze into doctrine.

Speaking of dynamically weaving ourselves together, though, a community does need some stable agreements that they’re not tweaking and revising every time somebody has a revelation in breathwork. But these agreements need to stay open to lived experience.

When agreements get frozen, religious communities tend to call the frozen version doctrine. Secular communities, on the other hand, often call it “the process,” which can become just as sacred.

To give an example of striking the balance: My contemplative practice is grounded in the early Buddhist suttas, with a critical reading. Most are treasure, while some are obviously late additions (sometimes to the point of real absurdity). A living community should relate to its own teachings in that same critical manner while staying devoted to following what they’ve decided on so far.

# Practicing Community Can Start Now

I realize that for most people reading this there are a lot of reasons why they can’t just head off and go join or build community on some land. Jobs, kids, pets, debt, a mortgage, a sick parent, or several of these arranged in a circle... That’s fine! The most important pl/ork (play + work) doesn’t need land anyway.

The How-To Manual which follows this essay covers the whole model: inner, relational, and medicine pl/ork, the container that holds them, tech, jealousy, kids, money, land, founder power, how the communities spread, leaving, etc.

People sometimes buy forty acres and then bring all their unprocessed stuff with them to a more scenic location. It’s a more beautiful view for sure, but now you have the conflict PLUS goats. A group that practices together weekly for two years may never own land. So what? It’s already a real piece of community, and it can still make a difference when people are going through a divorce, an illness or grief. Parenting too, and plain old loneliness.

## Choose Values Before Falling in Love With the View

Have you noticed people often choose their community in the same way they would choose a vacation rental? “Wow! Look at this beautiful land! And a pond, with the sunset in it!! Who needs a beach?! And the people seem really nice, let’s get in here!” Fast forward two years. “Wait, there’s a vaccination policy?? And you need paperwork for it?!?!”

Bring your values with you when you go to visit a community. Make a list of what you really believe about children, money, property ownership, romance, medicine, food, spiritual practice, technology, privacy, dealing with conflict, leaving the community, etc. It’s a long list, I know. Some of them will just get a question mark, if you’re not sure yet.

Sexuality and gender belong on there too. Would LGBTQ+ people be able to live openly in your community, date, be in relationships, and stay fully protected by the community? Should children be taught to question their sexual identities from a young age, and what happens if one of the community’s children comes out as gay or transgender? Until you answer these, your community’s values on freedom and on raising children aren’t complete. Yep. Both of them.

Are you willing to experiment? That’s a real value too. Put it on your list, because the people you live next to might think the experiment ended in 1978, and everything that came out of it is doctrine now.
