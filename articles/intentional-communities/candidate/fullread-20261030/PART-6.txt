# The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together

image 7

Communities tend to solve whichever part most interests the founders. The political people write bylaws. The spiritual people meditate. The therapists process. The psychedelic people discover that everyone is God and forget to decide whose name goes on the deed.

I divide the model into four parts. Yes, four sounds suspiciously tidy for something involving jealousy, children, land, money, God, and iboga. Bear with me. I’ve published detailed guides for the first three—free, with no funnel and no retreat to buy. Most of this article is about the fourth.

Food, housing, and logistics are not enough reason to bind whole lives together. Shared inner practice gives the difficulty meaning: we are not merely maintaining property together, but helping one another heal and become less governed by fear. An outward escuelita and federation purpose adds another reason to keep going—the community exists not only to protect the people already inside it, but to help other people build.

## The Medicine Part - Yes, I’m Naming It

Most of the writing on communities hasn’t caught up with psychedelics. They’ve gone, or are going, from taboo toward regulated use for physical and mental health indications really fast as their benefits become more studied. And most intentional communities using psychedelics are still not public about it, due to possible trouble (even where the law allows some use) from parents worried about children, from neighbors imagining chaos, from insurance providers who might drop them, and from local officials who may not understand any of the distinctions.

I’m talking about psychedelics here because not to talk about it would be to falsify my model. Many of the people who read this article are already familiar with my writing about psychedelics, including my loveyhuasca/Haoma material.

And I believe there are real reasons to take psychedelics communally too - e.g. they can lower defensive barriers which otherwise dominate every serious conversation, and create strong bonds between members of the community who have been through the harder nights together.

### Integration Starts Well Before Ceremony

Using medicine is secondary to all of the other pl/ork that comes first: reparenting, developing somatic capacity, and learning to do peer counseling. The medicine can deepen the well you already learned how to dig, or it can show you a window into the future you want to see, but it’s not a simple shortcut.

This prerequisite also takes care of another issue in one shot: it weeds out the experience collectors. If someone is unwilling to put in 6 months learning to listen without imposing their solution, it is unlikely they will come out of a ceremony more relationally mature, regardless of how many self-transforming fractalized machine elves told them they are the reincarnation of King David & Elvis.😂

Integration is a term thrown around so much that I’m not sure if it still has meaning. But basically, post-ceremony integration is your everyday life. The ceremony is supposed to open something up in you. Look at your life in the weeks after the ceremony and see how things have changed. That includes your relationships, behaviors, sleeping habits, your decision-making, and perhaps most importantly, how well you can deal with frustration without declaring a new spiritual emergency. 😂

As a community, you need to write down what is and isn’t allowed, and which activities are private (to the individual, to a group in the community, or to the community vs the outside).

### Community Is What Makes Psychedelics Pl/ork Best!

- **Part of it is integration:** an insight needs somewhere to land. What happens when you come back from an amazing experience which gave you insight and clarity about your life, into the same old environment where nobody knows what you’ve been through and you’re faced with the same old cues? It can be super helpful to have gone through the experience in a community, where the people who held you through it can also notice what happens to you over the following months. - **The deeper you go, the more alienated you can feel.** After my original ayahuasca ego deaths at age 25, I had trouble finding anyone in the physical world who seemed to have experienced anything similar. I couldn’t even find them at Buddhist monasteries despite all the preaching about enlightenment. That helped give me a God complex, a Savior complex, or both, which then attracted exactly the right people to show me my limits. 😂 I often feel like if I’d gone thru that in a community with others, it would have been much more of a healthy and durable transformation, although without the challenges I guess we don’t learn as much also… - **Do you trust the people you’re relying on for support?** Support from people you trust is different from paid support, which is often paying people you don’t know to look after you for a night. To me it seems way too much of a gamble if I have a better alternative.

In a community, your sober sitter could ideally be someone who’s known you for years. Someone who not only watches you based on a safety protocol, but someone you know cares about you. They not only want the best for you altruistically, but because they have to live with the version of you that emerges 😊. This is especially important when doing iboga or other long and medically risky experiences. For those you need to be properly screened beforehand and monitored continuously.

Your peers can also reality check any downloads you may have had. Psychedelics can give you insight and they can give you convincing nonsense, all in the same bright shining light. A few friends who know your history may notice the difference between new understanding and… mom. - One of the benefits of community is **rhythm**, and many traditional medicinal practices occur within ceremonies that have a communal schedule. The shared schedule of ceremonies, rather than each person doing ceremonies when they want, can serve to limit compulsive use and provide for integration time. - **There are the practical benefits:** logistics are easier when it comes to things like having childcare arranged by the group that parents are comfortable leaving their kid(s) with, not needing to drive away from the ceremony, having someone bring water to someone or just sit quietly with them while they come back to normal consciousness, etc. And if you scream in release, the neighbors and landlord don’t call 911. - **And then there will be people to play with!** Raves often let strangers fast-track intimacy (getting to a level of intimacy that usually comes after building trust), but imagine doing ecstatic music & dance in a community that already knows each other (and each other’s inconvenient habits). 💃🕺

### The Case Against Psychedelics

Physical and psychological negative effects of ayahuasca were common in the Global Ayahuasca Survey, although severe effects were far less common and context mattered. And these medicines can open things up faster than a person (or their group) can deal with. One of the best resources on this topic is the Challenging Psychedelic Experiences project.

Some people and communities should avoid them. Where access is ahead of competence, people will become casualties of enthusiasm. Is your community humble enough to say no? To pause? Using the medicine takes that. It also takes humility to refer people out, and admit when an experience did harm.

They do also attract people who are all for revelation and hate the follow-through, people who can collect 12 ceremonies and 6 origin stories and still can’t say sorry to their housemate. Any community based mainly around the peak experience is going to find out sooner or later that the insight doesn’t do the practical work for it. Sometimes the dishes need doing and a scared kid needs comforting.

### No Special Shamans

Traditional lineages usually disagree with me on this, and many ayahuasca tribes and Bwiti iboga communities believe the medicine should stay under trained lineage authority. I respect what those traditions have preserved, and I’ve learned some “dos & don’t dos” from their sensible practices and less sensible practices. For example, although I do highly value loving kindness meditation, inner child reparenting, and nibbana meditation before ceremony, during, and at all times, I notice a lot of people want their ceremony to look and feel like some kind of abstract mix of indigenous rituals & props to make it feel more authentic and sacred than their everyday life. I understand the benefit of setting aside one time and place for sacred experiences (the book, “God is Red” comes to mind), although the shadow side to that is making the spiritual seem like another world apart from your regular life, which is sort of opposite to integration.

And while I do think it’s valuable to have someone highly experienced in navigating the spirit worlds who is able to sense where you are in those and guide you, I also wouldn’t trust many of these shamans to guide me, considering it’s common to find that even the ones who can heal people very well may still be happy to send evil spirits to kill someone, if that hit job is lucrative. I also don’t trust their medical intuition in general, considering the Huni Kuin shamans are famous for claiming that all other ayahuasca shamans are giving people cancer by using the knotted B. caapi vine (since the knots remind them of tumors). They’re right though that modern folk do very often decide they’re ready to lead ceremonies long before they’re really trained and prepared. I’m sure I could still learn a lot from those traditions, and they will continue to develop their understandings as well.

My basic issue is with placing one charismatic person above everyone else’s direct experience. The facilitator on the retreat circuit may be wise. Or they may be someone who hasn’t been vetted by anybody, who isn’t accountable to anyone, maybe even a sexual predator, or financially dependent on people coming back to them for more feather-waving. And the more deeply someone is opening up, the more it costs not to personally know who’s running their ceremony.

Even in a culture of peer medicine, there could still be an informal “shaman”, someone everyone trusts and gradually stops questioning, even though nobody gave them the title. I don’t know yet how to avoid this. Things that might help would be to have rotation of roles, and public accountability. The four questions in “Founderism” below apply to these roles too.

Still, I want people to learn to guide themselves and care for each other.

### My Communal Ceremony Principles

I’ve written before about best psychedelic ceremony practices, from courses I took, but I will sum up here what’s most relevant to this article.

Just because it’s a peer-led ceremony doesn’t mean it’s ok to be casually irresponsible. In fact, since the authority is spread around, there needs to be more competence.

All of the “supports” are important: preparation, nutrition, screening for drug interactions, being disciplined about the dose, having the right kind of sober sitter present, and having a plan for dealing with emergencies. Those matter more than ceremony aesthetics. My current safety material is at Altered States Triage.

While there may be some pure learning that could come from having several altered members figure out what rules and boundaries are needed on the spot, it’s not something I recommend.

Also, I am especially cautious about medicines and protocols that I see as more destabilizing and/or presenting more of a medical burden. This includes some uses of ketamine and MDMA and bufo. My reasons are given in those articles, respectively, since they need more space.

### After the Psychedelic Calling

![A traveler in a cloak stands in a carved stone doorway, with a glowing world of giant mushrooms and sacred geometry behind them, looking out over a village at sunset where people share a meal and walk the paths.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5y74!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0844bbea-ba0a-4ff0-8b62-4e46426dae51_1672x941.png)

A lot of people feel the urge to form a village after going through some serious psychedelic and/or mystical experiences. Finding their people and helping others get to whatever just opened up is part of the same urge. That call may be real, but forming a community based on omnipotence is a terrible idea.

I’ve felt that urge myself. The problem is that it can come with grandiosity, and I’ve felt that too. You come back believing love can heal anyone, and then you meet people who don’t want to change, people who have some part of them that sincerely wants to change yet they keep choosing the opposite, and people in such a crisis that their need for help is more than your enthusiasm can hold.

Once the euphoria wears off and people have a more sober view of the call they feel, it can become useful. A community can be a place for people to train up in the practices, and sometimes go off and start another experiment. That answers the reasonable objection that communes are just privileged escape pods, which gets double dipped with the objection that psychedelics are also privileged escape pods (or, alternatively, as some say, “a vacation for the poor”).
