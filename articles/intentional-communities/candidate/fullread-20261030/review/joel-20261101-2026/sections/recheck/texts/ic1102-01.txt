The New Age May Dawn Suddenly

I just put a movie my father made in 1988 up on the Internet for the first time: *Where’s Utopia?* It was the result of years of work, traveling to all kinds of communes and communal experiments around the country, trying to answer a question that for him was primary: How do we live together without recreating the same loneliness, domination, and stupidity we were supposedly escaping?

He had already spent 17 years in the Bruderhof, a Christian community that doesn’t use money internally. They also accepted my dad as a Jewish atheist, making him a remarkably long-running doctrinal exception. In meditation there, he came to his own understanding that “God is the Universe, and the Universe is Love.”

This was the banner my mom painted that hung in our living room for decades. I will try to locate the actual photo.

He might have stayed, but the U.S. antiwar movement during Vietnam needed somebody who knew how to set up printing presses. He eventually founded the Aquarian Research Foundation—not a commune, but a nonprofit devoted to researching how the planet could have a positive future and publishing what it found. His conclusion was simple and enormous: human beings need small tribal communities again, because Civilization Is A Hoax, as I blogged about at the beginning of the Covid plandemic.

That’s where the ***escuelita*** in my title comes in. Escuelita means “little school.” I’m borrowing the word from the Zapatistas in Mexico, whose *Escuelita* had outsiders stay with Zapatista families for a while and learn self-government by taking part in everyday life. In the same way, people would live in the healing communities I’m proposing, to heal and to learn the relational and practical methods, so that some of them could eventually leave and set up another one somewhere else. Otherwise, we might have a lovely refuge for a few lucky souls, but we’re doing even less than a retreat center to heal the planet.

I spent much of my childhood being carried through Twin Oaks, Acorn, East Wind, Padanaram, Zendik, and many other communities. I was usually just a visitor’s kid. I could see how people lived, but I was never really part of the tribe. At the time it was mostly boring, with some adventures mixed in, but it means I’ve seen a lot of communities to compare against.

From 1999 to 2001, my family actually lived with the Love Israel family in Washington while escaping Y2K. In case you aren’t familiar with Y2K, it was the Year 2000 (represented as year 00) computer bug which some folks, like my dad especially, felt might be the end of civilization. We lived in a yurt. At Love Israel, I had my dream of picking all kinds of wild berries in nature come true. I was also lonely, bored, irritated by the cult programming, and missing my friends in Philadelphia.

I wanted to befriend the children there, but they treated me as an outsider. My mom never liked the place. The leader was also named Love Israel, and to me he seemed like the fakest person I’d ever met, complete with the expected God complex.

We spent a lot of time on our knees in the community garden growing food they could sell in town. The money was opaque and seemed to travel mostly upward toward Love. Meanwhile everybody sang, “Here in the garden, there’s nothing left to do-ooo-oo, but eat, sleep, and keep clean!” Again and again. 🌱

My father eventually gave the community his airplane. They sold it instead of using it. He felt tricked, began exposing what was happening, and then we were asked to leave.

The East Wind commune was almost the opposite way. There were maybe eighty people when I was there, enough to have a variety of personalities, small enough to actually make friends. I was happy there. I especially liked showering in a communal shower with both sexes present. I wonder if that still goes on at East Wind? That’s the sort of thing that doesn’t make it into the history books, and which takes some balls (no pun intended), considering how the wider society counts this as potentially damaging to children’s psyches, despite it being the way we’ve lived for thousands of years prior.

Even at East Wind, though, there was some political power usurpation under the surface. They had a nut butter factory that brought in a lot of their income, which seemed to make the woman running it very hard to question. Otherwise, I thought the community had a great social life, a nice way of distributing labor, and no obvious guru.

There were like eight people at Acorn when I was there, and no kids. I couldn’t tell whether it was a commune I might join or a group of people who were already friends, and who happened to live on some really beautiful land and be good at building cool stuff.

Twin Oaks was much bigger, like two or three hundred people at the time, and almost institutional. It was friendly and interesting, but I didn’t feel like I was in a tribe there. It was more like a tiny hippie town, but with a highly structured political system that didn’t seem intuitive for hippies.

Looking back, almost none of these communities had a communal therapy practice. People brought the usual human pain and suffering with them, and it all got translated into politics.

Which meant weekly meetings, run according to Robert’s Rules of Order, with enormous agendas, where most of the things you needed to talk about couldn’t be talked about, and you generally felt like you’d rather be sleeping. (Some people, including me, solved this problem by just going to sleep in the meeting and openly snoring.)

Zendik went in the other direction. They held a brutal community meeting that I’m still recovering from 35 years later. 🤣 Five-year-old me was put in the center and told I was bad because I hadn’t volunteered to help adults with whatever jobs they were doing. Apparently kindergarten had already failed to teach me labor consciousness.

But what I gathered from a bird’s eye view is that the secular communities often thought they could make everyone feel loved and respected by adopting optimal democratic procedures. The spiritual communities went deeper in search of real purpose, belonging, and divine connection, but often put one person in charge of everybody’s inner life. Most of my big little article here is me attempting to describe the missing bridge between those.
