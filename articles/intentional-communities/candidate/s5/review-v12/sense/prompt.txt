# The Four Parts That Have to Pl/ork Together

[an image: a diagram of a house: the roof says COMMUNITY; under it the line "nothing suppressed · everything processed · authority stays with the practitioner"; three pillars: INNER PL/ORK (reparenting, somatic regulation, contemplative practice), RELATIONAL PL/ORK (peer counseling, jealousy processing, honesty practices, conflict metabolism) and MEDICINE PL/ORK (peer-guided, no shamans, gated behind the other work); the base: THE CONTAINER (membership · land · money · governance · children · exit); and below it: "Skip a domain and the community falls apart along exactly that seam." (Joel sent it on 2026-10-02 23:49: "the actual article uses an image")]
Communities tend to focus on the aspect of their project that was most interesting to the original founders, for example political types getting super into writing by-laws, or spiritual types just meditating all the time, or therapists processing each other, or people who like psychedelics discovering they’re all God together and never getting to the point of deciding who will put their name on the deed.

I’ve broken the model I propose down into four parts. Obviously the details are complicated, and jealousy, children, money, land, God, and iboga don’t fit neatly into four boxes, but let’s go with it. I’ve written detailed guides on the first three already (free to read, not a sales pitch for a retreat or anything), but this article will be mostly about the fourth.

In a nutshell, there's a difference between working together just to provide food/housing/logistics, etc., and working together to support each other in an internal practice. Why would we commit our whole lives to each other if the only thing we have is the need to take care of property? When we're all helping each other heal and be less run by fear, etc, then the hard parts mean something, and it makes sense to bind our lives together. And the outward *escuelita* and federation purpose gives us even more reason to do so, because we're not only protecting each other, but helping others build as well.

## One principle runs through all of them

Nothing suppressed, everything processed. Each of us keeps authority over our own practice, and the community is the container. Whatever answers we find stay fluid and organic, and don't freeze into doctrine.

These four parts leak into one another almost immediately. Governance can end up slyly incorporating personal tensions like jealousy. Those who have positions with more control of shared resources will automatically be affected by that psychologically (power corrupts, as they say). Those who know medicine tend to have another type of god complex since they may make life or death decisions for people routinely. How folks choose to raise children can expose values the founders never discussed. And myriad other categories arise, of course.

Also, a note on agreements: a community does need some stable agreements that they're not tweaking and revising every time somebody has a revelation in breathwork. But these agreements need to stay open to lived experience.

When agreements get frozen, religious communities tend to call the frozen version doctrine. Secular communities on the other hand often call it “the process,” which can become just as sacred.

### 1. Inner pl/ork

Pl/ork means play + work. Calling therapy “inner work” forgets the inner child, who also wants to play.

Forgive the neologism. I hope *pl/ork* isn’t too corny. 🌽

Here’s an example: you teach your inner child geography, while s/he teaches you the world is a cat!

[an image]
[Inner-child reparenting](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/inner-child-self-love-tips-and-guided) means learning to identify as the best inner adult, which is comprised of the three inner adult roles: Nurturer, Protector, and Guide. The newly developed adult reparents their newly recognized inner child. The child receives all that love, protection, and guidance that you give it, and in this process actually gains more freedom to remain alive inside you.

And it's built on [somatic regulation](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/somatic-modalities-strategic-sequencing) first. Listening to the body's feelings before making a decision with the mind. And that actually symbolizes the adult/child relationship to me in some way as well.

My contemplative practice is grounded in the early [Buddhist suttas](https://suttacentral.net/), with a critical reading. Most are treasure, while some are obviously late additions (sometimes to the point of real absurdity). A living community should relate to its own teachings in that same critical manner while staying devoted to following what they've decided on so far.

### 2. Relational pl/ork

[Hearthwork](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/hearthwork-an-integrated-peer-counseling) distributes counseling among peers: equal time, attention without interpretation or fixing, and then you switch.

The idea is that the capacity to be there for people when they need help isn’t located in one professional or guru, or the founder of the group that everyone emotionally depends on, but in the community as a whole.

Another thing is that the person listening to you shouldn’t get power, just from that, over whether or not you can be a member of the group, whether or not you can work for the group, whether or not you can live there, what happens with your children, whether or not you can take part in the medicine work, whether or not you’re believed in a dispute, etc. Letting someone turn what they learned while caring for you into administrative power over you is just creating a new hierarchy.

Another thing is knowing which problems are beyond what peers can handle and need outside or expert help.

And another is to deal with jealousy and conflict while it's still between the people involved, before it gets put on the agenda (generally at the bottom of the agenda).

Also, read my [Romance Guide](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/romance-advice-i-wish-my-parents), because no matter how much your founding document says that you’re all beyond romance, you’re still gonna have dyads.

### 3. Medicine pl/ork

Doing serious plant medicines carefully with peer guidance without exalting any one person as a special shaman.

This doesn’t mean that a peer-led ceremony should be done without structure. Things like screening participants, making sure the sitters are sober, having a plan for dealing with emergencies, planning for integration, and other rules and boundaries need to be dealt with beforehand. While there may be some pure learning that could come from having several altered members figure out what rules and boundaries are needed on the spot, it’s not something I recommend.

### 4. The container

Membership, land, resources, governance, children, leaving, etc.

Personally, I’m aiming for communities with less and less money involved. I think the path there has three stages.

First there will be no money exchanged between members for basic community functions like food, housing, care, tools, education, transport etc. Members might have outside jobs, and they bring an agreed share of that income in. This is probably a good place to start if someone wants to create a community now and have it be realistic.

Later, all wages and other income earned by the members from work outside the community, or from business inside the community, will be paid into the common purse of the community. No member accumulates private wealth while a member of the community. Some version of this is how things have been done in the [Bruderhof](https://www.bruderhof.com/intentional-community) community for generations, and my father lived there for 17 years. It is also done, in a somewhat different way, at [Twin Oaks](https://www.twinoaks.org/about-twinoaks-community/about-income-sharing), and a number of other income-sharing communities. So a common purse isn’t just a theory.

Eventually, the community would also stop using money outside. People would meet their needs directly, or through exchange (be it trade, gift, work, obligation, or whatever). I couldn't find a well-documented modern community that has gone this far, and in fact, it may be impractical in some locations because of government requirements like taxes on land. So perhaps the practical end point here is a community that is nearly entirely moneyless, but has a small collective cash boundary on the outside.

None of this does away with scarcity or economic power. There are lots of areas where such power can be wielded: access to food storage, housing, tools, land, medicine, transportation, and the outside world’s currency, to name a few. Even in a moneyless community, someone can control what's scarce. Who decides how these things are distributed? What do members owe one another? What happens when someone wants to leave?

These are less exciting topics to discuss spiritually. It's rare to see someone come back from an ecstatic vision with news of how to revamp the community’s policies on provisional members, or how to divvy up assets with a departing member, or how to prevent one founder from having control over the land, or what to do when a child reports something serious. And if they do, it's still something that needs a sober thinking-through.

But no amount of gorgeous land will make up for the mess that is created when one person owns the land or has the final say about what will happen on it. And when members come on board faster than the group can support them, all the peer counseling in the world won't make up for it, any more than medicine makes up for the lack of a written exit agreement.

The first three parts are the inside of the container, and this part is the container itself.

If one of them gets skipped, it will need to be dealt with eventually, at high stress, usually after the group has invested land, labor, or other resources and everyone has an opinion on how things should be.

(heading) The Medicine Part, Without Pretending It Isn’t There

[1] If you went by most of what’s written about communities, you’d badly underestimate how quickly psychedelics have moved from taboo toward regulated use. [2] Australia’s authorized psychiatrists have had [limited access to psilocybin and MDMA](https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/explore-topic/mdma-and-psilocybine-hub) for specified conditions since 2023, and [Oregon’s licensed psilocybin service centers](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/ph/preventionwellness/pages/oregon-psilocybin-services.aspx) began opening that year. [3] In 2025, New Mexico enacted a [Medical Psilocybin Act](https://www.nmlegis.gov/Legislation/Legislation?Chamber=S&LegNo=219&LegType=B&year=25) and Colorado started licensing under a regulated natural-medicine system it created.

[4] It hasn't been a smooth ride, either, and it isn't inevitable. [5] In 2024, the US Food and Drug Administration issued a [Complete Response Letter](https://download.open.fda.gov/crl/CRL_NDA215455_20240808.pdf) for the submitted MDMA-assisted therapy application, declining to approve it and asking for more evidence. [6] Portugal often gets described, lazily, as a place where "they've legalized everything" -- which is false! [7] They've [decriminalized possessing small amounts for personal use](https://www.euda.europa.eu/system/files/publications/642/PolicyProfile_Portugal_WEB_Final_289201.pdf), but trafficking is still criminal.

[8] To be sure, the legality of psychedelics varies by country and by state. [9] It varies by substance, too. [10] And it can depend on a group’s religious status and on what exactly it does. [11] So if psychedelic medicine is going to be part of a community at all, that community should definitely get actual local legal advice.

[12] Most intentional communities that are using psychedelics still don't say so publicly, because even where the law allows some use there may be issues with parents worried about the children, or neighbors imagining chaos, or insurance disappearing, or a local official who doesn't understand any of the distinctions.

[13] I'm talking about it here because not to talk about it would be to falsify my model. [14] Many of the people who read this article are already familiar with my [writing about psychedelics](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/s/psychedelics), including my [loveyhuasca/Haoma material](https://soma.u-dont-exist.com/). [15] If this article is going to be honest, it has to talk about both the value and the risk.

[16] There’s a serious case against having medicine as part of a community. [17] Physical and psychological negative effects of ayahuasca were common in the [Global Ayahuasca Survey](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36962494/), although severe effects were far less common and context mattered. [18] And these medicines can open things up faster than a person (or their group) can deal with.

[19] They also attract people who love revelation but can’t stand the follow-through. [20] You can meet someone with 12 ceremonies and a pile of origin stories who still can’t apologize to a housemate. [21] Eventually, a community organized mainly around the peak experience learns that the practical work of living together is still there after the insight (somebody has to wash dishes and calm a frightened child).

[22] From what I’ve seen, the case for communal use is real too. [23] Psychedelics can loosen the defenses that otherwise take over every serious conversation. [24] People who sit through difficult nights together can end up powerfully bonded. [25] But psychedelics can also leave people confused or grandiose. [26] Some end up dependent. [27] And then there’s the mess between people. [28] Is the community around them mature enough to tell the difference?

[29] Community Is What Makes Psychedelics Pl/ork Best!

[30] Part of it is integration: an insight needs somewhere to land. [31] When you gain insight and clarity about your life, you come back to the same environment you were in before, with all the old cues still waiting, and no one knows what happened to you. [32] This is where it can be super helpful to have gone through the experience in a community, where the people who held you through it can also notice what happens to you in the months after.

[33] The deeper you go, the more alienated you can feel. [34] After my original ayahuasca ego deaths at 25, I had trouble finding anyone in the physical world who seemed to have experienced anything similar. [35] I couldn’t even find them at Buddhist monasteries despite all the preaching about enlightenment. [36] That helped give me a God complex, a Savior complex, or both, which then attracted exactly the right people to show me my limits. 😂

[37] And the people you’re relying on to support you, do you trust them? [38] Being supported by people you trust is different from the commercial alternative, which is often to pay people you don’t know to look after you for a night. [39] Sometimes they’re great, but sometimes the person with the feather has only known you for 4 hours and already has a cosmology they’re using to explain everything you say.

[40] In a community, though, your sober sitter may be someone who’s known you for years, and you feel the difference between being watched and being held. [41] That matters even more with iboga and other long, medically risky experiences, which require screening beforehand and continuous observation.

[42] Peers can reality-check the downloads too, since psychedelics produce genuine insight and convincing nonsense, and the two look alike (same lighting and everything). [43] Friends who know your history may notice which parts look like a new understanding and which look like your mother.

[44] And people living together fall into a rhythm. [45] Many traditional medicine systems hold ceremonies on communal timing, not one person’s appetite. [46] With a shared calendar, folks can cut down on compulsive repetition and leave time for integration.

[47] Logistics get easier in a community. [48] Having the group arrange childcare it can trust. [49] Not having to drive away from the ceremony. [50] Having people that can bring water, or sit quietly with somebody as they come back to normal consciousness.

[51] There will be people to play with! [52] Raves often let strangers skip to that part of intimacy where they feel close before there’s any trust between them. [53] Imagine doing ecstatic music and dance with people in a community who already know each other, inconvenient habits and all… 💃🕺

[54] But I won’t idealize psychedelics. [55] Some people and communities should avoid them. [56] Where access is ahead of know-how, people will become casualties of somebody’s enthusiasm. [57] Is your community humble enough to say no, or pause? [58] Using the medicine takes that. [59] It also takes humility to refer people out, and admit when an experience did harm.

(heading) My Ceremony Principles

[60] No special shamans, which traditional lineages usually disagree with me on. [61] Many ayahuasca and Bwiti communities believe the medicine should stay under the authority of whoever’s trained in the lineage. [62] And I’ve learned from those traditions myself. [63] I respect what they’ve preserved.

[64] My issue is with placing one charismatic person above everyone else’s direct experience. [65] The facilitator on the retreat circuit may be wise. [66] They may also be someone who hasn’t been vetted by anybody, who isn’t accountable to anyone, who is a sexual predator, who is financially dependent on people coming back to them specifically, or who is simply wrong. [67] And the more deeply someone is opening up, the more it costs not to know which one you’re getting.

[68] In a peer-led medicine culture, how do you keep an informal shaman from showing up anyway? [69] That’s the person everybody trusts and slowly stops questioning, even though nobody gave them the title. [70] I don’t know yet. [71] Rotating roles might help, and so might public accountability. [72] I don’t think either one fixes it all the way.

[73] Still, I want people to learn to guide themselves and care for each other, within the limits of their competence. [74] I’ve written more about [peer-held ceremonies](http://ceremonies.u-dont-exist.com/), and about [the best psychopath shaman I ever met](http://sidney.u-dont-exist.com/).

[75] Peer-led never means casual. [76] When authority is spread across a group, people need more competence, not less. [77] So there’s screening for medical and psychiatric contraindications, plus a review of medications and combinations. [78] You need an appropriate sober sitter. [79] And before anybody takes anything, it’s already settled what calls for professional or emergency care.

[80] Also, I’m especially cautious about medicines and protocols I consider more destabilizing or more of a medical burden. [81] That includes some uses of [ketamine and MDMA](http://mdmaket.u-dont-exist.com/) and [bufo](http://bufo.u-dont-exist.com/). [82] My reasons are in those articles, not smuggled into one sentence here.

[83] The medicine stays behind the other pl/ork. [84] Reparenting and somatic capacity come first. [85] Peer counseling does too. [86] Nobody gets months of honest relationship by pharmacological decree, but if a practice is already there, medicine can deepen it.

[87] Making that a prerequisite also keeps out experience collectors. [88] If someone won’t spend 6 months learning to listen without fixing, they’re unlikely to become more relationally mature because the visions were especially geometric.

[89] Integration is just regular life. [90] The ceremony opens something, and you find out in the weeks after whether anything changed. [91] Changes show up in relationships and daily behavior. [92] You’ll see them in sleep and decisions too. [93] Another sign is whether the person can tolerate frustration without declaring a new spiritual emergency.

[94] Supports matter. [95] By supports I mean preparation and nutrition. [96] I also mean interaction screening and dose discipline. [97] Plus an emergency plan, worked out ahead of time. [98] That all matters more than ceremony aesthetics. [99] My current safety material is in [Altered States Triage](https://badtrips.u-dont-exist.com/).

[100] Legal issues differ greatly, and are sometimes not well defined, so a community should write down where it stands: what’s allowed and what’s prohibited. [101] It should write down which activities are private and what’s actually insured. [102] And it should note which activities have had a legal review. [103] “I don’t think anybody here has ever heard of that” is information, but it’s not the same as a legal opinion.

(heading) After the Psychedelic Calling

[104] [an image] Quite a few people feel a strong urge to form a village after some deep psychedelic or mystical experiences. [105] They want to find their people, and to help others get to whatever just opened up for them.

[106] I know this urge. [107] And I know the grandiosity that can go along with it: you come back believing love can heal anyone. [108] And then you run into the people who don’t want to change. [109] And you run into the people who sincerely want to change, but who keep choosing the opposite. [110] And you run into the people whose crisis is more than your enthusiasm can hold.

[111] Yeah, the call may be real. [112] A community founded on omnipotence is a terrible idea.

[113] A group that begins in collective spiritual euphoria can recruit too quickly and promise too much, and only do the math on how much need it can absorb once several people already depend on it. [114] The sections on capacity and on membership, later on in this article, are partly there to protect the original generosity from that first rush.

[115] After the euphoria wears off and the call sobers up, it can become useful. [116] A community can be a training ground where people learn the practices and sometimes go off to start another experiment. [117] If people are training there so they can help others build communities like it, they’re building a genuine alternative, not just escaping society. [118] That answers the reasonable objection that communes are just privileged escape pods.
