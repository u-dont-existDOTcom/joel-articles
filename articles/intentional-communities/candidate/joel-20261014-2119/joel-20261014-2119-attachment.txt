
# Founderism: The Four Questions

In every community there was somebody who took the initiative to get it started. Somebody found the land, wrote the first documents, taught the method, recruited the first members, or kept things going when everybody else lost interest.

That person ends up with real influence, and pretending they don't just makes that influence harder to look at. When that power fails to get distributed as the conditions ripen for that, it's called founderism.

I've seen founderism in many communes that I've visited, and I can see it playing out if I were to be a founder as well. I've got all these proposals and visions here in my guides, and so it would be very easy for me to become the person who decides whether or not everyone else “understood” them correctly, even while I wish to remain humble and open to the input of all involved.

A few things help keep that in check. One is to judge the method by results people can actually see. Will they see the members safely flourishing without being trapped? Can conflicts get resolved? Are the kids doing well once they grow up? When the method gets a prediction wrong, can the community say so and change course? And if the community is meant to spread, do new projects actually start up and last? Those questions seem more important to me than how big or old the commune gets, or how many decisions are made by consensus.

Allow for parallel implementations. Fork the method. If the community finds a way to alter Hearthwork and get better results, they've taught me something. If every change they make still needs the founder's “blessing”, then it's not really running in parallel, is it?


The questions so far have been about the method. These four are about the founder:

Can the community respectfully override the founder? Can the community replace the role of the founder? Can the community alter the vision of the founder? Can the community exist without the founder?

Then follow the practical power. Whose name is on the deed? Who's in charge of the housing and the food stores, the vehicles and tools, and any bank account outside the community? Who keeps the records? Who decides who gets admitted, and who picks the people who review the founder? Who controls access to the kids, or to medicine, and who can make a complaint go away? The org chart might look flat and horizontal, while the keys, passwords, titles and even just general reputation all point to one person.

Remember, it's not an override unless it can actually work on the person in question. If the founder selects the person to review the founder, holds the records, has title to the land, and can kick out complainers, then there's no real way for the community to override the founder, no matter how many meetings it holds.

Also watch for the daily signs of founderism. Increasing focus on the founder over time. Sense that disagreement is failure. Every bad result is blamed on lack of good implementation. Increasing “insider” knowledge while public accountability decreases. Results are never measured. Sense that former members become morally contaminated the moment they leave.

Presence of one of these alone isn't proof of abuse, but if several are present, they are worth paying attention to before the founder can explain how the situation is uniquely different in a spiritual way.

Ask these questions about any community, teacher or method. And ask them about me, if people end up living on land I buy.

While founderism is a problem in every community, it's especially so in communities which form naturally over time as I'm describing may happen in my case. In such cases, there's no formal community and no formal rules until people decide to come together to make them. It doesn't make sense to do that at first, if, say, I'm the only one there and I don't know who else will be there that I could trust. If there's one other person who comes to visit, it also doesn't make sense, so there's a critical mass point at which people decide "we were just living together like friends, but now we want to formalize the community."

If I could find a suitable commune to join, that would basically reverse this problem. I would then be the one on probation. But if we are all starting together from scratch, then we would all be at an equal probationary level with each other.

It's a bit like the process of getting married. At first, you visit your girlfriend or boyfriend when schedules align and each of you agrees. Over time as you get closer to marriage (which hopefully is not a fully unconscious process), you start making decisions together and considering sharing more and more resources. Once married, if you decide to divorce, you split up the resources, although that's also contentious depending on how long the marriage was for, whether one person feels tricked, etc, and unfortunately often ends up in court. Leaving a commune is the divorce half of it, where the shared resources have to get split up. When joining or forming a commune, we can hopefully be more conscious about the process and less romantic.

Friends who come to visit don't get written terms, though. That's not how anything works, and at first they don't even know if they'll stick around for a day or a week. There are implied terms, like be respectful and be honest, and those shouldn't need to be in writing. If someone is offered something like an internship or a probationary period, that would require written terms. Or if it naturally evolves and they bring up wanting something like that, it could be done.
