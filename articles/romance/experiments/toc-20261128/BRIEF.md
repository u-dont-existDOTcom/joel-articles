# Reading a table of contents

You're testing the table of contents of a long online article on Substack, "Romance Advice I Wish My Parents Taught
Me", by Joel. It's first-person relationship advice from his own marriages and loves: what "love" means, talking about
sex before having it, casual sex and situationships, whether to be in a relationship at all, finding a partner, the
start of a relationship and seeing each other's flaws, idealizing, going slow or fast, the relationship as a crucible,
attraction and polarity, twin flames, community, commitment, vows and jealousy, psychedelics, trouble in an existing
relationship, children, ending a relationship and what comes after. It's about 21,000 words. You'll see only its table
of contents, the way the post's narrow contents sidebar lists it: each heading on one line at its level (#, ##, ###,
####), with how many words are under it, and a heading too long for the sidebar cut off with "…". You won't see the
text, and you shouldn't guess at it beyond what a reader skimming the contents would.

Read the table of contents file and the situations file your task line names, then write your answer file as Markdown,
in two parts:

1. **Where you'd look.** A table with one row per situation: the situation's number; the heading you'd go to first,
   exactly as the contents shows it ("…" included); "sure" or "guess"; and, if it's a guess, the
   heading you'd try next. Answer quickly, as someone in that situation would, scanning a sidebar.
2. **What gets in the way.** Headings that don't say what's under them or could be read two ways; headings that say or
   ask the same thing as the one just before them or their parent (in the sidebar a parent and its first child sit on
   two lines in a row, and the parent's text doesn't show); sections that seem to be in the wrong place, at the wrong
   level, or under the wrong parent. Say why for each. Then list your hardest headings, one per line, in exactly this
   form: `HARD: <line number> | <why>` (the line numbers are at the start of each line of the contents file).

The writer writes playfully on purpose ("Slow & steady may win the race, but turtles have problems too!", "De-ontologizing
Twin Flames FTW"): a playful heading isn't a problem by itself, only one a reader can't use to find their way.
