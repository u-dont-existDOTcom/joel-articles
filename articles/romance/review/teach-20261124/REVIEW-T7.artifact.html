What we mean by “love” ❤️

P1English isn’t a scientific language when it comes to love. This leads to a lot of romantic confusion because we don’t have one word for one meaning.

P2One meaning is selfless love—what contemplative traditions call agape, metta, loving-kindness, divine love, and so forth. In Buddhist metta practice you sit there wishing, “May all beings be at ease, pure of heart, and free.” It’s not about them being yours.

P3Then we use the same word for romantic love (eros): "I want us to be special to each other, with our lives entangled. I want us to pay a lot of attention to each other and to desire each other sexually." I don’t think there’s anything fake or shameful about this.

P4A good relationship must have both kinds of love at once. Too often, the trouble is there’s a whole lot of I want you and hardly any I want you to be happy.

P5Most people fail this test: what would I want if she herself thought she’d be happier with somebody else? The first reaction is horror, but if they can get past it, wanting her and wanting her to be happy stop fighting each other. Being wanted is part of what makes me want someone, so if she’d be happier with somebody else, I’d want her less anyway.

P6I still have to go through that horror, because I can’t wait until I know I’m wanted before I let her know I want her, and if we both do that, nothing gets started. Once we’re together I still can’t wait to be sure I’m wanted. If I start feeling less wanted, I may pull back, and she feels me pulling back and starts feeling less wanted herself, so she pulls back too.

P7Agape or divine love does two jobs at once to rescue erotic love. First, most obviously, it keeps eros from becoming totally selfish and fickle by making the other person’s happiness part of the wanting: I want you because I know you’ll be happy with me. Second, it gives eros a landing pad. When two people pull away because eros seems unreciprocated for a moment, the backdrop of actual, genuine care, keeps the couple from simply abandoning each other, and then they might even rekindle the eros when they see that it’s based on something deeper.

P8Eros can help build the agape too. It has a drive to it: it keeps pulling me toward this particular person, and over time that closeness can grow an agape that survives the drive itself.

P9That drive can weaken for reasons that aren’t really my fault. I can get old, sick, depressed, or grieving, and I would hope the agape is deep enough that she sticks by me through some of that even if it puts strain on eros. But if I just get bored with life, stop having much of a life outside her, and make her responsible for nearly all my warmth, meaning, reassurance, and excitement, that’s different. I don’t get to be shocked if she wants me less.

Talk about making love before you do it

P1From here on, when I say making love, I’m talking about two people who care about each other deeply and feel super connected, using their bodies as part of that connection. If you’ve never experienced this, it may be like trying to explain the taste of an orange. If you don’t know what love is, you don’t know. That’s another conversation, and I’d suggest reading my inner child reparenting guide.

P2Having sex is just the physical act, whether that deeper connection is there or not. But sometimes it can create the connection also.

P3What I eventually took from my dad’s advice was a bigger question: would we like to raise children together, and are we ready for that? If we can really talk about raising children together, most of the other important questions come up on their own. What would sex mean between us? What kind of life do you and I want? What kind of life would we want for our child?

P4What do you actually want? Women are kind of notorious for proudly not knowing what they want, and pressuring men into making it look like men know what they want. But everyone should know, for themselves, what they want, regardless of gender norm pressure. So what have you learned from your past desires, fulfilled or not? (I learned that sex isn't everything, but it still counts for a lot.) Do certain things make your body relax and open? Is there anything kinky you need to feel comfortable saying out loud?

P5You may not know all the answers yet. “I don’t know, but I’m willing to find out with you” is a perfectly good answer.

P6And what is sex for each of you? One person may mainly think of bonding while for the other, it's play. Sometimes it may feel sacred; another time it may just be how you come down from a bad day. It doesn’t have to mean the same thing every time nor score over 10 on a woman's magazine sex quiz consistently. But it's important for partners to be on the same page about how the sex is helping their relationship overall.

P7So talk about sexual compatibility before getting undressed. If that kills the vibe, even better in my book, since I'd be suspicious of a vibe that's ruined by genuine communication. This applies whether you’re leaning too libertine or too traditional. And once you are undressed, the communication doesn’t stop there either. Can you feel comfortable with each other enough to express what turns you on, what this means to you, and what you’re unsure about? It’s that psychological trust & intimacy that builds the bridge between “having sex” and “making love.”

P8I remember reading a Christian magazine’s letter from a newlywed Christian woman who found out that her husband didn’t have much interest in sex after she saved herself for marriage. He trapped her, because he knew she would not feel good about divorce due to the religion. Of course, it didn’t have to be nefarious. I had a relationship with E. that was a bit like that about 15yrs ago. She wanted sex once a week, and it was driving me nuts because my libido was thru the roof. She also had no interest in trying to take a safe herb or supplement to increase her desire. Of course everyone does change over time as well, so that's one more reason to think about the relationship together before anyone gets their feelings hurt.

Affection and the simmer

P1Doug Toft, who has been married for fifty years, has a useful list called 50 Things I Learned from 50 Years of Marriage. One of his points is to touch his wife without an agenda. A hug, cuddle, kiss, or back rub should sometimes be allowed to end right there. If every affectionate touch becomes a bid for sex, affection itself can start feeling like pressure.

P2Kim Anami calls the sexual current between encounters “the simmer”. Maybe she texts from work, “I can’t wait to touch you.” Maybe he tells her what he wants to do later. If we supposedly want each other but hardly ever flirt or let each other know it, that’s called taking each other for granted. Great sex probably isn’t going to materialize out of nowhere at bedtime just based on the flirting you did 5 years ago.

P3It shouldn’t become relationship homework, either: “Hey babe, Kim Anami told me to flirt with you more.”

P4And if our sex life suddenly changes, keep some curiosity about why rather than automatically going with the flow and creating a potential “new normal.” Maybe we’re pissed off at each other. Maybe one of us doesn’t feel wanted. Maybe somebody’s sick, stressed, on a new medication, whatever.

P5I also don’t want my partner to have to manufacture my desire for me. And if sex is one of the main things separating our relationship from friendship, giving it whatever exhausted scraps are left after everything else seems pretty dumb.

Situationships

P1And then there’s the situationship, which is hard to define because that’s basically the point. Usually it’s a happenstance relationship based on sex. There’s less communication, and what communication exists is more superficial. Yet it is obviously some kind of relationship even while one or both people refuse to name it. You’re regularly having sex, going out together, spending time alone together, and doing things you wouldn’t do with a regular friend, so it feels like it’s filling a relationship void, but only accidentally and occasionally—maybe just enough to keep you from looking for anything deeper.

P2It can go either way, but often the man wants to keep things undefined while the woman wants a big diamond ring, partly because she’s the one risking pregnancy (and maybe she can at least sell the ring later if things don't work out). Whoever keeps things undefined gets the fun without the responsibility, supposedly, but people still get pregnant and attached, whatever they call the relationship. Psychedelic Timing

P1This section will be relevant to some readers and easy for others to skip.

P2I’m putting the warning before anything good I say about psychedelics for relationships, because people need to hear it before they take anything. I don’t think sexual consent should be negotiated for the first time while high, when the drug is changing how safe, loving, open, and connected everything feels. And even if the two of you plan it all in advance, getting high and starting a relationship is still a risk.

P3Starting a relationship through psychedelics has another danger. Key and I met and then spent a few days taking iboga with friends. We got to know each other very deeply that way, and that was great. But it was especially her higher self I was getting to know. I tend to idealize people even sober, and that gets worse when I’m high. It was still beautiful to see the real higher self potential in her, I’m not denying that. But that information didn’t help me understand how we would get along in normal life.

P4I’ve also had friends get together at MDMA parties and stuff. It’s rarely a good idea. The intimacy can be completely real without telling you whether the two of you actually work together sober. Or you might even sometimes have the opposite problem, like I had with H., where the connection continues to deepen while sober, and it becomes something that one person might cling to desperately, never imagining they could find that again.

P5The obvious advice is to wait before making large promises and spend time together sober, around other people, and in ordinary situations. Write down what each of you learned, do some peer counseling, check with friends to see if you’re too pie-in-the-sky. But the problem is, you won’t necessarily be interested in sober advice when you’re high. You’re “beyond all that.” And the high can continue after sobriety because intimacy itself is like rose-colored glasses.

Returning to the heart to get unstuck

P1Psychedelics, especially certain types of shrooms, very pure LSD, very pure therapeutic-grade MDMA, ayahuasca, loveyhuasca haoma, and iboga, can be extremely useful when a couple is trapped in defensive stories.

P2With Bee and Key, there were many times when we could argue forever sober, then take something and finally understand each other. It could feel like we were telepathically synced. Instead of each person defending their version, we directly understood what the other was perceiving, feeling, misunderstanding.

P3But the problem is most people are lazy about integrating what they learn during this window of clarity. We enjoyed the understanding while it lasted, and then the same defensive story would resurface later. In neuroscience, this is called state-dependent learning. What you learn in altered states doesn’t automatically translate into your normal sober mind state without the work to do that.

Measuring the benefits

P1Probably one of the most important benefits we can hope to get long term from any therapy is the ability to express what we feel in a non-combative way. Otherwise, people tend to ping pong between not expressing or downplaying their feelings and expressing them combatively. MDMA can make this temporarily much easier because all the problems can look so small and silly. The question is what happens when you’re sober again.

P2So I would say the therapy worked if a week or two later, you and your partner can comfortably say, “Honey, I’m feeling unappreciated right now. I know you’re not trying to make me feel that way, and maybe you feel like I’m making you feel that way, but I’d like us to take some time soon to discuss the issue beneath this in a kinder way. What do you say?”

P3If you don’t have a partner, you can do the same thing with whoever you tend to have trouble relating with, in a less lovey way but still with compassion.

P4It’s not always possible in that moment to change a defensive posture into non-defensive loving communication. That’s why I suggest setting a time soon with a nice intention, like a ritual. Sober is fine. You can put some rose petals around you or something to build the feeling, or play some song you both like.

Why all of this sounds artificial

P1By now you’re probably thinking all of this sounds terribly artificial: is anyone actually going to do scheduled check-ins, delayed cohabitation, conversations about hypothetical disasters, imagination exercises, not to mention resisting the heat-of-the-moment stuff and continuing your own reparenting practices when a warm body is available to do some of the soothing?

P2Well, yeah, it is painfully artificial, because in a healthy communal society, the couple would have access to witnesses, elders, friends, and other emotional outlets and resources. Most of us these days don’t have that, so we’re trying to recreate consciously and clumsily some of what the wider field would have done without a checklist.

If you’re already in it

P1Most of this article is about what to do before jumping head first into head over shoulders. But if you’re already married with children, advice about slowing down arrives a little late.

P2I wish I had a special method for fixing that. Mostly I learned the obvious thing the hard way: one person can’t fix the relationship (unless they’re the main problem in it).

P3With Bee and Key, the biggest thing sitting underneath everything was trust. They wanted to be with me and would say they trusted me, but really they didn’t trust me, themself, or anyone else. It was all or nothing: blind trust, or none. I kept saying, “It’s not possible to have a relationship without trust.” I think that rigidity came from trauma and never really growing up.

Outside help can sometimes break the loop fast

P1Bee and I were once desperately misunderstanding each other, so we called my friend Eshwar, the only friend of mine whom she respected. He told a related story about something that had happened with his sister. Somehow, Bee immediately understood what I had been trying to say. It was like magic.

P2So, don’t think of outside help as a formal last resort. I think of it as a part of building community when we learn to rely on each other for this kind of intimate, real-life support. It could be calling up a mutual friend, peer counselor, couples therapist, doing Imago dialogue, Restorative Circle, and/or joining a men’s or women’s group. The key is that both people need to respect the person or process enough to hear something new.

P3What they say is also partly true: “The only one you can change is yourself.” Not fully true, because we do have influence, and changing ourself does ripple out. Restart whatever you stopped doing because the relationship was soothing you: inner-child reparenting, meditation, body practices, peer counseling.

What will honesty produce?

P1Many people ask this question of honesty, but it’s unfair. Honesty doesn’t produce a certain outcome. It is its own reward. It might repair the relationship, open it, turn the romance into friendship, preserve the family through separation, or show you that ending it is better. Of course, if the other person won’t look at the truth, you can’t make honesty a mutual value. Then you have to decide what you can live with. Temporary separation helped Bee and I, although it became less temporary as time went on, but I’ve always thought maybe we would get back together in a few decades.

Children

P1I think everyone generally agrees that parents have more responsibility to children than they do to each other, since children can’t take care of themselves.

P2Before having or adopting children, sit down for an imagination session to figure out whether you two lovebirds can preserve some civil co-parenting capacity after a terrible breakup. That’s the actual long-term commitment.

P3When it comes to stepchildren, even if they aren’t technically your children, they still become attached. If you’ve been acting as their parent for years, it’s not right to vanish from their life just because the marriage ended. You don’t have legal parental rights, but you can stay a mentor or close friend, and hopefully your partner would agree with this. When children have parental figures come and go, it can damage their ability to have securely attached relationships as adults.

P4It’s standard advice to never recruit children into the adult war. They get their own relationship with the other parent. However, this was particularly difficult during my first marriage, because Ann was very much unfit at the time to care for our son, Bear. I did fight for custody, but, as my lawyer told me, there was no point, because men generally lose. Eventually I exhausted my options for fighting, and I was left with weekly supervised visits.

P5Ann was brainwashing Bear into believing that I was “breaking his bones” at these visits even though he didn’t know what bones were. That’s when I told him I had to stop the visits before I end up in jail. So it was for my protection, but his as well, because if he realizes one day his dad is in jail because his mom manipulated him into telling a dangerous story, that could really wreck him.

P6As it is now, he may miss me, but he is well cared for (I think) by his grandmother, and probably soon he’ll seek me out when he gets his own phone.

P7I know I sound like a broken record, like my dad, but still: It takes a village. The nuclear family, built on parents’ romance, can often be the worst arrangement for children. If there’s trouble in paradise, parents will try to protect the kids by hiding their tension. But the truth is, children absorb that unresolved tension and integrate it into their very being. In a community, romance is one bond among several, and adult stability for children doesn’t rise and fall with it.

P1Before you think about how to leave, settle whether you’re leaving, and whether counseling is worth trying first.

P2You don’t owe it to them to try counseling unless you also owe it to yourself. If you actually don’t want a good relationship with them anymore, go. No amount of counseling will fix something that has nothing at the base of it.

P3If you love them and wish it could work, ask if they feel the same, and then go to counseling. On the other hand, even when you love each other, sometimes you already know the issue is beyond what counseling could fix.

Gaslighting in the dark

P1Do you have irreconcilable memories of key events? I did with both my first and second wives. I took my first wife, Ann, to two counselors. The male counselor eventually told us, “I’ve never told anyone this, but you guys need to split up for at least six months.”

P2There’s no way to resolve a situation where one person remembers that you ruined her life on purpose, while you remember that she was having a manic episode and had to be committed for her own safety. We also didn’t have mutual friends who could corroborate what happened—only friends on each side who were partial.

P3Many say it’s best to save face and work things out privately, but I’d disagree. If you don’t have friends who can help with the conflict, then you’re really stuck (and you might wanna find some new friends). But do it fairly, with the goal of helping the conflict, not just getting approval for your own interpretation. Both people should get a chance to speak, and good friends should hear both sides before validating either.

P4I can believe a friend’s experience and still tell them that my ability to know what is really going on is extremely limited. If someone says, “I don’t have all the evidence,” I trust their thinking more. Living in community would help because your friends would not only hear two stories afterward. Sometimes they would actually see how the two of you treat each other when you argue.

P5If you’ve been there for someone, don’t abandon them in a crisis unless it’s unsafe for you to stay or they’re creating the crisis to keep you, or they aren’t willing to help themself. You may need to arrange other care, but that’s no reason to pretend the romance still has a future.

After leaving

P1Often times the breakup is hard, but the aftermath is worse. Public demonization should be avoided, but I don’t think that means hiding the truth about the relationship to save face. Sometimes, the truth is that you were abused, coerced, or seriously deceived. So how much you say, and to whom, really depends on what happened and why you’re telling people.

P2Once you’re out, you can see things you genuinely couldn’t see while you were bonded. Look at what you contributed to how things turned out. And don’t paint your ex as a cartoon villain: try to understand whatever was true in their perspective too, including the conflicts they were carrying inside themselves.

P3Sometimes the easiest story is that they were fake all along, because it explains everything. Grab it only if it really is the simplest explanation of what happened. People who aren’t heavily invested in seeing your innocence can help you tell, and a therapist, pastor, or even a stranger may see the evidence more clearly than a lifelong friend. Even the curiosity itself can be therapeutic for you.

P4Spiritual practice during an ending is critical, but don’t use it to get around the feelings. Pray for your ex’s wellbeing. Remember what was real and good, and don’t use your pain as permission to hate them. Avoid the New Age belief that everyone is simply a mirror of you. Of course, we do partly mirror one another, but each person was formed by plenty that had nothing to do with you. No matter how enlightened the Buddha was, his cousin Devadatta still kept trying to kill him: looking honestly at your part doesn’t erase theirs. Sometimes it helps to ask, “What would Mr. Rogers do?” (if you’re old enough to remember that show).

P5I also learned that public demonization doesn’t always have to be the last chapter, even if things ended on that bitter note. If you avoid contact forever, they might never apologize and learn honesty. But it can help to keep distance for a while until cooler heads prevail. On the other hand, it can be tempting to let them back into your life after a private apology, and especially after a public one, but keep in mind you deserve a stable love. You’re not a yo-yo.

What I gained from loss

P1At this point, I can say I’m better off because of what happened with Bee, Key, and H., without pretending every part of those relationships was beneficial or destined. Loss taught me things love alone never would, or to paraphrase Rumi, “You must keep breaking your heart until it stays open.” (The real quote: “The reed must be torn from its bed, cut, hollowed out, and pierced with holes (wounded) before it can channel the breath of the musician into music.”)

P2From Bee, I learned what it means for me to be really in love. I learned the kind of woman I actually want: deeply spiritual, sensitive, creative, unique, somebody who inspires me and makes me question everything.

P3Key repeated a lot of that, which is why I fell in love with her. I learned some of the same harsh lessons from both of them: don’t take openness and curiosity to be fully good. They can also indicate that someone hasn’t developed their own sense of self or opinions, or enough inner adult.

P4From H., I learned that I can be extremely close to someone and feel completely understood and supported without actually falling in love. I still treasure the support we gave/give each other.

P5Too many people judge a relationship only by whether they stayed together. Relationships are a process of growth in many dimensions, so start measuring what you actually learned from yours.

Tough Love

P1Romantic love is not easy, and the things that actually make it work are not very marketable. I can’t sell you the fix with one click or one monthly subscription to my love class. (Just kidding, click here!)

P2A relationship like this is rare, and that’s probably why romantic love has been seen with suspicion as a destabilizing force throughout history. One of the best books that makes that point today might be the one I was reading with Key: The Spirit of Intimacy, by Sobonfu Somé. Somé came from the Dagara tribe in Burkina Faso. To her people, marriage is an arranged, ritualized spiritual and communal responsibility. They’re suspicious of romance. But one of her ideas fits what I’m trying to say: the relationship is bigger and more important than the egos in it. She calls it the spirit of the relationship.

P3That spirit is like the universe living its desire to know itself through us, which might be why we see ourselves in ways we can’t imagine prior. Nobody can command the spirit to remain. At our best, we serve it and listen for what Spirit wants to change. When we start thinking we own it or deserve it, that presumptuousness tends to push it away.

P4My parents agreed with Somé that community matters and that we serve Spirit. They were also not romantic, though they were loving and poly. I actually wished they were in love with each other, I guess, although I couldn’t have articulated that since I didn’t even have that concept at the time. Also reminiscent of Somé, they also wore rose-colored glasses and taught me that everybody was basically good. So I learned something about love and community, but not how hard romance is without a community around it, or how much damage basically good people can do when their fears, trauma, and undeveloped parts take over.

P5It took me many decades to figure out what probably everyone else already knows: real private pathology exists. But pathology festers when the environment is perfect for it. The dominant culture hermetically seals two people together and calls that a luxurious privacy. It tells them that sealed room should provide all the fresh air you’d get from a lover, family, community, therapist, and so on. Then it treats the predictable failure as privately caused as well, like mold that grows out of nowhere.

P6The myths around romantic love become real because we co-create them. We act as though jealousy proves love, permanent feelings can be promised, conflict should stay hidden, and the relationship failed if it ended. Then we suffer inside ideas that were never natural laws, and we walk blindly into our first, second, third, fourth, and fifth marriages!

P7It might be that I wrote this whole article for my son, Bear, who sadly, I last saw when he was about five, right before he was supposed to get the Rosenblum sex talk.

P8Bear, sex can be what you do when you’re older and find a friend you love in a super special way that makes you want to raise children together. Don’t imagine you love each other just because you want each other. Don’t feel pressure to promise what your heart will feel. Let the love and bond it produces blossom genuinely as your hearts sync. Learn about each other before getting too close, and make sure to keep friends and community around who know both of you.

P9I believe Rumi was right: A sacred relationship will open and purify your hearts regardless of whether it ends.
