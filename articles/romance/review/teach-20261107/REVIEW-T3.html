Labels Are Descriptive, Not Prescriptive

P1Here’s the funny thing about labels: you can call someone your boyfriend or girlfriend for years and still have one foot out the door, but the word at least shows pretty fast whether you both think you’re in the same relationship.

P2True commitment grows out of relational depth, not a label: if we spend hours telling each other our deepest longings, fears, and problems, I’ll naturally be ready to help when you need me. I don’t need to promise that on paper. If someone says “I love you, I feel you, I’m here for you,” yet they hardly ever talk to you about what matters, that’s a red flag.

P3The person getting more of what they want may think the arrangement is fulfilling. Usually they’re just less aware that it isn’t. In college I had a friend with four girlfriends who boasted about it to girls while trying to get a fifth. I asked him, “But are you really happy?” He said, “No, I’m miserable, dude. I feel empty inside.”

P4The full conversation is so big that almost nobody would have casual sex if they took it seriously. Which is why almost nobody has the full conversation. I can’t decide what is okay for anybody else. I can say that most people who defend casual sex are not being very honest with themselves about pregnancy, attachment, or what they might owe the person afterward.

P5Outside a loving poly community or tribe, I think honest casual sex is almost impossible. If you want something closer to “casual love-making” without quite so many ways to damage each other, you probably need to find a free-love community where the people aren’t disposable, the bonds can be acknowledged, and any children have a village. But if the community is specifically designed to prioritize free love, you might find it attracts people who have traumas that produce trouble with connecting deeply, as ex-Tamera leader, Martin Winiecki wrote about after leaving recently.

P6This is a horrible catch-22. Wounded people get lonely, and loneliness itself can kill. But the wounds that make somebody desperate for a relationship may also make them a terrible person to be in one with. People don’t only trigger each other and learn lessons. Sometimes they terrorize or kill each other.

P7I’m pretty conservative about this. I would tell most of my friends not to date yet. First love your inner child. If you can’t even do that, forget a relationship for now. Get a cat or a puppy. If you’re dishonest, can’t care what happens to another person, or keep turning every relationship into the same disaster, come on. You’re not ready.

P8You don’t have to be completely healed, but the present-day adult has to be able to parent whatever intimacy wakes up inside you, and responsible enough to deal with an actual child if sex creates one. If you can’t be a parent in either sense, you aren’t ready to date.

P9A good relationship can teach you self-love too, which is part of the problem: you may need one most when you’re least capable of creating a good one, recognizing one, or keeping it good. It depends what each of you are ready for and have the resources for. Here’s a video that can help understand the long process.

Slow & steady

P1Going slowly can show you how somebody’s moods move over time, how they treat you, and how they treat other people, but it still can’t tell you how good the sex will be or how either of you will act afterward.

P2You can know somebody for twenty years and then discover on the first night that the polarity isn’t there. Touch or smell can feel wrong, or your desire levels or kinks don’t fit. Maybe one person is sexually shut down, or the bodies just don’t connect.

Jealousy on trial

P1These days, even the brief inkling of attraction to another person becomes almost a sure sign that sexual infidelity is next. That’s why I felt I had no choice but to address the issue head on when B. wanted to marry me. I told her, “I can’t fully commit to you if I’m still attracted to other women, so before I do, let’s see if I can fix that.”

P2I meditated for a while and surprised my own self to find something like a switch in my mind. It felt like evolutionary programming around whether I had found my life mate and true love. I asked myself, “Is she the one?”

P3The inner Magic 8 Ball came back YES, and I flipped off the mate-finding switch.

P4It worked about 99% of the time. I was no longer interested in other women, aside from one extremely beautiful girl who grabbed my attention once.

P5That amazed me, although B. never quite believed the switch was real. She even started hallucinating that I was looking at or talking to other women when I wasn’t. So her jealousy was not simply tracking what I was doing in the present. More likely it was insecure attachment from childhood trying to justify its continued existence.

P6Jealousy is always insecurity, but sometimes that insecurity can be an important indicator that the relationship actually is not secure, rather than just a remembered script from childhood. Although, either way, it can become a self-fulfilling prophecy.

P7Instead of stewing in jealousy and throwing out microaggressions now and then, speak your heart without hiding the insecurity behind anger, as in the above video I found on IG. In a monogamous relationship, you might say:

P8“I notice you pay attention to this other girl more than me, and it makes me feel unloved and insecure. Can we talk about it? Do you really want to explore things with her? If so that’s fine, but don’t make the decision lightly, because it’s irreversible.”

P9Maybe he really is neglecting you or wanting to be with someone else. Maybe the “attention” was one glance and your baseline insecurity inflated it into a threat that it wasn’t. That part should not be projected onto your partner. Every couple has to work out for themselves what actually feels healthy.

P10I do believe two people can choose sexual exclusivity honestly. They can admit that attraction to other people exists and decide not to act on it each day. And even better than simply suppressing it, they can put it to work. I agree with Esther Perel, who writes that this sexual energy felt outside the relationship can be used and redirected into the relationship to make it more alive.

P11Imagine if you and your partner both saw those outside attractions as beneficial energy sources rather than a threat to your relationship. It sure would beat beating around the bush because of fear and jealousy. You can start playing around with opening up about the minor, momentary attractions, rather than wait in hiding until a serious crush is dramatically confessed.

P12Lots of couples promise forever to each other and nobody else. Look around at how many actually live that way. On the other hand, in a polyamorous relationship, the boundaries might be more negotiable, but then somebody has to sit down and negotiate them, down to who gets to stay the night. That can end up sometimes creating more drama than it's worth.

P13Polyamory can also be a way of escaping deeper intimacy, like my one poly college friend who boasted about having 4 girlfriends, but admitted to me that he felt deeply unhappy. Or it can be a way of escaping guilt, like when someone keeps cheating until they finally "come out of the closet" as poly, as if that solves their prior dishonesty. But they are right to finally come clean and hopefully avoid that dishonesty in future.

P14I learned most of this by screwing it up. Some of it I just thought through, and I'm still not sure about all of it.
