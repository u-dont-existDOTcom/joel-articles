## The window of clarity, and how it closes

The attachment theory part comes from my own experience and from reading I’ve done. Sex turns on bonding chemistry in our brain (like oxytocin, dopamine, vasopressin) that usually helps us form attachments, so that we don't just abandon our children.

## Peeking thru the window

Before the window of clarity closes, the main thing is to find out whether you are actually compatible. Do you have the same goals and values, especially around children, religion, politics, health, love, and romance? Where do you want to live? Do you both want to find a community? What kind of relationship do you want with technology and money?

So we have to find a way to ask deep questions. Conversation-card games may seem silly, but they can help people get past superficial talk and see if they’re compatible.

But watch out, because ironically, a card game to get closer and deeper can backfire! If we spend two hours telling each other things nobody else knows, we may feel incredibly understood and close, but that may mean the game worked without telling us whether the relationship will.

You can also bring the person to a party, around your friends and family and see how they act, or take them to activities you enjoy and see if they really enjoy them too (although if they seem to enjoy every single one of them, they might be pretending). Try doing what you would normally do in your day alone, but with them, and see if it feels weird. Are they wondering why you spend hours playing with your cat? Do they look bored, like they’d rather just hit you up later for some fun?

See their negative side first too. If there is no negative side, that’s a red flag for fakery. Ask whether the spiritual story they tell about themselves survives an inconvenient PMS afternoon. How recent is the healing story—two months or two years? You may not see their jealousy, abandonment and possessiveness until there is stress and entanglement, and that’s also when you find out whether the “I’m so much better than I used to be” story survives. By then, of course, you’re stressed and entangled. 😆

Sometimes the only thing I have is a little “wait” feeling. If I'm about to ignore a question because I really want this to work, that "wait" is enough reason for me to ask the question before I keep going.

## The conversation about flaws

Card games and social gatherings don’t tell you everything, but asking someone out of the blue, “What did you do wrong in your past relationships?” would make for a pretty weird date.

“I have baggage” isn't as useful as learning what you're having trouble with now. What might make it hard for you to function well in a relationship?

I’d want both of us to be honest about how well we actually handle our own issues. What addictions or impulsive behaviors can’t we reliably control? What are we probably going to ask forgiveness for more than once? That tells me more than either of us proclaiming that we’re healed.

Look at the most recent conflicts and ask if you were able to regulate yourself during them. What did you do to the other person, and how did you repair the damage afterward? Not doing can be just as bad as overdoing. If conflict makes you shut down, does it mean you stop talking to your partner for days, leaving them feeling abandoned?

Has your judgment gotten better, or would your friends or exes say you keep falling for the same stuff?

## The Relationship As Crucible

It was during my first marriage that I read about Dr. David Schnarch’s Crucible Approach in Passionate Marriage. Schnarch saw marriage as a natural “people-growing process.” His idea of differentiation was learning to hold onto yourself while staying close, especially when intimacy brings anxiety and unresolved problems to the surface, which is definitely what relationship has done to me.

One warning before I romanticize the crucible too much: sometimes (as in my first marriage) this isn’t two wounded people triggering each other but one person terrorizing or controlling the other. If you’re scared to say no or tell the truth, or of what happens if you leave, that’s way past a mutual communication exercise, so get other people involved and think about safety first.

Toft sees the crucible of his marriage as his main spiritual practice. He also continues his meditation, yoga, prayer, and church activities, but sees them as secondary. I understand what he means, because it’s much easier for me to feel spiritually advanced when nobody is close enough to expose the parts of me that aren’t.

## Two Pillars Don’t Hold The Roof Up

Even if I found my twin flame, she’d still be one person. The polarity could be perfect, but we’d still need other people around us. Maybe an unusually strong couple can get away without much outside support, but I think that’s rare. There's almost always going to be some points in a relationship when understanding between partners breaks down.

Whenever something serious comes up in my relationships, we each run to our own friends for support. Separate/non-mutual confidants can be helpful and harmful. Sometimes you need privacy from your partner and somewhere else to say what you’re feeling (not to mention somewhere else to sleep if it comes to that one night). But if your confidant doesn’t have a good connection with your partner, they’re only hearing your side, usually while you’re upset, and they may just be trying to protect you, so take what they say with a grain of salt.

Then I resent the one-sided story she’s telling, and she resents the one-sided story I’m telling. Some of the friends may even be exes who want us to break up, and eventually neither of us wants to talk to the other’s friends anymore. So then at that point when we actually need the most help, we suddenly become the most isolated.

Ideally, a friend who actually knows us both sees the dysfunctional pattern before either of us does. But having the same confidant creates the opposite problem: now you may not have a release valve when you want privacy from your partner, so neither way is perfect.

And if both people are really falling apart, sometimes there is only so much anyone else can do to help.

With H., I learned another part of the same dyad problem: not to treat what either of us thought the relationship was as a guarantee of what it would remain, because feelings can and usually do change over time. In our case, the relationship continued to naturally deepen while our outside relationships often felt more shallow, so it was natural for the dependency to increase. She was never into monogamy, but that didn’t stop her from becoming possessive once she found someone really worth keeping. And then she began demonizing me when I refused to be possessed. Because we did not have a shared community capable of supporting us, her friends heard her side and affirmed her truth.

Make as much community as you can wherever you are, like going to Meetups together and making deep friendships as a couple. Find some shared purpose beyond maintaining the relationship. Introduce your old friends to your partner and try to mutualize those friendships as much as possible, so they aren’t experienced as slyly antagonistic or partial to one side. You don’t need to spend all your time together, but two completely separate social worlds will automatically start taking sides when something goes wrong.

## What are you actually choosing together?

When we say, “We’re together,” what does that mean exactly? I can hear a whole future in those two words—sex, exclusivity, living together, money, children, caregiving. She might mean, “We really like each other; let’s see what happens.” I actually like letting a relationship flow with some parts left unnamed, but the problem is when we’re using the same words for two different futures.

Sometimes what two people want is a practical partnership: sharing a home, rearing kids and taking care of each other. Many people in the world actually believe sex is part of that practical arrangement, and they don't think romance has to be the basis of all that. There’s nothing inherently wrong with that arrangement if both people know that’s what they’re doing. But I suspect they simply have never been in love, so they don’t have that expectation. And that becomes a problem if they ever do fall for someone.

And the biggie: what happens if one of us falls in love with someone else? Is that, in and of itself, a betrayal, since it's not under our control? If our lives change, can we compassionately sit down and rethink the agreement? And if we split up, what do we still owe each other (and our kids, if we have any)?

## Which marriage vows are honest?

Conventional marriage vows are dishonest and inept, and most people know it. You can’t promise how you’ll feel in the future, but you can promise actions if you really believe you’re capable of delivering them. So for example, you could promise to tell the truth, to keep showing up emotionally, to support the children, or whatever else you genuinely believe you can do even if your feelings change. More importantly, though, if you do keep those promises, it's quite likely that your connection will deepen, so it's not like we truly have no control of future feelings, but just not completely, directly or unilaterally.

We also find something like my warning in the New & Old Testaments. Ecclesiastes 5:4–6 says it is better not to make a vow than to make one and fail to keep it, and that if you then tell the priest you didn’t mean what you promised, God will be angry and destroy your work.

## Why all of this sounds artificial

By now you’re probably thinking all of this sounds terribly artificial: is anyone actually going to do scheduled check-ins, delayed cohabitation, conversations about hypothetical disasters, imagination exercises, not to mention resisting the heat-of-the-moment stuff and continuing your own reparenting practices when a warm body is available to do some of the soothing?

Well, yeah, it is painfully artificial, because in a healthy communal society, the couple would have access to witnesses, elders, friends, and other emotional outlets and resources. Most of us these days don’t have that, so we’re trying to recreate consciously and clumsily some of what the wider field would have done without a checklist.

## What will honesty produce?

Many people ask this question of honesty, but it’s unfair. Honesty doesn’t produce a certain outcome. It is its own reward. It might repair the relationship, open it, turn the romance into friendship, preserve the family through separation, or show you that ending it is better. Of course, if the other person won’t look at the truth, you can’t make honesty a mutual value. Then you have to decide what you can live with. Temporary separation helped Bee and I, although it became less temporary as time went on, but I’ve always thought maybe we would get back together in a few decades.

## Ending consciously

Before you think about how to leave, settle whether you’re leaving, and whether counseling is worth trying first.

## Gaslighting in the dark

Many say it’s best to save face and work things out privately, but I’d disagree. If you don’t have friends who can help with the conflict, then you’re really stuck (and you might wanna find some new friends). And when you do ask friends for help, do it fairly, with the goal of resolving the conflict, not just getting approval for your own interpretation: both people should get a chance to speak, and good friends should hear both sides before validating either.

## After leaving

Often times the breakup is hard, but the aftermath is worse. Public demonization should be avoided, but I don’t think that means hiding the truth about the relationship to save face. Sometimes, the truth is that you were abused, coerced, or seriously deceived. So how much you say, and to whom, really depends on what happened and why you’re telling people.

Once you’re out, you can see things you genuinely couldn’t see while you were bonded. Look at what you contributed to how things turned out. And don’t paint your ex as a cartoon villain: try to understand whatever was true in their perspective too, including the conflicts they were carrying inside themselves.

Sometimes the easiest story is that they were fake all along, because it explains everything. Grab it only if it really is the simplest explanation of what happened, and even the curiosity about that can be therapeutic for you. People who aren’t heavily invested in seeing your innocence can help you tell, and a therapist, pastor, or even a stranger may see the evidence more clearly than a lifelong friend.

Spiritual practice during an ending is critical, but don’t use it to get around the feelings. Pray for your ex’s wellbeing. Remember what was real and good, and don’t use your pain as permission to hate them. Avoid the New Age belief that everyone is simply a mirror of you. Of course, we do partly mirror one another, but each person was formed by plenty that had nothing to do with you. No matter how enlightened the Buddha was, his cousin Devadatta still kept trying to kill him: looking honestly at your part doesn’t erase theirs. Sometimes it helps to ask, “What would Mr. Rogers do?” (if you’re old enough to remember that show).

I also learned that public demonization doesn’t always have to be the last chapter, even if things ended on that bitter note. If you avoid contact forever, they might never apologize and learn honesty. But it can help to keep distance for a while until cooler heads prevail. On the other hand, it can be tempting to let them back into your life after a private apology, and especially after a public one, but keep in mind you deserve a stable love. You’re not a yo-yo.

## What I gained from loss

Too many people judge a relationship only by whether they stayed together. Relationships are a process of growth in many dimensions, so start measuring what you actually learned from yours.

## Tough Love

A passionate romantic relationship that’s also stable is rare, and that’s probably why romantic love has been seen with suspicion as a destabilizing force throughout history. One of the best books that makes that point today might be the one I was reading with Key: The Spirit of Intimacy, by Sobonfu Somé. Somé came from the Dagara tribe in Burkina Faso. To her people, marriage is an arranged, ritualized spiritual and communal responsibility, and they’re suspicious of romance. But one of her ideas fits what I’m trying to say: the relationship is bigger and more important than the egos in it. She calls it the spirit of the relationship.

That spirit is like the universe living its desire to know itself through us, which might be why we see ourselves in ways we couldn’t imagine before. Nobody can command the spirit to remain. At our best, we serve it and listen for what Spirit wants to change. When we start thinking we own it or deserve it, that presumptuousness tends to push it away.

My parents agreed with Somé that community matters and that we serve Spirit. They were also not romantic, though they were loving and poly. I actually wished they were in love with each other, I guess, although I couldn’t have articulated that since I didn’t even have that concept at the time. And like Somé, who saw everyone as naturally good in their natural environment, flowing with life the way our ancestors showed us, they trusted the universe, rose-colored glasses and all, and taught me that everybody was basically good. So I learned something about love and community, but not how hard romance is without a community around it, or how much damage basically good people can do when their fears, trauma, and undeveloped parts take over.

Bear, sex can be what you do when you’re older and find a friend you love in a super special way that makes you want to raise children together. Don’t imagine you love each other just because you want each other. Don’t feel pressure to promise what your heart will feel. Let the love and bond it produces blossom genuinely as your hearts sync. Learn about each other before getting too close, and make sure to keep friends and community around who know both of you.
