[146] ## Two Pillars Don’t Hold The Roof Up

[147] Even if I found my twin flame, she’d still be one person. The polarity could be perfect, but we’d still need other people around us. Maybe an unusually strong couple can get away without much outside support, but I think that’s rare. There's almost always going to be some points in a relationship when understanding between partners breaks down.

[148] Whenever something serious comes up in my relationships, we each run to our own friends for support. Separate/non-mutual confidants can be helpful and harmful. Sometimes you need privacy from your partner and somewhere else to say what you’re feeling (not to mention somewhere else to sleep if it comes to that one night). But if your confidant doesn’t have a good connection with your partner, they’re only hearing your side, usually while you’re upset, and they may just be trying to protect you, so take what they say with a grain of salt.

[149] Then I resent the one-sided story she’s telling, and she resents the one-sided story I’m telling. Some of the friends may even be exes who want us to break up, and eventually neither of us wants to talk to the other’s friends anymore. So then at that point when we actually need the most help, we suddenly become the most isolated.

[150] Ideally, a friend who actually knows us both sees the dysfunctional pattern before either of us does. But having the same confidant creates the opposite problem: now you may not have a release valve when you want privacy from your partner, so neither way is perfect.

[151] And if both people are really falling apart, sometimes there is only so much anyone else can do to help.

[152] With H., I learned another part of the same dyad problem: not to treat what either of us thought the relationship was as a guarantee of what it would remain, because feelings can and usually do change over time. In our case, the relationship continued to naturally deepen while our outside relationships often felt more shallow, so it was natural for the dependency to increase. She was never into monogamy, but that didn’t stop her from becoming possessive once she found someone really worth keeping. And then she began demonizing me when I refused to be possessed. Because we did not have a shared community capable of supporting us, her friends heard her side and affirmed her truth.

[153] Make as much community as you can wherever you are, like going to Meetups together and making deep friendships as a couple. Find some shared purpose beyond maintaining the relationship. Introduce your old friends to your partner and try to mutualize those friendships as much as possible, so they aren’t experienced as slyly antagonistic or partial to one side. You don’t need to spend all your time together, but two completely separate social worlds will automatically start taking sides when something goes wrong.

[158] ## Which marriage vows are honest?

[159] Conventional marriage vows are dishonest and inept, and most people know it. You can’t promise how you’ll feel in the future, but you can promise actions if you really believe you’re capable of delivering them. So for example, you could promise to tell the truth, to keep showing up emotionally, to support the children, or whatever else you genuinely believe you can do even if your feelings change. More importantly, though, if you do keep those promises, it's quite likely that your connection will deepen, so it's not like we truly have no control of future feelings, but just not completely, directly or unilaterally.

[160] We also find something like my warning in the New & Old Testaments. Ecclesiastes 5:4–6 says it is better not to make a vow than to make one and fail to keep it, and that if you then tell the priest you didn’t mean what you promised, God will be angry and destroy your work.

[187] ## After leaving

[188] Often times the breakup is hard, but the aftermath is worse. Public demonization should be avoided, but I don’t think that means hiding the truth about the relationship to save face. Sometimes, the truth is that you were abused, coerced, or seriously deceived. So how much you say, and to whom, really depends on what happened and why you’re telling people.

[189] Once you’re out, you can see things you genuinely couldn’t see while you were bonded. Look at what you contributed to how things turned out. And don’t paint your ex as a cartoon villain: try to understand whatever was true in their perspective too, including the conflicts they were carrying inside themselves.

[190] Sometimes the easiest story is that they were fake all along, because it explains everything. Grab it only if it really is the simplest explanation of what happened, and even the curiosity about that can be therapeutic for you. People who aren’t heavily invested in seeing your innocence can help you tell, and a therapist, pastor, or even a stranger may see the evidence more clearly than a lifelong friend.

[191] Spiritual practice during an ending is critical, but don’t use it to get around the feelings. Pray for your ex’s wellbeing. Remember what was real and good, and don’t use your pain as permission to hate them. Avoid the New Age belief that everyone is simply a mirror of you. Of course, we do partly mirror one another, but each person was formed by plenty that had nothing to do with you. No matter how enlightened the Buddha was, his cousin Devadatta still kept trying to kill him: looking honestly at your part doesn’t erase theirs. Sometimes it helps to ask, “What would Mr. Rogers do?” (if you’re oily enough to remember that show).

[192] I also learned that public demonization doesn’t always have to be the last chapter, even if things ended on that bitter note. If you avoid contact forever, they might never apologize and learn honesty. But it can help to keep distance for a while until cooler heads prevail. On the other hand, it can be tempting to let them back into your life after a private apology, and especially after a public one, but keep in mind you deserve a stable love. You’re not a yo-yo.

[195] ## Tough Love

[196] A passionate romantic relationship that’s also stable is rare, and that’s probably why romantic love has been seen with suspicion as a destabilizing force throughout history. One of the best books that makes that point today might be the one I was reading with Key: The Spirit of Intimacy, by Sobonfu Somé. Somé came from the Dagara tribe in Burkina Faso. To her people, marriage is an arranged, ritualized spiritual and communal responsibility, and they’re suspicious of romance. But one of her ideas fits what I’m trying to say: the relationship is bigger and more important than the egos in it. She calls it the spirit of the relationship.

[197] That spirit is like the universe living its desire to know itself through us, which might be why we see ourselves in ways we couldn’t imagine before. Nobody can command the spirit to remain. At our best, we serve it and listen for what Spirit wants to change. When we start thinking we own it or deserve it, that presumptuousness tends to push it away.

[198] My parents agreed with Somé that community matters and that we serve Spirit. They were also not romantic, though they were loving and poly. I actually wished they were in love with each other, I guess, although I couldn’t have articulated that since I didn’t even have that concept at the time. And like Somé, who saw everyone as naturally good in their natural environment, flowing with life the way our ancestors showed us, they trusted the universe, rose-colored glasses and all, and taught me that everybody was basically good. So I learned something about love and community, but not how hard romance is without a community around it, or how much damage basically good people can do when their fears, trauma, and undeveloped parts take over.
