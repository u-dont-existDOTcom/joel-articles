### Peeking thru the window

B123: Before the wisdom window closes, the main thing is to find out whether you are actually compatible. Do you have the same goals and values, especially around children, religion, politics, health, love, and romance? Where do you want to live? Do you both want to find a community? What kind of relationship do you want with technology and money?

B124 [HIS NEW VERSION, 22 Nov: read it as context, don't report it]: So we have to find a way to ask deep questions. Conversation-card games may seem silly, but they can help people get past superficial talk and see if they’re compatible.

B125: But asking, “How do you handle jealousy?” can get me a beautiful answer that tells me almost nothing. I’d rather ask what she thinks she would do in an actual situation. If all she can give me is a general answer, I assume she probably doesn’t know yet.

B126 [HIS NEW VERSION, 22 Nov: read it as context, don't report it]: But watch out, because ironically, a card game to get closer and deeper can backfire! If we spend two hours telling each other things nobody else knows, we may feel incredibly understood and close, but that may mean the game worked without telling us whether the relationship will.

B127 [HIS NEW VERSION, 22 Nov: read it as context, don't report it]: You can also bring the person to a party, around your friends and family and see how they act, or take them to activities you enjoy and see if they really enjoy them too (although if they seem to enjoy every single one of them, they might be pretending). Do they look bored, like they’d rather just hit you up later for some fun?

B128: See their negative side first too. If there is no negative side, that’s a red flag for fakery. Ask whether the spiritual story they tell about themselves survives an inconvenient PMS afternoon. ### The conversation about flaws

B132: Card games and social gatherings don’t tell you everything, but I wouldn’t ask someone out of the blue, “What did you do wrong in your past relationships?” That would be a pretty weird date.

B133: At the right time, though, in the right way (especially if they bring it up tangentially), I would ask about their past relationships, especially the recent ones. If every story ends with everything being the other person’s fault, I’d ask, “Okay, so what lessons did you find for yourself?”

B134 [HIS NEW VERSION, 22 Nov: read it as context, don't report it]: “I have baggage” isn't as useful as learning what you're having trouble with now. What might make it hard for you to function well in a relationship?

B135 [HIS NEW VERSION, 22 Nov: read it as context, don't report it]: I’d want both of us to be honest about how effective we actually are at handling our own issues. What addictions or impulsive behaviors can’t we reliably control? What are we probably going to ask forgiveness for more than once? B136 [HIS NEW VERSION, 22 Nov: read it as context, don't report it]: Look at the most recent conflicts and ask if you were able to regulate yourself during them. # The Relationship As Crucible

B203: It was during my first marriage that I read about Dr. David Schnarch’s Crucible Approach in Passionate Marriage. Schnarch saw marriage as a natural “people-growing process.” His idea of differentiation was learning to hold onto yourself while staying close, especially when intimacy brings anxiety and unresolved problems to the surface.

B204: That’s definitely what relationship has done to me.

B205: One warning before I romanticize the crucible too much: sometimes (as in my first marriage) this isn’t two wounded people triggering each other but one person terrorizing or controlling the other. If you’re scared to say no or tell the truth, or scared of what happens if you leave, don’t treat that as a mutual communication exercise—get other people involved and think about safety first.

B206: Toft sees the crucible of his marriage as his main spiritual practice. He also continues his meditation, yoga, prayer, and church activities, but sees them as secondary. I understand what he means. It’s much easier for me to feel spiritually advanced when nobody is close enough to expose the parts of me that aren’t.

B207: Being in a relationship showed me how vulnerable I actually am when I open my heart. It’s really easy to feel invulnerable and strong when you’re alone, or even just with friends. Taking off the masks and showing your inner child is a gamble, but it’s required if you want to go deeper in yourself and deeper with another person.

### Don’t make your partner your whole world

B216: When I’m in love, my partner is my everything. Everything else drops away. I still try to make some time for friends, but really all I want to do is be with her.

B217: That little paradise is wonderful, but it contains the seed of its own destruction. Part of why it feels like a magical wonderland is that you can suddenly stop parenting yourself now that you have someone who’s fully got your back. This makes you soft and weak, kinda like a sweet treat or heroin. It is wonderful to have their love and support, but don’t let that bring you away from your own self-love, reparenting, meditation, yoga, and other healing practices that keep you independently healthy. Otherwise, you’ll end up blaming them when you feel bad.

B218: So don’t let your world get so small. Don’t abandon your friends unless they really aren’t good for the relationship. And if they aren’t, ask why. Are they bad friends? Are they slyly interested in one of you romantically? Or is the relationship so weak that it can’t handle anybody with an outside view?

B219: You don’t need to spend as much time with friends as you did before the romance. But keep some room to explore life on your own.

### Babies growing up again

### Different levels in different domains can be complementary
