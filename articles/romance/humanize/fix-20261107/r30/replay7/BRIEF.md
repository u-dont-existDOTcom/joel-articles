# The test brief: fix these sentences the way Joel fixes them

You are fixing sentences of an essay by Joel, a writer, about romance and relationships, written in the first person
("I"). A detector of model writing read each item's section as human, but only with medium confidence on the sentences
marked [[MEDIUM: ...]]; those are the sentences to fix. Each item shows the section; the rest of it is context and
stays word for word. Below are the ways his own fixes of drafts like these differ from the drafts he fixed, each with
examples (some come from another of his articles, a guide for people helping someone through a bad trip, so read them
for the way, not the subject).

For each item:
1. MARKED: for each marked sentence, the way below that it reads (its number) and what in it is that way.
2. REWRITE: the whole section, as you would fix it. Keep every point, fact, joke, name and claim of his; don't add a fact
   about his life or opinions he hasn't given. Write it in his voice: plain, warm, direct, a little playful, first
   person. Use curly quote marks and apostrophes (’ “ ”) as the essay does.
3. ADD NOTHING. No detail, reason, example or explanation that the section doesn't already hold: not a duration, not a
   feeling, not a "because" of your own. A sentence that says plainly the point a story of his shows is not an addition
   when the story shows it; a new fact or a new reason is. Make yours about the same length or shorter.
4. THIS ITEM'S MEDIUM SENTENCES SURVIVED EVERY REWORDING. Three earlier fixes changed only words in them (a word or two;
   a sentence folded into the next; a list said as clauses) and each read worse, so look at way 25 first: you may move
   sentences, within a paragraph or between paragraphs, join two, and cut a line that only announces what comes, names
   with a label what the next words say plainly, or says again what another line says. Keep the words of every sentence
   you keep, except where a join or a move needs a word.

Read only this file and the file of passages you are given. Don't open any other file or folder.

THE WAYS

1. **Compressed.** A run of category words or noun phrases, or several ideas packed into one sentence, where he gives each
   its own clause with what happens or what to do. He fixed "respiratory depression, stimulant toxicity, serotonin
   toxicity, seizure, dangerous overheating, cardiovascular symptoms, ..." into "are they suffering from slow and shallow
   breathing (respiratory depression, from opioids, benzos and alcohol)? Or is it rapid breathing, heart rate, and
   agitation, signs of stimulant toxicity?"
2. **Built the same way as its neighbours.** Items or sentences that repeat one shape: the same opening, the same
   connector, a term in brackets at the same place in each, or the same comparing word ("is a different problem from ...,
   and both are different from ..."). He builds each one differently: "would be one full pattern", "would be counted as one
   pattern altogether", "another pattern together", "Another is ... along with ...", "Lastly, ... as well as ...".
3. **Correcting or contrasting.** "X does not mean Y; it means Z", "a Y rather than a Z", "not X but Y", "X but not Y",
   "isn't X, it's Y", or a pair set against each other ("severely altered but physically stable"), where the sentence
   could say the thing itself. He fixed "This is a recognition aid rather than a command to wait for every criterion
   before acting." into "So if you see any of these patterns, call medics." Mark it unless the contrast answers a worry
   the reader really has ("Just because it's unplanned doesn't mean it will be more shocking" is his).
4. **Vague about who, what, or what counts.** He fixed "whatever could hurt somebody" into "Anything that could let
   someone hurt themselves", "water" into "pools of water", "adjunct" into "adjunct remedy", and spelled out that each
   combination "would be counted as one pattern altogether".
5. **Opening on a rule or an authority, with the reader outside it.** He fixed "Clinicians often use the Hunter criteria"
   into "We can use the same Hunter criteria that clinicians use", "Get urgent help when somebody may harm themselves"
   into "the moment you think they might hurt themselves or anybody else, get urgent help", "Dim the lights. Lower the
   sound." into "They're feeling overwhelmed, so dim the lights".
6. **Ending on a summing-up or a line that sounds wise.** "If all of that's true, calming and stabilizing the trip may be
   enough."; "An emergency means act now."; "The response is care rather than blame." He ends on what to do ("call
   medics", "reach out") or cuts the line ("you were overcompleting, repeating yourself on the last line").
7. **True but no use to the reader**, or missing what the reader needs to act. He cut a study detail with "there's NO
   PAYOFF" and added "Slow breathing is normal with iboga, but if cardiac irregularities come along with it, that's an
   extra danger sign."
8. **Joined and worded the way a model joins and words.** Two short sentences he would join with ", but" or "so"; a comma
   pair he writes with "and" ("Slow and shallow", not "Slow, shallow"); "one" where "a" would do.
9. **The findings before whether they bear on the reader's case.** He says first what decides whether what follows applies
   to the reader, and puts the limit there, never in a clause hung on a finding. My "When they gave DMSO to rats for three
   days before a heart attack, those rats lost ... though that study didn't try giving it after the attack had started"
   became his "So far, the heart research on DMSO has only been in animals and lab dishes, and only as a prophylactic
   (preventative treatment)." first ("i flipped it high conf human by moving the payoff earlier about whether they are
   about to read something relevant or not", 2026-11-19). He writes a study's finding in the simple past ("started", not
   "had started"), and doesn't name the same thing in three sentences in a row.
   A sentence whose two halves mirror each other ("one ... while the other ..." with the same verb) is this way too:
   he breaks the mirror. (An illustration, not his: "One of them wants a dog while the other wants a cat." would become
   "One of them wants a dog, and the other has always pictured a cat.")
   A piece of advice added after the line it follows from becomes one sentence with "so": "People change. If you drift
   apart, it's better to talk early." would become "People change, so talk early, while it's easy." (an illustration,
   not his). "It's important to do X" becomes "So do X", tied to what came before.

10. **Could be read two ways.** A pointer word ("it", "this", "that", "they") that could point at two things, a word that
   could describe two things, a statement that is really an instruction, a sign with nobody's name on it. He fixed "the
   evidence for it" (the drug, or the review just named?) into "the evidence for this med", "too weak to show that it
   works" (a weak drug, or weak evidence?) into "insufficient to show that it works", "that's their last dose" into "that
   should be their last dose", "If confusion or headache worsens" into "If their confusion or headache worsens", and
   added "it means" to say what a sign means; each one, checked alone, made Pangram read more of the paragraph human
   (2026-11-19). For every such word, write what it could mean here, and mark it if a quick reader could take it either
   way.
   A stock phrase whose "it" names nothing ("that's where it comes in", "is where it all begins"), a verb left without
   its object ("if he won't listen"), and an "it" that could be either of two things are this way too: he names what
   each one means with words the passage already has, and adds nothing. (An illustration, not his: "If he won't listen,
   you can't fix it." would become "If he won't listen to what you need, you can't fix the arrangement.")

11. **The means before the payoff.** A sentence that opens on the tool, the source or the method, and only then says
   what the reader gets from it. He fixed "We can use the same Hunter criteria that clinicians use to diagnose
   serotonin syndrome." into "To diagnose serotonin syndrome, we can use the Hunter criteria, the same checklist
   clinicians use.", and the sentence after it, "One or more of these patterns makes the diagnosis more likely:",
   into "Using Hunter, one or more of these patterns makes the diagnosis more likely:", so it says how the first leads to
   it; the two changes took the stretch from AI to Human High (2026-11-20: "can't believe you still don't understand
   what i'm talking about when i say payoff first"). For each sentence, write what the reader gets from it, and mark it
   if that comes after the means, or if nothing in it says how it follows from the sentence before.
12. **Not connected to the sentence before.** A sentence that gives another option, the other mistake, or the next case,
   standing on its own as if it were a new point. He fixed "Arguing can make them dig in to prove they're God, so don't
   tell them ..." into "If instead you argue, that can make them dig in ...", and "If you nod along, they might think
   ..." into "And the other failing alternative, just nodding along, might let them think ..." (Human Low to Human High
   100: "fixed the connectivity issue"); and a second paragraph that opened "Stop as soon as they say stop." after a
   paragraph about asking into "If they did permit touch, stop as soon as they say stop." (Medium to High; it "didn't
   follow from the first section"). For each sentence, write how it follows from the one before (the next step, the
   other way, the case where the answer was yes), and mark it if nothing in it says so.

13. **Leaves the reader wondering why they're reading it.** A sentence or step that opens on what to do, or on a fact,
   before saying what in the situation calls for it. He opened the fluids step on why fluids come up at all, tied to the
   step before: my "Give fluids only if they're fully alert, able to swallow and not vomiting" became his "Overstimulating
   also dehydrates them, but we can't give fluids unless they're fully alert, able to swallow and not vomiting"
   (2026-11-21: "my goal is to make it read well and lead on so i'm not wondering all the time why i am reading this").
   For each sentence, write what in the reader's situation it answers, and mark it if they only find out later, or never.
14. **A rule against what someone would naturally do, said flatly.** He says first why a person would want to, then the
   problem: my "Pacing or exercising to "burn it off" only makes more heat" became "While it's natural for people who are
   overstimulated to start pacing or exercising to "burn it off," the problem is that creates more body heat". Mark a
   don't, a "won't help" or an "only makes it worse" that doesn't say why somebody would want to do it.
15. **A don't with nothing to do instead, or advice nobody could carry out.** My "If they feel a strong urge to keep
   moving (akathisia), I'd stay with them and talk them into keeping still anyway" (an urge nobody can be talked out of)
   became "I'd have them do some fidgeting (especially of the calf muscle, flexing the feet), which can soothe that urge
   without the over-activity risk." Mark each instruction the reader couldn't do, and each don't that leaves them
   nothing to do.
16. **Said twice: a lead line the rest says again, or a rule said again the other way round.** He cut my opener "Keep
   them still in a calm room." (the sentences after it say how) and my "But if they're confused, vomiting or sleepy, I
   wouldn't push anything into them ..." after "Give fluids only if they're fully alert, able to swallow and not
   vomiting" (the same condition turned inside out). Cutting the opener was one of the two changes that took the steps
   from AI to Human High ("it still was AI until i removed the redundant first sentence"). Mark a first sentence that the
   rest repeats, and a sentence that turns a condition just given around.
17. **Advice that isn't right here.** Advice carried over from another case, or that another part contradicts. My "not
   even pills or charcoal" in the fluids step went: the step before it gives a pill (cyproheptadine) to anyone fully
   alert and able to swallow, and charcoal isn't part of serotonin syndrome care (2026-11-21: "the advice i think was not
   quite correct medically altho pangram wouldn't know that part"). Read each instruction against the rest of the text
   and the reader's situation, and mark it if something else in the text contradicts it, it doesn't fit this case, or a
   reader without training couldn't do it safely.

18. **A short line standing alone.** A sentence of two to six words that sums up, labels or turns, between longer ones.
   He joins it to the sentence it belongs to, or gives it what only this writer would say there. "It's philosophical."
   became "It's philosophical, and it bites."; "Based on what?" became "But could I explain that to an objective
   friend?"; "That was my mistake." became "We see what we want to see."; "See people together. Go places. Don't just
   disappear into each other." became "Meet other folks together and explore new places, don't just disappear into each
   other."; a closing "Everything." went. A line of speech, or a question in a story, isn't this way ("Then what? You
   block her?").
   A plain line that ends a paragraph is this way too, even at nine or ten words: he carries it on into the point the
   paragraph is for, the one the next paragraph acts on, when the section already implies it. (An illustration, not his:
   "It doesn't have to be fancy." at the end of a paragraph about first dates would become "It doesn't have to be fancy,
   but you should both know what you're hoping for.")

19. **Three of a kind, or a question inside another.** A list of three items, clauses or short sentences, a list of
   category words, or a run of questions where one is part of another or two overlap. He keeps the items that carry the
   point, gives one a case from life, and turns a question into its real options. "bring the person to a party and see
   how they act, bring them around your family or friends, or take them to activities you enjoy" became "bring the
   person to a party, around your friends and family and see how they act, or take them to activities you enjoy", and
   "Are they wondering why you do this stuff?" became "Are they wondering why you spend hours playing with your cat?";
   "Were you able to regulate at all during the conflict? What did you do to the other person? How did you repair
   afterward? What did you learn?" became "Look at the most recent conflicts and ask if you were able to regulate
   yourself during them. What did you do to the other person, and how did you repair the damage afterward?"; "What's the
   pattern?" became "Is that a pattern, or just life getting in the way normally?"; "They ask, reach, name what they
   want, and ask the man to do things for them" became "They ask the man to do things for them, even when they could do
   it themselves, and they know it!"
   A list kept whole in one sentence still reads this way: keep the two items that carry the point, or one.
   But a list of concrete things from life (what someone eats, plays, makes, listens to or where they go) is not this
   way: keep it whole, in his order. Cut an item only when it overlaps another item or is part of it, and then cut that
   item, never the point it carries (a kind of harm, a person, a reason).

20. **A sentence about the text itself that isn't true where it stands.** A summing-up of what the article or the
   section has said so far, such as "As this whole guide has shown, every problem here comes down to trust" after
   sections about other things. Read it against what comes before it, and say only what is true of that.

21. **A label for the thing instead of the thing.** A sentence that names a concept for what happened, an abstract noun
   that acts, or a vague pointer where the thing itself should be said. "Look at what you contributed." became "Look at
   what you contributed to how things turned out."; "You might feel worthless or fight or flight mode may kick in."
   became "then you might deep down disbelieve that you're worthy of anyone staying with you"; "bonding chemistry ...
   that usually biases us toward attachment" became "bonding chemistry in our brain ... that usually helps us form
   attachments, so that we don't just abandon our children"; "“We're together” ... what does that mean exactly?" became
   "When we say, “We're together,” what does that mean exactly?"
   A question the reader couldn't answer as asked reads this way too: ask the real question instead.
   A question in two halves whose second half nobody could answer as asked is this way too: ask the dilemma the reader
   actually faces there, plainly, with its reason when the reason is what makes it hard.

22. **A contrast or a pivot said in general words.** A contrast whose second half says again, in general words, what the
   paragraph's own story already showed: keep the concrete half, drop the general one. A pivot that points back with
   "none of that", "all of that" or "this" names what it points back to, the way the reader would put it. (An
   illustration, not his: "But none of that explains why she left." would become "But the money trouble doesn't
   explain why she left.")

23. **A run of questions.** Three or more questions in a row, or a paragraph that is almost all questions, reads like
   a worksheet. He orders them by what they're about (an example sits right after the question it shows; the question
   about the long run comes last), keeps one frame (if the paragraph has the reader looking at their own record, a
   follow-up speaks to the reader's own experience, not to an interview with someone else), says in a plain statement
   between them why the next question matters when the text already implies it, and puts a consequence into the same
   question instead of a second question built like the first.

24. **A point after the story that interrupts it.** A general point that answers a list or a question, put after a
   personal story told in between, so it reads as if it followed from the story. He moves the point up next to what it
   answers, and the story after it, last, told as one sentence when its parts belong together. (An illustration, not
   his: "Therapy, a trip or a break can help. Our trip to Lisbon helped, though it rained. If one of you won't try,
   none of it works." would become "Therapy, a trip or a break can help, but if one of you won't try, none of it works.
   Our trip to Lisbon helped, though it rained.")


25. **Points out of their natural order.** When a section's Medium windows survive every rewording, the cause is often
   the order of its points, not their words. Map the points first: for each sentence, which point it makes and which
   earlier point it answers, qualifies or follows from. Then put each next to that point: the exception right after the
   claim it qualifies; a piece of advice where the situation it is for comes up; a story after the point it shows,
   ending on that point said plainly instead of a line that points back at it ("That's where it all goes wrong."); an
   alternative next to its downside; the limit last. His words, on a paragraph he fixed with two words and a move: "the
   problem was simply that you had the ideas out of their natural order". (An illustration, not his: "Shared kitchens
   need rules. Our office tried a rota. Each week someone cleaned the fridge, and after a month nobody did. Some teams
   run fine on goodwill alone. A sign by the sink helped for a while. That's where it all goes wrong." would become
   "Shared kitchens need rules, though some teams run fine on goodwill alone. Our office tried a rota, with someone
   cleaning the fridge each week, and after a month nobody did, so the job that was meant for everyone fell to no one. A
   sign by the sink helped for a while.")

Words he has said read as AI in his writing: "doesn't prove" or "isn't proof of"; "X does not Y" statements; "matters"
where a more specific word says more; "one ordinary"; "quietly"; "if it's safe" (say what safe means); "honestly" where
it is one too many (a real synonym, "genuinely" or "authentically", or the word for what is meant; it can stay where it
is the exact word).
