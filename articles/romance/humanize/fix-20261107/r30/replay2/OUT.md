## Item 1
MARKED:
- "Before me, Bee had also stayed with a boyfriend..." — way 10: "who was totally broke and crazy and supported him for a while" reads as if the boyfriend did the supporting; it was Bee. Way 13: it opens on her past, and the reader only finds out two sentences later why her ex comes up right after my knee.
- "Helping carry somebody through a temporary incapacity..." — way 12: it's the other side of "I agree" (my knee was a different thing), but nothing in it says so. Its contrast stays, since it answers the reader's real worry: wasn't her reluctance with my knee the lesson she took from that boyfriend?
- "Some women are fixer types, which can create the opposite pattern." — way 10 and 21: "the opposite pattern" could be the provider role turned around (her carrying him) or the opposite of her reluctance with my knee; it names a pattern instead of saying it. Way 6: the paragraph ends on a general line about "some women". Way 12: nothing ties it to Bee's story, which is what it generalizes from.
REWRITE:
Before me, Bee had also supported a totally broke and crazy boyfriend, and stayed with him for a while. Later she blamed herself for staying through that, and I agree. Some women are fixer types, which can flip the provider role around. But helping carry somebody through a temporary incapacity, like my knee, is different from becoming the permanent support system for a man who won't take responsibility.

## Item 6
MARKED:

REWRITE:
Sometimes what two people want is much more practical. They want to share a home and raise children, take care of each other, financially too, maybe have sex, without pretending it all depends on overwhelming romantic love. I'd call that a practical partnership, and there's nothing inherently wrong with it if both people know that's what they're doing. But I suspect they've simply never been in love, so they don't expect to be, and their arrangement becomes a problem if one of them ever does fall for someone.

## Item 8
MARKED:
- "Often times the breakup is hard, but the aftermath is worse." — way 3: a pair set against each other where it could say the thing itself. Way 2: its "but" is followed by another "but" in the next sentence.
- "Public demonization should be avoided, but..." — way 5 and 10: a rule with nobody's name on it (who shouldn't demonize whom? you, your ex); it's really an instruction. Way 13: the reader only finds out in the last sentence that this is about telling people. Way 14: a flat rule against what someone would naturally do; the section gives no reason why someone would want to, so I don't add one. (The "I don't think that means" stays: it answers the reader's real worry, that not demonizing means hiding abuse.)
- "Sometimes the truth is that you were abused, coerced, or seriously deceived." — way 19: three of a kind; "coerced" overlaps "abused".
- "So how much you say, and to whom, and how publicly..." — way 19 and 2: three items joined "and ..., and ...", and "to whom" overlaps "how publicly".
REWRITE:
Often times the aftermath is even worse than the breakup. When you tell people about the relationship, I'd avoid demonizing your ex publicly, but I don't think that means hiding the truth to save face. Sometimes the truth is you were abused or seriously deceived. So how much you say, and how publicly, really depends on what happened and why you're telling people.
