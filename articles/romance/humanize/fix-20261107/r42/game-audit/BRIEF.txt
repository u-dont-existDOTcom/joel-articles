5: # What we mean by “love” ❤️
6: English isn’t a scientific language when it comes to love. This leads to a lot of romantic confusion because we don’t have one word for one meaning.
7: One meaning is selfless love—what contemplative traditions call agape, metta, loving-kindness, divine love, and so forth. In Buddhist metta practice you sit there wishing, “May all beings be at ease, pure of heart, and free.” It’s not about them being yours.
8: Then we use the same word for romantic love (eros): "I want us to be special to each other, with our lives entangled. I want us to pay a lot of attention to each other and to desire each other sexually." I don’t think there’s anything fake or shameful about this.
9: A good relationship must have both kinds of love at once. Too often, the trouble is there’s a whole lot of I want you and hardly any I want you to be happy.
10: Most people fail this test: what would I want if she herself thought she’d be happier with somebody else? The first reaction is horror, but if they can get past it, wanting her and wanting her to be happy stop fighting each other. Being wanted is part of what makes me want someone, so if she’d be happier with somebody else, I’d want her less anyway.
11: I still have to go through that horror, because I can’t wait until I know I’m wanted before I let her know I want her, and if we both do that, nothing gets started. 12: Agape or divine love does two jobs at once to rescue erotic love. First, most obviously, it keeps eros from becoming totally selfish and fickle by making the other person’s happiness part of the wanting: I want you because I know you’ll be happy with me. Second, it gives eros a landing pad. When two people pull away because eros seems unreciprocated for a moment, the backdrop of actual, genuine care, keeps the couple from simply abandoning each other, and then they might even rekindle the eros when they see that it’s based on something deeper.
13: Eros can help build the agape too. It has a drive to it: it keeps pulling me toward this particular person, and over time that closeness can grow an agape that survives the drive itself.

105: ### The Missing Middle
106: Modern life barely has an intermediate stage between being interested in somebody and getting sexually entangled. 107: Well, it didn’t exactly solve the problem: in late-1700s New England, where lots of couples bundled, 30–40 percent of brides were already pregnant on their wedding day. I’m not trying to bring bundling back. I just think we need some stage that everybody recognizes, where two people can explore tenderness and attraction without immediately sliding toward sex, moving in together, and a life bond.

115: ### Peeking thru the window
116: Before the window of clarity closes, the main thing is to find out whether you are actually compatible. Do you have the same goals and values, especially around children, religion, politics, health, love, and romance? Where do you want to live? Do you both want to find a community? What kind of relationship do you want with technology and money?
117: So we have to find a way to ask deep questions. Conversation-card games may seem silly, but they can help people get past superficial talk and see if they’re compatible.
118: But asking, “How do you handle jealousy?” can get me a beautiful answer that tells me almost nothing. I’d rather ask what she thinks she would do in an actual situation. If all she can give me is a general answer, I assume she probably doesn’t know yet.
119: But watch out, because ironically, a card game to get closer and deeper can backfire! If we spend two hours telling each other things nobody else knows, we may feel incredibly understood and close, but that may mean the game worked without telling us whether the relationship will.
120: You can also bring the person to a party, around your friends and family and see how they act, or take them to activities you enjoy and see if they really enjoy them too (although if they seem to enjoy every single one of them, they might be pretending). Try doing what you would normally do in your day alone, but with them, and see if it feels weird. Are they wondering why you spend hours playing with your cat? Do they look bored, like they’d rather just hit you up later for some fun?
121: See their negative side first too. If there is no negative side, that’s a red flag for fakery. Ask whether the spiritual story they tell about themselves survives an inconvenient PMS afternoon. How recent is the healing story—two months or two years? You may not see their jealousy, abandonment and possessiveness until there is stress and entanglement, and that’s also when you find out whether the “I’m so much better than I used to be” story survives. By then, of course, you’re stressed and entangled. 😆
122: Sometimes the only thing I have is a little “wait” feeling. If I'm about to ignore a question because I really want this to work, that "wait" is enough reason for me to ask the question before I keep going.
123: If they’re nasty to their friends, family, or other people, assume they will eventually be nasty to you. 124: ### The conversation about flaws
125: Card games and social gatherings don’t tell you everything, but asking someone out of the blue, “What did you do wrong in your past relationships?” would make for a pretty weird date.
126: At the right time, though, in the right way (especially if they bring it up tangentially), I would ask about their past relationships, especially the recent ones. If every story ends with everything being the other person’s fault, I’d ask, “Okay, so what lessons did you find for yourself?”
127: “I have baggage” isn't as useful as learning what you're having trouble with now. What might make it hard for you to function well in a relationship?
128: I’d want both of us to be honest about how well we actually handle our own issues. What addictions or impulsive behaviors can’t we reliably control? What are we probably going to ask forgiveness for more than once? That tells me more than either of us proclaiming that we’re healed.
129: Look at the most recent conflicts and ask if you were able to regulate yourself during them. What did you do to the other person, and how did you repair the damage afterward? Not doing can be just as bad as overdoing. If conflict makes you shut down, does it mean you stop talking to your partner for days, leaving them feeling abandoned?
130: Has your judgment gotten better, or would your friends or exes say you keep falling for the same stuff?

323: # Two Pillars Don’t Hold The Roof Up

327: Ideally, a friend who actually knows us both sees the dysfunctional pattern before either of us does. But having the same confidant creates the opposite problem: now you may not have a release valve when you want privacy from your partner, so neither way is perfect.
328: And if both people are really falling apart, sometimes there is only so much anyone else can do to help.
329: I lived all the permutations of this with Bee, except that we never lived in community. I suspect if we had, we might still be together. 332: So if you don’t have a real community already, what can you do about it practically, right now?
333: Make as much community as you can wherever you are, like going to Meetups together and making deep friendships as a couple. Find some shared purpose beyond maintaining the relationship. Introduce your old friends to your partner and try to mutualize those friendships as much as possible, so they aren’t experienced as slyly antagonistic or partial to one side. You don’t need to spend all your time together, but two completely separate social worlds will automatically start taking sides when something goes wrong.
334: # What are you actually choosing together?
335: A lot of romantic pain starts with two lovey bunnies entering one of the biggest arrangements of their lives without ever discussing their ideas, dreams, and fears about it.
336: When we say, “We’re together,” what does that mean exactly? I can hear a whole future in those two words—sex, exclusivity, living together, money, children, caregiving. She might mean, “We really like each other; let’s see what happens.” I actually like letting a relationship flow with some parts left unnamed, but the problem is when we’re using the same words for two different futures.
337: Sometimes what two people want is a practical partnership: sharing a home, rearing kids and taking care of each other. Many people in the world actually believe sex is part of that practical arrangement, and they don't think romance has to be the basis of all that. There’s nothing inherently wrong with that arrangement if both people know that’s what they’re doing. But I suspect they simply have never been in love, so they don’t have that expectation. And that becomes a problem if they ever do fall for someone.
338: Another honest option is a romance with humility, even if romance and humility seem to clash. Two people really are in love and want to build something together, and they can choose exclusivity, shack up, have children, and plan a life, as long as nobody pretends they can guarantee what either heart will feel ten years later.
339: Then the questions change for me: if the romance changes, which parts are still supposed to remain? Are we promising actions (or low-key pretending to promise permanent feelings)? Which needs are we still going to meet outside the relationship?
340: And the biggie: what happens if one of us falls in love with someone else? Is that, in and of itself, a betrayal, since it's not under our control? If our lives change, can we compassionately sit down and rethink the agreement? And if we split up, what do we still owe each other (and our kids, if we have any)?
341: These are just a few questions I could think of to start the ball rolling, but be creative and see what you come up with! You can make a card game out of this and have some fun with it. If there’s real love, it will survive a bit of honest curiosity. And again, community can make all of this a lot less scary.
342: ## Which marriage vows are honest?
343: Conventional marriage vows are dishonest and inept, and most people know it. You can’t promise how you’ll feel in the future, but you can promise actions if you really believe you’re capable of delivering them. So for example, you could promise to tell the truth, to keep showing up emotionally, to support the children, or whatever else you genuinely believe you can do even if your feelings change. More importantly, though, if you do keep those promises, it's quite likely that your connection will deepen, so it's not like we truly have no control of future feelings, but just not completely, directly or unilaterally.
344: We also find something like my warning in the New & Old Testaments. Ecclesiastes 5:4–6 says it is better not to make a vow than to make one and fail to keep it, and that if you then tell the priest you didn’t mean what you promised, God will be angry and destroy your work.
345: Matthew 5:33–37 and James 5:12 make the wedding vow itself look even more suspicious (to me, anyway): let your “Yes” be “Yes” and your “No” be “No,” and anything beyond that comes from evil.
346: Where did the idea of promising to have romantic interest in your partner come from, anyway? It turns out it never existed in any culture I’m aware of until the 19th century, when people in England began citing romance as their reason for marriage. That trend has continued evolving like a snowball until today. But what our ancestors knew was not wrong: You can sign a contract about what you’ll do, but feelings aren’t contractual.
347: ### Attraction and exclusivity
348: It’s hard to find sexually monogamous animals; have you ever looked? And as it turns out, we humans aren’t a natural exception to the rule, either. Plenty of tribal cultures were (and some still are) much looser about this, while still generally retaining the primary-partner “social monogamy.” Sexclusivity started gaining sway around the time we started planting crops and owning land. That’s when it made sense to keep track of how to keep our land in the family bloodline.
349: Ironically, sexclusivity, which we treat as the basis of romance, started out as that kind of bureaucratic record-keeping. By the 17th century, when England began mass urbanization by forcing peasants off their land, it became the legal definition of marriage. As for what comes naturally to us underneath that law, I’m not trying to say we’re just like bonobos, but the academic consensus is that humans tend toward flexible pair-bonding, with a propensity for occasional infidelity.

366: ## Imagination as a discernment tool
367: We all fantasize about a new exciting person we just met, right? The useful thing about the fantasy is that it shows me what I want. But I usually make the mistake of playing out just the first part of the fantasy. So I might start with the regular lovey sexy stuff, and that’s fine because that’s what’s drawing me in. Then after that, when I have a bit of wisdom available, I continue onto the mundane and difficult scenes. I’m not trying to force anything or oppose anything. I just try to open my eyes when they want to close, ignore, look away. Here’s my checklist:
368: - What’s it feel like after we have sex? Do we feel comfortable and happy about that, or do we feel guilty that we gave in to temptation too quickly and now there’s some weird feelings? Name the feelings and explore.
369: - How about a week into the relationship? What’s starting to get annoying perhaps? What are we trying not to say and not to notice?
370: - Get in the ring before the ring gets on you. Let’s imagine we have a real fight about money, jealousy, or something else important. What do we do to handle the conflict?
371: - Fast forward. Now we’ve got kids. What might be the values conflicts in how we raise them? Schools, screen time, vaxxes, religion, helicoptering vs freedom, food, gender & sexual orientation issues.
372: - Rewind: what if we disagree on having kids?
373: - Fast forward five years: what has each of us gained or given up, and do we have any resentment about it?
374: This isn’t to make predictions and we’re not consulting the magic 8 ball nor doing Istikhara rituals here. I mean you can do that too if you want. Distinguish between fears and what really seems likely just as you do between wishes and reality.
375: If every scene is beautiful, consider that you may be, and almost certainly are, still rehearsing the relationship you want. There’s nothing wrong with expecting the best, but it takes work to get there in a wise way.
376: And if psychedelics are involved, every scene can look even more beautiful, which makes this same problem of idealization much stronger.
377: ## Psychedelic Timing
378: This section will be relevant to some readers and easy for others to skip.
379: I’m putting the warning before anything good I say about psychedelics for relationships, because people need to hear it before they take anything. I don’t think sexual consent should be negotiated for the first time while high, when the drug is changing how safe, loving, open, and connected everything feels. And even if the two of you plan it all in advance, getting high and starting a relationship is still a risk.
380: Starting a relationship through psychedelics has another danger. Key and I met and then spent a few days taking iboga with friends. We got to know each other very deeply that way, and that was great. But it was especially her higher self I was getting to know. I tend to idealize people even sober, and that gets worse when I’m high. It was still beautiful to see the real higher self potential in her, I’m not denying that. But that information didn’t help me understand how we would get along in normal life.
381: I’ve also had friends get together at MDMA parties and stuff. It’s rarely a good idea. The intimacy can be completely real without telling you whether the two of you actually work together sober. Or you might even sometimes have the opposite problem, like I had with H., where the connection continues to deepen while sober, and it becomes something that one person might cling to desperately, never imagining they could find that again.
382: The obvious advice is to wait before making large promises and spend time together sober, around other people, and in ordinary situations. Write down what each of you learned, do some peer counseling, check with friends to see if you’re too pie-in-the-sky. But the problem is, you won’t necessarily be interested in sober advice when you’re high. You’re “beyond all that.” And the high can continue after sobriety because intimacy itself is like rose-colored glasses.
383: ### Returning to the heart to get unstuck
384: Psychedelics, especially certain types of shrooms, very pure LSD, very pure therapeutic-grade MDMA, ayahuasca, loveyhuasca haoma, and iboga, can be extremely useful when a couple is trapped in defensive stories.
385: With Bee and Key, there were many times when we could argue forever sober, then take something and finally understand each other. It could feel like we were telepathically synced. Instead of each person defending their version, we directly understood what the other was perceiving, feeling, misunderstanding.
386: But the problem is most people are lazy about integrating what they learn during this window of clarity. We enjoyed the understanding while it lasted, and then the same defensive story would resurface later. In neuroscience, this is called state-dependent learning. What you learn in altered states doesn’t automatically translate into your normal sober mind state without the work to do that.
387: ### Measuring the benefits
388: Probably one of the most important benefits we can hope to get long term from any therapy is the ability to express what we feel in a non-combative way. Otherwise, people tend to ping pong between not expressing or downplaying their feelings and expressing them combatively. MDMA can make this temporarily much easier because all the problems can look so small and silly. The question is what happens when you’re sober again.
389: So I would say the therapy worked if a week or two later, you and your partner can comfortably say, “Honey, I’m feeling unappreciated right now. I know you’re not trying to make me feel that way, and maybe you feel like I’m making you feel that way, but I’d like us to take some time soon to discuss the issue beneath this in a kinder way. What do you say?”
390: If you don’t have a partner, you can do the same thing with whoever you tend to have trouble relating with, in a less lovey way but still with compassion.
391: It’s not always possible in that moment to change a defensive posture into non-defensive loving communication. That’s why I suggest setting a time soon with a nice intention, like a ritual. Sober is fine. You can put some rose petals around you or something to build the feeling, or play some song you both like.
392: ## Why all of this sounds artificial
393: By now you’re probably thinking all of this sounds terribly artificial: is anyone actually going to do scheduled check-ins, delayed cohabitation, conversations about hypothetical disasters, imagination exercises, not to mention resisting the heat-of-the-moment stuff and continuing your own reparenting practices when a warm body is available to do some of the soothing?
394: Well, yeah, it is painfully artificial, because in a healthy communal society, the couple would have access to witnesses, elders, friends, and other emotional outlets and resources. Most of us these days don’t have that, so we’re trying to recreate consciously and clumsily some of what the wider field would have done without a checklist.
395: # If you’re already in it
396: Most of this article is about what to do before jumping head first into head over shoulders. But if you’re already married with children, advice about slowing down arrives a little late.
397: I wish I had a special method for fixing that. Mostly I learned the obvious thing the hard way: one person can’t fix the relationship (unless they’re the main problem in it).
398: With Bee and Key, the biggest thing sitting underneath everything was trust. They wanted to be with me and would say they trusted me, but really they didn’t trust me, themself, or anyone else. It was all or nothing: blind trust, or none. I kept saying, “It’s not possible to have a relationship without trust.” I think that rigidity came from trauma and never really growing up.
399: Even dishonesty was sometimes hard for me to define in these relationships. Bee once opened a door and, a second later, said she hadn’t opened it. That drove me crazy, because why would she lie like that? On the other hand, although she was technically lying, there was no strategy behind it, and she was usually quite honest. Non-strategic lying is literally defined as "pathological lying," so I should have seen it more like an illness than a failure of virtue.
400: Some things were less mysterious: they were acting as if the trust problem wasn’t there because they still wanted the relationship. Attractions to other people sometimes went unsaid too, and sometimes got imagined from nowhere. Some people keep a relationship like this going for a long time, just sweeping everything under the rug.
401: On the other hand, sometimes I was more honest than my partner could handle, which was its own kind of damage. Ask whether both of you actually want to prioritize radical honesty, first. But the other person also can’t keep saying “not now” forever and think that’s going to save the relationship.
402: If the two of you are stuck in that “not now,” that’s when you need agape the most, because the best thing for both of you may not be holding onto the relationship pattern you originally committed to or dreamt of.
403: When did you two last dance? And not the “we dance around our problems” joke (LOL).. If that’s where things are at, ouch, the trust is out the window. I know that one from experience with my first 2 wives and K, too. That generally marks the point of no return, so try not to wait for that. Because once you’re there, as my dad explained a thousand times whenever we had a visitor, unconscious resentment begins to snowball, and we feel colder together than alone. Pretty soon, old Romeo & Juliette might get wandering eye syndrome.
404: ## Outside help can sometimes break the loop fast
405: Bee and I were once desperately misunderstanding each other, so we called my friend Eshwar, the only friend of mine whom she respected. He told a related story about something that had happened with his sister. Somehow, Bee immediately understood what I had been trying to say. It was like magic.
406: So, don’t think of outside help as a formal last resort. I think of it as a part of building community when we learn to rely on each other for this kind of intimate, real-life support. It could be calling up a mutual friend, peer counselor, couples therapist, doing Imago dialogue, Restorative Circle, and/or joining a men’s or women’s group. The key is that both people need to respect the person or process enough to hear something new.
407: What they say is also partly true: “The only one you can change is yourself.” Not fully true, because we do have influence, and changing ourself does ripple out. Restart whatever you stopped doing because the relationship was soothing you: inner-child reparenting, meditation, body practices, peer counseling.
408: ## What will honesty produce?
409: Many people ask this question of honesty, but it’s unfair. Honesty doesn’t produce a certain outcome. It is its own reward. It might repair the relationship, open it, turn the romance into friendship, preserve the family through separation, or show you that ending it is better. Of course, if the other person won’t look at the truth, you can’t make honesty a mutual value. Then you have to decide what you can live with. Temporary separation helped Bee and I, although it became less temporary as time went on, but I’ve always thought maybe we would get back together in a few decades.
410: # Children
411: I think everyone generally agrees that parents have more responsibility to children than they do to each other, since children can’t take care of themselves.
412: Before having or adopting children, sit down for an imagination session to figure out whether you two lovebirds can preserve some civil co-parenting capacity after a terrible breakup. That’s the actual long-term commitment.
413: When it comes to stepchildren, even if they aren’t technically your children, they still become attached. If you’ve been acting as their parent for years, it’s not right to vanish from their life just because the marriage ended. You don’t have legal parental rights, but you can stay a mentor or close friend, and hopefully your partner would agree with this. When children have parental figures come and go, it can damage their ability to have securely attached relationships as adults.
414: It’s standard advice to never recruit children into the adult war. They get their own relationship with the other parent. However, this was particularly difficult during my first marriage, because Ann was very much unfit at the time to care for our son, Bear. I did fight for custody, but, as my lawyer told me, there was no point, because men generally lose. Eventually I exhausted my options for fighting, and I was left with weekly supervised visits.
415: Ann was brainwashing Bear into believing that I was “breaking his bones” at these visits even though he didn’t know what bones were. That’s when I told him I had to stop the visits before I end up in jail. So it was for my protection, but his as well, because if he realizes one day his dad is in jail because his mom manipulated him into telling a dangerous story, that could really wreck him.
416: As it is now, he may miss me, but he is well cared for (I think) by his grandmother, and probably soon he’ll seek me out when he gets his own phone.
417: I know I sound like a broken record, like my dad, but still: It takes a village. The nuclear family, built on parents’ romance, can often be the worst arrangement for children. If there’s trouble in paradise, parents will try to protect the kids by hiding their tension. But the truth is, children absorb that unresolved tension and integrate it into their very being. In a community, romance is one bond among several, and adult stability for children doesn’t rise and fall with it.
418: # Ending consciously
419: Before you think about how to leave, settle whether you’re leaving, and whether counseling is worth trying first.
420: You don’t owe it to them to try counseling unless you also owe it to yourself. If you actually don’t want a good relationship with them anymore, go. No amount of counseling will fix something that has nothing at the base of it.
421: If you love them and wish it could work, ask if they feel the same, and then go to counseling. On the other hand, even when you love each other, sometimes you already know the issue is beyond what counseling could fix.
422: ### Gaslighting in the dark
423: Do you have irreconcilable memories of key events? I did with both my first and second wives. I took my first wife, Ann, to two counselors. The male counselor eventually told us, “I’ve never told anyone this, but you guys need to split up for at least six months.”
424: There’s no way to resolve a situation where one person remembers that you ruined her life on purpose, while you remember that she was having a manic episode and had to be committed for her own safety. We also didn’t have mutual friends who could corroborate what happened—only friends on each side who were partial.
425: Many say it’s best to save face and work things out privately, but I’d disagree. If you don’t have friends who can help with the conflict, then you’re really stuck (and you might wanna find some new friends). And when you do ask friends for help, do it fairly, with the goal of resolving the conflict, not just getting approval for your own interpretation: both people should get a chance to speak, and good friends should hear both sides before validating either.
426: I can believe a friend’s experience and still tell them that my ability to know what is really going on is extremely limited. If someone says, “I don’t have all the evidence,” I trust their thinking more. Living in community would help because your friends would not only hear two stories afterward. Sometimes they would actually see how the two of you treat each other when you argue.
427: If you’ve been there for someone, don’t abandon them in a crisis unless it’s unsafe for you to stay or they’re creating the crisis to keep you, or they aren’t willing to help themself. You may need to arrange other care, but that’s no reason to pretend the romance still has a future.
428: ## After leaving
429: Often times the breakup is hard, but the aftermath is worse. Public demonization should be avoided, but I don’t think that means hiding the truth about the relationship to save face. Sometimes, the truth is that you were abused, coerced, or seriously deceived. So how much you say, and to whom, really depends on what happened and why you’re telling people.
430: Once you’re out, you can see things you genuinely couldn’t see while you were bonded. Look at what you contributed to how things turned out. And don’t paint your ex as a cartoon villain: try to understand whatever was true in their perspective too, including the conflicts they were carrying inside themselves.
431: Sometimes the easiest story is that they were fake all along, because it explains everything. Grab it only if it really is the simplest explanation of what happened, and even the curiosity about that can be therapeutic for you. People who aren’t heavily invested in seeing your innocence can help you tell, and a therapist, pastor, or even a stranger may see the evidence more clearly than a lifelong friend.
432: Spiritual practice during an ending is critical, but don’t use it to get around the feelings. Pray for your ex’s wellbeing. Remember what was real and good, and don’t use your pain as permission to hate them. Avoid the New Age belief that everyone is simply a mirror of you. Of course, we do partly mirror one another, but each person was formed by plenty that had nothing to do with you. No matter how enlightened the Buddha was, his cousin Devadatta still kept trying to kill him: looking honestly at your part doesn’t erase theirs. Sometimes it helps to ask, “What would Mr. Rogers do?” (if you’re old enough to remember that show).
433: I also learned that public demonization doesn’t always have to be the last chapter, even if things ended on that bitter note. If you avoid contact forever, they might never apologize and learn honesty. But it can help to keep distance for a while until cooler heads prevail. On the other hand, it can be tempting to let them back into your life after a private apology, and especially after a public one, but keep in mind you deserve a stable love. You’re not a yo-yo.
434: ## What I gained from loss
435: At this point, I can say I’m better off because of what happened with Bee, Key, and H., without pretending every part of those relationships was beneficial or destined. Loss taught me things love alone never would, or to paraphrase Rumi, “You must keep breaking your heart until it stays open.” (The real quote: “The reed must be torn from its bed, cut, hollowed out, and pierced with holes (wounded) before it can channel the breath of the musician into music.”)
436: From Bee, I learned what it means for me to be really in love. I learned the kind of woman I actually want: deeply spiritual, sensitive, creative, unique, somebody who inspires me and makes me question everything.
437: Key repeated a lot of that, which is why I fell in love with her. I learned some of the same harsh lessons from both of them: don’t take openness and curiosity to be fully good. They can also indicate that someone hasn’t developed their own sense of self or opinions, or enough inner adult.
438: From H., I learned that I can be extremely close to someone and feel completely understood and supported without actually falling in love. I still treasure the support we gave/give each other.
439: Too many people judge a relationship only by whether they stayed together. Relationships are a process of growth in many dimensions, so start measuring what you actually learned from yours.
440: # Tough Love
441: Romantic love is not easy, and the things that actually make it work are not very marketable. I can’t sell you the fix with one click or one monthly subscription to my love class. (Just kidding, click here!)
442: A passionate romantic relationship that’s also stable is rare, and that’s probably why romantic love has been seen with suspicion as a destabilizing force throughout history. One of the best books that makes that point today might be the one I was reading with Key: The Spirit of Intimacy, by Sobonfu Somé. Somé came from the Dagara tribe in Burkina Faso. To her people, marriage is an arranged, ritualized spiritual and communal responsibility, and they’re suspicious of romance. But one of her ideas fits what I’m trying to say: the relationship is bigger and more important than the egos in it. She calls it the spirit of the relationship.
443: That spirit is like the universe living its desire to know itself through us, which might be why we see ourselves in ways we couldn’t imagine before. Nobody can command the spirit to remain. At our best, we serve it and listen for what Spirit wants to change. When we start thinking we own it or deserve it, that presumptuousness tends to push it away.
444: My parents agreed with Somé that community matters and that we serve Spirit. They were also not romantic, though they were loving and poly. I actually wished they were in love with each other, I guess, although I couldn’t have articulated that since I didn’t even have that concept at the time. And like Somé, who saw everyone as naturally good in their natural environment, flowing with life the way our ancestors showed us, they trusted the universe, rose-colored glasses and all, and taught me that everybody was basically good. So I learned something about love and community, but not how hard romance is without a community around it, or how much damage basically good people can do when their fears, trauma, and undeveloped parts take over.
445: It took me many decades to figure out what probably everyone else already knows: real private pathology exists. But pathology festers when the environment is perfect for it. The dominant culture hermetically seals two people together and calls that a luxurious privacy. It tells them that sealed room should provide all the fresh air you’d get from a lover, family, community, therapist, and so on. Then it treats the predictable failure as privately caused as well, like mold that grows out of nowhere.
446: The myths around romantic love become real because we co-create them. We act as though jealousy proves love, permanent feelings can be promised, conflict should stay hidden, and the relationship failed if it ended. Then we suffer inside ideas that were never natural laws, and we walk blindly into our first, second, third, fourth, and fifth marriages!
447: It might be that I wrote this whole article for my son, Bear, who sadly, I last saw when he was about five, right before he was supposed to get the Rosenblum sex talk.
448: Bear, sex can be what you do when you’re older and find a friend you love in a super special way that makes you want to raise children together. Don’t imagine you love each other just because you want each other. Don’t feel pressure to promise what your heart will feel. Let the love and bond it produces blossom genuinely as your hearts sync. Learn about each other before getting too close, and make sure to keep friends and community around who know both of you.
449: I believe Rumi was right: A sacred relationship will open and purify your hearts regardless of whether it ends.
