[0] ## Affection and the simmer

[1] Doug Toft, who has been married for fifty years, has a useful list called 50 Things I Learned from 50 Years of Marriage. One of his points is to touch his wife without an agenda. A hug, cuddle, kiss, or back rub should sometimes be allowed to end right there, or she’ll start treating every touch like a sales pitch.

[2] Kim Anami calls the sexual current between encounters “the simmer”. Maybe she texts from work, “I can’t wait to touch you.” Maybe he tells her what he wants to do later. If we supposedly want each other but hardly ever flirt or let each other know it, that’s called taking each other for granted. Great sex probably isn’t going to materialize out of nowhere at bedtime just based on the flirting you did 5 years ago.

[3] It shouldn’t become relationship homework, either: “Hey babe, Kim Anami told me to flirt with you more.”

[4] And if our sex life suddenly changes, keep some curiosity about why rather then automatically going with the flow and creating a potential “new normal.” Maybe we’re pissed off at each other. Maybe one of us doesn’t feel wanted. Maybe somebody’s sick, stressed, on a new medication, whatever.

[5] I also don’t want my partner to have to manufacture my desire for me. And if sex is one of the main things separating our relationship from friendship, giving it whatever exhausted scraps are left after everything else seems pretty dumb.

[6] ### Situationships

[7] And than there’s the situationship, which is hard to define because that’s basically the point, but usually it’s a happenstance relationship based on sex. There’s less communication, and what communication exists is more superficial. Yet it is obviously some kind of relationship even while one or both people refuse to name it. You’re regularly having sex, going out together, spending time alone together, and doing things you wouldn’t do with a regular friend, so it feels like it’s filling a relationship void, but only accidentally and occasionally—maybe just enough to keep you from looking for anything deeper.

[8] It can go either way, but often the man wants to keep things undefined while the woman wants a big diamond ring, partly because she’s the one risking pregnancy (and maybe she can at least sell the ring later if things don't work out). Whoever keeps things undefined gets the fun without the responsibility, supposedly, but people still get pregnant and attached, whatever they call the relationship. And the one who's trying to keep things undefined still ends up with the existential void another superficial fix creates.

[9] They might ask, “Why are you making this heavy?” As if it's all your fault. But instead of taking that personally, you can re-frame it as if they said: "Don’t you want the fun? Are you about to give up what we've got?" My answer to that would be something like: "I’m trying to understand weather this is really fulfilling me or if it’s so superficial that the void it's filling keeps getting deeper."

[10] I had something sort of like that with H., although ours was much deeper than what people normally mean by situationship, and painfully uneven. We were (and still are) BFFs, and spent nearly all our time together. She was falling in love with me, and I wasn’t falling in love with her. She felt more like a sister to me, partly because I’m not attracted to white women.
