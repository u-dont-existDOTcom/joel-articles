It’s not always possible in that moment to change a defensive posture into non-defensive loving communication. That’s why I suggest setting a time soon with a nice intention, like a ritual. Sober is fine. You can put some rose petals around you or something to build the feeling, or play some song you both like.

By now this probably sounds terribly artificial. Is anyone actually going to do scheduled check-ins, delayed cohabitation, conversations about hypothetical disasters, imagination exercises, not to mention resisting the heat-of-the-moment stuff and continuing your own reparenting practices when a warm body is available to do some of the soothing?

Well, yeah. It is painfully artificial. In a healthy communal society, the couple would have access to witnesses, elders, friends, and other emotional outlets and resources. Most of us these days don’t have that, so we’re trying to recreate consciously and clumsily some of what the wider field would have done without a checklist.

Most of this article is about what to do before jumping head first into head over shoulders. But if you’re already married with children, advice about slowing down arrives a little late.

I wish I had a special method for fixing that. Mostly I learned the obvious thing the hard way: one person can’t fix the relationship (unless they’re the main problem in it).

With Bee and Key, the biggest thing sitting underneath everything was trust. They wanted to be with me and would say they trusted me, but really they didn’t trust me, themself, or anyone else. It was all or nothing: blind trust, or none. I kept saying, “It’s not possible to have a relationship without trust.” I think that rigidity came from trauma and never really growing up.

Even dishonesty was sometimes hard for me to define in these relationships. Bee once opened a door and, a second later, said she hadn’t opened it. That drove me crazy. Why would she lie like that? She was lying, but “she lied” only named the momentary behavior. There was no strategy behind it, and she was usually quite honest. Looking back, I could have understood the lie in the context of some kind of mental illness instead of treating it like strategic dishonesty.

Some things were less mysterious. They were acting as if the trust problem wasn’t there because they still wanted the relationship. Attractions to other people sometimes went unsaid too, and sometimes got imagined from nowhere. Some people keep a relationship like this going for a long time, just sweeping everything under the rug.

On the other hand, sometimes I was more honest than my partner could handle, which was its own kind of damage. Ask whether both of you actually want to prioritize radical honesty, first. But the other person also can’t keep saying “not now” forever and think that’s going to save the relationship.

This is the time for agape to shine, because the best thing for both of you may not be holding onto the relationship pattern you originally committed to or dreamt of.

When did you two last dance? And not the “we dance around our problems” joke (LOL).. If that’s where things are at, the trust is out the window. Ouch. I know that one from experience with my first 2 wives and K, too. That generally marks the point of no return, so try not to wait for that. Because once you’re there, as my dad explained a thousand times whenever we had a visitor, unconscious resentment begins to snowball, and we feel colder together than alone. Pretty soon, old Romeo & Juliette might get wandering eye syndrome.

Bee and I were once desperately misunderstanding each other, so we called my friend Eshwar, the only friend of mine whom she respected. He told a related story about something that had happened with his sister. Somehow, Bee immediately understood what I had been trying to say. It was like magic.

So, don’t think of outside help as a formal last resort. I think of it as a part of building community when we learn to rely on each other for this kind of intimate, real-life support. It could be calling up a mutual friend, peer counselor, couples therapist, doing Imago dialogue, Restorative Circle, and/or joining a men’s or women’s group. The key is that both people need to respect the person or process enough to hear something new.

What they say is also partly true: “The only one you can change is yourself.” Not fully true, because we do have influence, and changing ourself does ripple out. Restart whatever you stopped doing because the relationship was soothing you: inner-child reparenting, meditation, body practices, peer counseling.

Many people ask this question of honesty, but it’s unfair. Honesty doesn’t produce a certain outcome. It is its own reward. It might repair the relationship, open it, turn the romance into friendship, preserve the family through separation, or show you that ending it is better. Temporary separation helped Bee and I. It also became more permanent, although I’ve always thought maybe we would get back together in a few decades. If the other person won’t look, you can’t make it mutual. Then you have to decide what you can live with.

I think everyone generally agrees that parents have more responsibility to children than they do to each other, since children can’t take care of themselves.

Before having or adopting children, sit down for an imagination session to figure out whether you two lovebirds can preserve some civil co-parenting capacity after a terrible breakup. That’s the actual long-term commitment.

When it comes to stepchildren, even if they aren’t technically your children, they still become attached. If you’ve been acting as their parent for years, it’s not right to vanish from their life just because the marriage ended. You don’t have legal parental rights, but you can stay a mentor or close friend, and hopefully your partner would agree with this. When children have parental figures come and go, it can damage their ability to have securely attached relationships as adults.

It’s standard advice to never recruit children into the adult war. They get their own relationship with the other parent. However, this was particularly difficult during my first marriage, because Ann was very much unfit at the time to care for our son, Bear. I did fight for custody, but, as my lawyer told me, there was no point, because men generally lose. Eventually I exhausted my options for fighting, and I was left with weekly supervised visits.

Ann was brainwashing Bear into believing that I was “breaking his bones” at these visits even though he didn’t know what bones were. That’s when I told him I had to stop the visits before I end up in jail. So it was for my protection, but his as well, because if he realizes one day his dad is in jail because his mom manipulated him into telling a dangerous story, that could really wreck him.

As it is now, he may miss me, but he is well cared for (I think) by his grandmother, and probably soon he’ll seek me out when he gets his own phone.

I know I sound like a broken record, like my dad, but still: It takes a village. The nuclear family, built on parents’ romance, can often be the worst arrangement for children. If there’s trouble in paradise, parents will try to protect the kids by hiding their tension. But the truth is, children absorb that unresolved tension and integrate it into their very being. In a community, romance is one bond among several, and adult stability for children doesn’t rise and fall with it.

Whether to leave and how to leave are separate questions.

You don’t owe it to them to try counseling unless you also owe it to yourself. If you actually don’t want a good relationship with them anymore, go. No amount of counseling will fix something that has nothing at the base of it.

If you love them and wish it could work, ask if they feel the same, and then go to counseling. On the other hand, even when you love each other, sometimes you already know the issue is beyond what counseling could fix.

Do you have irreconcilable memories of key events? I did with both my first and second wives. I took my first wife, Ann, to two counselors. The male counselor eventually told us, “I’ve never told anyone this, but you guys need to split up for at least six months.”

There’s no way to resolve a situation where one person remembers that you ruined her life on purpose, while you remember that she was having a manic episode and had to be committed for her own safety. We also didn’t have mutual friends who could corroborate what happened—only friends on each side who were partial.

Many say it’s best to save face and work things out privately, but I’d disagree. If you don’t have friends who can help with the conflict, then you’re really stuck (and you might wanna find some new friends). But do it honestly, with the goal of helping the conflict, not just getting approval for your own interpretation. Both people should have a chance to speak, and good friends shouldn’t automatically validate either side.

I can believe a friend’s experience and still tell them that my ability to know what is really going on is extremely limited. If someone says, “I don’t have all the evidence,” I trust their thinking more. Living in community would help because your friends would not only hear two stories afterward. Sometimes they would actually see how the two of you treat each other when you argue.

If you’ve been there for someone, don’t abandon them in a crisis unless it’s unsafe for you to stay or they’re creating the crisis to keep you, or they aren’t willing to help themself. You may need to arrange other care. That doesn’t mean you have to pretend the romance still has a future.

Often times the breakup is hard, but the aftermath is worse. Public demonization should be avoided, but I don’t think that means hiding the truth about the relationship to save face. Sometimes the truth is abuse, coercion, or serious deception. How much you say, to whom, and how publicly depends on what happened and why you’re telling people.

A breakup can expose things you genuinely couldn’t see while you were bonded. Look at what you contributed, but also try to understand whatever was true in your ex’s perspective, including the conflicts they were carrying inside themselves, rather than one-dimensionalizing them.

Sometimes the easiest story is that they were fake all along. Don’t grab that story just because it makes everything easier to explain, and don’t reject it if it really is the most parsimonious explanation. Ask people who aren’t heavily invested in seeing your innocence. A therapist, pastor, or even a stranger may see the evidence more clearly than a lifelong friend. Even the curiosity itself can be therapeutic for you.

Spiritual practice during an ending is critical, but don’t use it to get around the feelings. Pray for your ex’s wellbeing. Remember what was real and good, and don’t use your pain as permission to hate them. Avoid the New Age belief that everyone is simply a mirror of you. No, that isn’t true. We partly mirror one another, but each person was formed by plenty that had nothing to do with you. No matter how enlightened the Buddha was, his cousin Devadatta still kept trying to kill him. Looking honestly at your part doesn’t erase theirs. Sometimes it helps to ask, “What would Mr. Rogers do?” (if you’re old enough to remember that show).

I also learned that public demonization doesn’t always have to be the last chapter. If you avoid contact forever, they might never apologize and learn honesty. But it can help to keep distance for a while until cooler heads prevail. It can be tempting to let them back into your life after a private apology, and especially after a public one, but keep in mind you deserve a stable love. You’re not a yo-yo.

At this point, I can say I’m better off because of what happened with Bee, Key, and H., without pretending every part of those relationships was beneficial or destined. Loss taught me things love alone never would. And to paraphrase Rumi, “You must keep breaking your heart until it stays open.” (The real quote: “The reed must be torn from its bed, cut, hollowed out, and pierced with holes (wounded) before it can channel the breath of the musician into music.”)

From Bee, I learned what it means for me to be really in love. I learned the kind of woman I actually want: deeply spiritual, sensitive, creative, unique, somebody who inspires me and makes me question everything.

Key repeated a lot of that, which is why I fell in love with her. I learned some of the same harsh lessons from both of them: don’t take openness and curiosity to be fully good. They can also indicate that someone hasn’t developed their own sense of self or opinions, or enough inner adult.

From H., I learned that I can be extremely close to someone and feel completely understood and supported without actually falling in love. I still treasure the support we gave/give each other.

Too many people judge a relationship only by the result, so start measuring what you actually learned from it instead. Relationships are a process of growth in many dimensions. The result is not only whether you stayed together.

Romantic love is not easy, and the things that actually make it work are not very marketable. I can’t sell you the fix with one click or one monthly subscription to my love class. (Just kidding, click here!)

A relationship like this is rare, and that’s probably why romantic love has been seen with suspicion as a destabilizing force throughout history. One of the best books that makes that point today might be the one I was reading with Key: The Spirit of Intimacy, by Sobonfu Somé. Somé came from the Dagara tribe in Burkina Faso. To her people, marriage is an arranged, ritualized spiritual and communal responsibility. They’re suspicious of romance. But one of her ideas fits what I’m trying to say: the relationship is bigger and more important than the egos in it. She calls it the spirit of the relationship.

That spirit is like the universe living its desire to know itself through us, which might be why we see ourselves in ways we can’t imagine prior. Nobody can command the spirit to remain. At our best, we serve it and listen for what Spirit wants to change. When we start thinking we own it or deserve it, that presumptuousness tends to push it away.

My parents agreed with Somé that community matters and that we serve Spirit. They were also not romantic, though they were loving and poly. I actually wished they were in love with each other, I guess, although I couldn’t have articulated that since I didn’t even have that concept at the time. Also reminiscent of Somé, they also wore rose-colored glasses and taught me that everybody was basically good. So I learned something about love and community, but not how hard romance is without a community around it, or how much damage basically good people can do when their fears, trauma, and undeveloped parts take over.

It took me many decades to figure out what probably everyone else already knows: real private pathology exists. But pathology festers when the environment is perfect for it. The dominant culture hermetically seals two people together and calls that a luxurious privacy. It tells them that sealed room should provide all the fresh air you’d get from a lover, family, community, therapist, and so on. Then it treats the predictable failure as privately caused as well, like mold that grows out of nowhere.

The myths around romantic love become real because we co-create them. We act as though jealousy proves love, permanent feelings can be promised, conflict should stay hidden, and the relationship failed if it ended. Then we suffer inside ideas that were never natural laws, and we walk blindly into our first, second, third, fourth, and fifth marriages!

It might be that I wrote this whole article for my son, Bear, who sadly, I last saw when he was about five, right before he was supposed to get the Rosenblum sex talk.

Bear, sex can be what you do when you’re older and find a friend you love in a super special way that makes you want to raise children together. Don’t imagine you love each other just because you want each other. Don’t feel pressure to promise what your heart will feel. Let the love and bond it produces blossom honestly as your hearts sync. Learn about each other before getting too close, and make sure to keep friends and community around who know both of you.

I believe Rumi was right: A sacred relationship will open and purify your hearts regardless of whether it ends.
