## Item 17 (r36-71-which-marriage-vows-are-honest)

Section: Which marriage vows are honest?

Conventional marriage vows are dishonest and inept, and most people know it. You can’t promise how you’ll feel in the future, but you can promise actions if you really believe you’re capable of delivering them. So for example, you could promise to tell the truth, to keep showing up emotionally, to support the children, or whatever else you genuinely believe you can do even if your feelings change. More importantly, though, if you do keep those promises, it's quite likely that your connection will deepen, so it's not like we truly have no control of future feelings, but just not completely, directly or unilaterally.

We also find something like my warning in the New & Old Testaments. Ecclesiastes 5:4–6 says it is better not to make a vow than to make one and fail to keep it. [[MEDIUM (MODEL): If you tell the priest you didn’t mean what you promised, the passage warns that God will be angry and destroy your work.]]

Matthew 5:33–37 and James 5:12 make the wedding vow itself look even more suspicious (to me, anyway): let your “Yes” be “Yes” and your “No” be “No,” and anything beyond that comes from evil.

Where did the idea of promising to have romantic interest in your partner come from, anyway? It turns out it never existed in any culture I’m aware of until the 19th century, when people in England began citing romance as their reason for marriage. That trend has continued evolving like a snowball until today. But what our ancestors knew was not wrong: You can sign a contract about what you’ll do, but feelings aren’t contractual.
