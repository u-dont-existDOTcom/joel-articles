# What we mean by “love” ❤️

English isn’t a scientific language when it comes to love. This leads to a lot of romantic confusion because we don’t have one word for one meaning.

One meaning is selfless love—what contemplative traditions call [agape, metta, loving-kindness, divine love](http://love.u-dont-exist.com/), and so forth. In Buddhist metta practice you sit there wishing, “May all beings be at ease, pure of heart, and free.” It’s not about them being yours.

Then we use the same word for romantic love (eros): "I want us to be special to each other, with our lives entangled. I want us to pay a lot of attention to each other and to desire each other sexually." I don’t think there’s anything fake or shameful about this.

A good relationship must have both kinds of love at once. Too often, the trouble is there’s a whole lot of I want you and hardly any I want you to be happy.

Most people fail this test: what would I want if she herself thought she’d be happier with somebody else? The first reaction is horror, but if they can get past it, wanting her and wanting her to be happy stop fighting each other. Being wanted is part of what makes me want someone, so if she’d be happier with somebody else, I’d want her less anyway.

I still have to go through that horror, because I can’t wait until I know I’m wanted before I let her know I want her, and if we both do that, nothing gets started. Once we’re together I still can’t wait to be sure I’m wanted. If I start feeling less wanted, I may pull back, and she feels me pulling back and starts feeling less wanted herself, so she pulls back too.

Agape or divine love does two jobs at once to rescue erotic love. First, most obviously, it keeps eros from becoming totally selfish and fickle by making the other person’s happiness part of the wanting: I want you because I know you’ll be happy with me. Second, it gives eros a landing pad. When two people pull away because eros seems unreciprocated for a moment, the backdrop of actual, genuine care, keeps the couple from simply abandoning each other, and then they might even rekindle the eros when they see that it’s based on something deeper.

Eros can help build the agape too. It has a drive to it: it keeps pulling me toward this particular person, and over time that closeness can grow an agape that survives the drive itself.

That drive can weaken for reasons that aren’t really my fault. I can get old, sick, depressed, or grieving, and I would hope the agape is deep enough that she sticks by me through some of that even if it puts strain on eros. But if I just get bored with life, stop having much of a life outside her, and make her responsible for nearly all my warmth, meaning, reassurance, and excitement, that’s different. I don’t get to be shocked if she wants me less.

# Talk about making love before you do it

From here on, when I say making love, I’m talking about two people who care about each other deeply and feel super connected, using their bodies as part of that connection. If you’ve never experienced this, it may be like trying to explain the taste of an orange. If you don’t know what love is, you don’t know. That’s another conversation, and I’d suggest reading my [inner child reparenting guide](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/).

Having sex is just the physical act, whether that deeper connection is there or not. But sometimes it can create the connection also.

What I eventually took from my dad’s advice was a bigger question: would we like to raise children together, and are we ready for that? If we can really talk about raising children together, most of the other important questions come up on their own. What would sex mean between us? What kind of life do you and I want? What kind of life would we want for our child?

What do you actually want? Women are kind of notorious for proudly not knowing what they want, and pressuring men into making it look like men know what they want. But everyone should know, for themselves, what they want, regardless of gender norm pressure. So what have you learned from your past desires, fulfilled or not? (I learned that sex isn't everything, but it still counts for a lot.) Do certain things make your body relax and open? Is there anything kinky you need to feel comfortable saying out loud?

You may not know all the answers yet. “I don’t know, but I’m willing to find out with you” is a perfectly good answer.

And what is sex for each of you? One person may mainly think of bonding while the other thinks of play. Sometimes it may feel sacred; another time it may just be how you come down from a bad day. It doesn’t have to mean the same thing every time.

It’s important to talk about sexual compatibility before getting undressed, even if that kills the vibe. Or even especially if it does kill the vibe, actually, since that’s a red flag if talking ruins things. And this applies whether you’re leaning too libertine or too traditional. And once you are undressed, the communication doesn’t stop there either. Can you feel comfortable with each other enough to express what turns you on, what this means to you, and what you’re unsure about? It’s that psychological trust & intimacy that builds the bridge between “having sex” and “making love.”

I remember reading a Christian magazine’s letter from a newlywed Christian woman who found out that her husband didn’t have much interest in sex after she saved herself for marriage. He trapped her, because he knew she would not feel good about divorce due to the religion. Of course, it didn’t have to be nefarious. I had a relationship with E. that was a bit like that about 15yrs ago. She wanted sex once a week, and it was driving me nuts because my libido was thru the roof. She also had no interest in trying to take a safe herb or supplement to increase her desire. Of course everyone does change over time as well. If our libidos later diverge, it’s better to talk about what we’d do before either person is already hurt.

## Affection and the simmer

Doug Toft, who has been married for fifty years, has a useful list called [50 Things I Learned from 50 Years of Marriage](https://dougtoft.substack.com/p/50-things-i-learned-from-50-years). One of his points is to touch his wife without an agenda. A hug, cuddle, kiss, or back rub should sometimes be allowed to end right there. If every affectionate touch becomes a bid for sex, affection itself can start feeling like pressure.

Kim Anami calls the sexual current between encounters [“the simmer”](https://kimanami.com/meet-another-well-fked-man/). Maybe she texts from work, “I can’t wait to touch you.” Maybe he tells her what he wants to do later. If we supposedly want each other but hardly ever flirt or let each other know it, that’s called taking each other for granted. Great sex probably isn’t going to materialize out of nowhere at bedtime just based on the flirting you did 5 years ago.

It shouldn’t become relationship homework, either: “Hey babe, Kim Anami told me to flirt with you more.”

And if our sex life suddenly changes, keep some curiosity about why rather than automatically going with the flow and creating a potential “new normal.” Maybe we’re pissed off at each other. Maybe one of us doesn’t feel wanted. Maybe somebody’s sick, stressed, on a new medication, whatever.

I also don’t want my partner to have to manufacture my desire for me. And if sex is one of the main things separating our relationship from friendship, giving it whatever exhausted scraps are left after everything else seems pretty dumb.

## Can Casual Sex or a Situationship Actually Be Honest?

Your body doesn’t know that you picked someone up at a bar and agreed it was only for fun. Then the next morning, you’re wondering why they didn’t call. Well, not getting pulled in any further is the best outcome still available, actually, since you just played Russian Roulette with a potential life bond to a stranger.

“I don’t know whether I want something serious with you. I’m not pretending I do. I’d still like to have sex.”

That’s honest about what you want tonight, but don't forget that tonight is enough time to make a baby. If this girl gets pregnant, she’s gonna call you. Then what? You block her? Agree to child support? Try to convince her to abort if she thinks abortion is killing her child? I'm still shocked and saddened about that, 20 years later (assuming it was true). This is what penetrative sex can do. No contraception is perfect.

The STI part is easy: say what you know, or say you don’t know. Feelings aren’t as easy, unless you’re both really numb or robotic about sex.

You have to care what happens to the person afterward. Otherwise you’re using and abusing them for pleasure. Maybe they’re using you too. Congratulations, now it’s mutual.

A lot of “I only want fun” really means, “This is the only version of the relationship that gets me laid.” Sometimes I know I’m hoping it turns into love. Sometimes I only find out I was hoping that when it doesn’t.

### Situationships

And then there’s the situationship, which is hard to define because that’s basically the point. Usually it’s a happenstance relationship based on sex. There’s less communication, and what communication exists is more superficial. Yet it is obviously some kind of relationship even while one or both people refuse to name it. You’re regularly having sex, going out together, spending time alone together, and doing things you wouldn’t do with a regular friend, so it feels like it’s filling a relationship void, but only accidentally and occasionally—maybe just enough to keep you from looking for anything deeper.

It can go either way, but often the man wants to keep things undefined while the woman wants a big diamond ring, partly because she’s the one risking pregnancy (and maybe she can at least sell the ring later if things don't work out). Whoever keeps things undefined gets the fun without the responsibility, supposedly, but people still get pregnant and attached, whatever they call the relationship. And the one who's trying to keep things undefined still ends up with the existential void another superficial fix creates.

They might ask, “Why are you making this heavy?” As if it's all your fault. But instead of taking that personally, you can re-frame it as if they said: "Don’t you want the fun? Are you about to give up what we've got?" My answer to that would be something like: "I’m trying to understand whether this is really fulfilling me or if it’s so superficial that the void it's filling keeps getting deeper."

I had something sort of like that with H., although ours was much deeper than what people normally mean by situationship, and painfully uneven. We were (and still are) BFFs, and spent nearly all our time together. She was falling in love with me, and I wasn’t falling in love with her. She felt more like a sister to me, partly because I’m not attracted to white women.

### Labels Are Descriptive, Not Prescriptive

Here’s the funny thing about labels: you can call someone your boyfriend or girlfriend for years and still have one foot out the door, but the word at least shows pretty fast whether you both think you’re in the same relationship.

True commitment grows out of relational depth, not a label we put on it. If we spend hours telling each other our deepest longings and fears, then I’ll naturally be ready to help when you need me. I don’t need to promise that on paper, and if I did, that would be more like a red flag. If someone says “I love you, I feel you, I’m here for you,” yet they hardly ever talk to you about what matters, that’s an even more obvious red flag.

The person getting more of what they want may think the arrangement is fulfilling, but usually they’re just less aware that it isn’t. In college I had a friend with four girlfriends who boasted about it to girls while trying to get a fifth. I asked him, “But are you really happy?” He said, “No, dude, I’m miserable and empty inside.”

The full conversation is so big that almost nobody would have casual sex if they took it seriously. Which is why almost nobody has the full conversation. I can’t decide what is okay for anybody else. I can say that most people who defend casual sex are not being very honest with themselves about pregnancy, attachment, or what they might owe the person afterward.

Outside a loving poly community or tribe, I think honest casual sex is almost impossible. If you want something closer to “casual love-making” without quite so many ways to damage each other, you probably need to find a free-love community where the people aren’t disposable, the bonds can be acknowledged, and any children have a village. But if the community is specifically designed to prioritize free love, you might find it attracts people who have traumas that produce trouble with connecting deeply, as ex-Tamera leader, Martin Winiecki [wrote about](https://www.facebook.com/martin.winiecki/posts/pfbid02V7nrbc7VLgPyLe91si9ximruTC7kwx2XjPBzUmUTnYr6sPViCPaTa7BxD2wmHAaJl) after leaving recently.

# Should you be in a relationship at all?

This is a horrible catch-22. Wounded people get lonely, and loneliness itself can kill. But the wounds that make somebody desperate for a relationship may also make them a terrible person to be in one with. People don’t only trigger each other and learn lessons. Sometimes they terrorize or kill each other.

I’m pretty conservative about this, so I would tell most of my friends not to date yet. First love your inner child. If you can’t even do that, forget a relationship for now. Get a cat or a puppy. If you’re dishonest, can’t care what happens to another person, or keep turning every relationship into the same disaster, come on, you’re not ready.

You don’t have to be completely healed, but the present-day adult has to be able to parent whatever intimacy wakes up inside you, and responsible enough to deal with an actual child if sex creates one. If you can’t be a parent in either sense, you aren’t ready to date.

A good relationship can teach you self-love too, which is part of the problem: you may need one most when you’re least capable of creating a good one, recognizing one, or keeping it good. It depends what each of you are ready for and have the resources for. [Here’s a video that can help understand the long process](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wT0dze2vnb0).

### I wasn’t ready, but I was lucky

At twenty, I thought I was totally fine until I became enamored with Alba. She was Dominican, and warm in a way I didn’t understand. Kissing your BFF on the lips was normal to her. ### Spirituality to bypass intimacy

I’ve used spirituality as a replacement for intimacy a lot because intimacy was simply unavailable. As a boy and young man, I was so deprived of female touch that it was driving me insane. Meditation sometimes helped me cope better with the deprivation, but it wasn’t a cure. I remember once as I was entering into an ayahuasca ego death, I said, “Actually, I really just want a girlfriend.” Right then, the crazy trip abruptly ended, and a voice said, “Ok then go find one.” Nobody believes this story when I tell it.

I don’t believe most people can be truly happy celibate. People generally aren’t so serious about spiritual liberation that giving up sex and partnership for life makes sense. The Buddha didn’t advise everyone to become monastics.

Some people become monks for practical reasons too. Of course, Mr. Nobody may sincerely want liberation, too. People can want several things at once.

### Practical partnerships

I’ve traveled the world as a (white) guy from a first-world country. Many women have wanted to marry me partly because they figured I could improve their status and comfort. That always makes me feel sad and lonely to think about, but yeah, I know it’s to be expected. The funniest time was when a female police officer in India said we should marry because we were both the same age. 😜

It’s reasonable for a woman to want support, since after all, she has to provide for her potential children, but it muddies the waters for me. I’m not stupid; I understand that she might really love me while still wanting money, stability, children, a visa, a home, etc. Men aren’t a different species, although sometimes I think I might be. A man can really love a woman for who she is while also seeing her as a source of sex, children, social legitimacy, or help running the house.

And hey, sometimes being objectified is kinda sexy, too. Men like to be objectified to a certain degree as providers and defenders, just as women often like being seen as sex dolls by the men they want. Being wanted that way feels good, but feeling held is important as well.

#### And deeper is the point

Beyond all that practical stuff, sex is extremely important for building and renewing the connection. You don’t stop loving the person because she got old, he lost his job, one of you got sick, or sex became impossible.

Sometimes they may even want you to find sexual satisfaction elsewhere if they can’t give it, because they love you and want you to be happy. Maybe they don’t. Nobody is owed that as proof of love.

## How to find a partner

(Insert OKCupid sponsorship here)

Looking for a partner is arguably backward, and can work against you. You can’t help looking if you want one, but once you start seeing every new person as a potential partner, you perform for them and they perform for you. Try to see people for who they are before you think of them that way. Actually it was Bee who kept drilling this into me: “You see a pretty girl, but you don’t see the ugly stuff she’s dealing with inside.”

On the other hand, if you aren’t looking for one, then you also aren’t filtering & comparing potential matches, which makes you more vulnerable to someone who targets you with their love bombs, not to mention the potentially good matches may slip thru the cracks of indifference.

Maybe a good compromise is to look for people that match you without directly looking for a romantic match. Join a local group that fits your interests. Go forage for wild plants, meditate, hike, study philosophy, whatever. At least you have fun, learn something, and maybe make some friends even if nobody attractive shows up.

### Long distance

I don’t think anyone wants to have a long-distance relationship, unless they’re not ready for a real in-person one, or it just so happens that their dream partner is currently far away. There is something super romantic about preferring one person no matter the distance. But it’s also not something we evolved to feel fulfilled by. And the less you’re physically together, the less you actually know each other. And actually, I would find it more romantic for my partner to say, “Let’s be together at any cost, physically” rather than “You should go take that better job and I’ll stay here.”

If you’re just starting one of these, I’d suggest you ask your partner about how long they are comfortable maintaining distance, to see if it really matches what you want. If you’re already in a relationship of any type, it’s worth discussing how much distance you’d tolerate and for how long.

### Dating App Horror Show

I would never recommend dating apps based on my own experience, although I suppose it depends on the person. Maybe you don’t care much about spirituality or anything, you’re not unique and don’t care if they are, you just “like big butts and you cannot lie,” and they want the same sort of thing. Then maybe the app fits what you’re looking for. 😅

Case in point, OKCupid didn’t tell me my first wife was going to try to kill me and my family, take my son, and then put me on child support that I can’t pay and wouldn’t pay on principle if I could. That was the worst choice I ever made, even though I still love my son and I’m happy he was born.

On the other hand, the app did give me information that I ignored because she was cute and interested in me. Her OKCupid profile almost made her sound like a prostitute, or at least someone who liked casual sex. Both turned out to be true. But once she started chatting with me and I saw the nice photos, I thought maybe she had been joking in the profile or something.

These days almost nobody even has a profile. They just have photos, which is showing you something too.

Ok wait, Judge not, lest ye be judged... An app could be fine as an introduction if you use it as a proper gate. Assume 99.99% of the people there are a bad match for you. If you’re just going to get excited by every cute guy or girl who hits you up, forget it. That’s such an easy way to find a weirdo, a psycho, a mooch, a faker, or whatever. Those people are easy enough to find in modern society in person anyway.

I always tell young guys especially: don’t worry about not finding a girl. Worry about finding the wrong girl. Girls are usually more selective, but they still need to think twice, because they often go for guys who aren’t respectful or mature.

I’m almost embarrassed to admit, but I recently tried Hinge again for a few days while I was in Kenya after my last breakup. I was getting something like a hundred girls interested in my profile every day, so I looked through a lot of profiles. Some were cute but none seemed deep. So the app did tell me something useful.

### Community makes the match

Sometimes when you don’t go looking, others go looking for you! Some time in my teen years, my dad decided I needed a girlfriend. This was long before dating apps, so he suggested we put up posters around the neighborhood advertising me as eligible for girls to date. I was horrified and we dropped the idea. That gives you some idea of the mildly autistic background I’m coming from: we didn’t understand people. That’s why it might seem absurd the level to which I’m trying here to help you understand what perhaps is already obvious. 😅

### The Missing Middle

Modern life barely has an intermediate stage between being interested in somebody and getting sexually entangled. One old example is [bundling](https://doi.org/10.1093/maghis/18.4.9), a mostly extinct practice now especially associated with the Amish, even though it existed much more widely. Courting couples could spend the night together in bed, usually clothed, with the idea that they could talk and be close without having sex, which sounds a lot like the missing middle I’m talking about.

Well, it didn’t exactly solve the problem: in late-1700s New England, where lots of couples bundled, 30–40 percent of brides were already pregnant on their wedding day. I’m not trying to bring bundling back. I just think we need some stage that everybody recognizes, where two people can explore tenderness and attraction without immediately sliding toward sex, moving in together, and a life bond.

### Peeking thru the window

Before the wisdom window closes, the main thing is to find out whether you are actually compatible. Do you have the same goals and values, especially around children, religion, politics, health, love, and romance? Where do you want to live? Do you both want to find a community? What kind of relationship do you want with technology and money?

So we have to find a way to ask deep questions. [Conversation-card games](https://innerself.love/blog/romance-card-game/) may seem silly, but they can help people get past superficial talk and see if they’re compatible.

But asking, “How do you handle jealousy?” can get me a beautiful answer that tells me almost nothing. I’d rather ask what she thinks she would do in an actual situation. If all she can give me is a general answer, I assume she probably doesn’t know yet.

But watch out, because ironically, a card game to get closer and deeper can backfire! If we spend two hours telling each other things nobody else knows, we may feel incredibly understood and close, but that may mean the game worked without telling us whether the relationship will.

You can also bring the person to a party, around your friends and family and see how they act, or take them to activities you enjoy and see if they really enjoy them too (although if they seem to enjoy every single one of them, they might be pretending). Try doing what you would normally do in your day alone, but with them, and see if it feels weird. Are they wondering why you spend hours playing with your cat? Do they look bored, like they’d rather just hit you up later for some fun?

See their negative side first too. If there is no negative side, that’s a red flag for fakery. Ask whether the spiritual story they tell about themselves survives an inconvenient PMS afternoon. How recent is the healing story—two months or two years? Jealousy, abandonment, possessiveness, and the “I’m so much better than I used to be” story may not be understood until there is stress and entanglement. By then, of course, you’re stressed and entangled. 😆

Sometimes the only thing I have is a little “wait” feeling. If I'm about to ignore a question because I really want this to work, that "wait" is enough reason for me to ask the question before I keep going.

If they’re nasty to their friends, family, or other people, assume they will eventually be nasty to you. We mostly seem to get attracted to the inner-child part because we’re coming from our inner child and we want to have fun. But you also need to find out whether there is a healthy adult there before you’re too bonded to want the answer.

### The conversation about flaws

Card games and social gatherings don’t tell you everything, but asking someone out of the blue, “What did you do wrong in your past relationships?” would make for a pretty weird date.

At the right time, though, in the right way (especially if they bring it up tangentially), I would ask about their past relationships, especially the recent ones. If every story ends with everything being the other person’s fault, I’d ask, “Okay, so what lessons did you find for yourself?”

“I have baggage” isn't as useful as learning what you're having trouble with now. What might make it hard for you to function well in a relationship?

I’d want both of us to be honest about how effective we actually are at handling our own issues. What addictions or impulsive behaviors can’t we reliably control? What are we probably going to ask forgiveness for more than once? That tells me more than either of us proclaiming that we’re healed.

Look at the most recent conflicts and ask if you were able to regulate yourself during them. What did you do to the other person, and how did you repair the damage afterward? Not doing can be just as bad as overdoing. If conflict makes you shut down, does it mean you stop talking to your partner for days, leaving them feeling abandoned?

Has your judgment gotten better, or would your friends or exes say you keep falling for the same stuff?

## Beware idealization in either direction

Idealization can be pathological, but it can also come very close to real deep love and appreciation. That’s why it’s so easy to get confused.

### Anti-PTSD Can Be Just As Dangerous As PTSD

When I’m in love, I see everything just from the love angle. I should probably admit that before I give anybody more advice.

# The Relationship As Crucible

It was during my first marriage that I read about Dr. David Schnarch’s Crucible Approach in [Passionate Marriage](https://www.crucible4points.com/books/passionate-marriage/). Schnarch saw marriage as a natural “people-growing process.” His idea of differentiation was learning to hold onto yourself while staying close, especially when intimacy brings anxiety and unresolved problems to the surface.

That’s definitely what relationship has done to me.

One warning before I romanticize the crucible too much: sometimes (as in my first marriage) this isn’t two wounded people triggering each other but one person terrorizing or controlling the other. If you’re scared to say no or tell the truth, or scared of what happens if you leave, don’t treat that as a mutual communication exercise—get other people involved and think about safety first.

Toft sees the crucible of his marriage as his main spiritual practice. He also continues his meditation, yoga, prayer, and church activities, but sees them as secondary. I understand what he means. It’s much easier for me to feel spiritually advanced when nobody is close enough to expose the parts of me that aren’t.

Being in a relationship showed me how vulnerable I actually am when I open my heart. It’s really easy to feel invulnerable and strong when you’re alone, or even just with friends. Taking off the masks and showing your inner child is a gamble, but it’s required if you want to go deeper in yourself and deeper with another person.

### From flow to nitty gritty

Living alone is easy in so many ways. I can make my bed whenever I want. When I have a girlfriend, making the bed in the morning can become one of the ways I show her I love her. If I don’t do it well, somehow it may become a bad omen that I don’t care about her, which is a lot of emotional weight for a bed.

Dealing with somebody else’s emotional insecurities can also be exhausting. It’s easy to feel free when I don’t have to do that, especially since I’m a bit of an introvert and 10% autistic. All that works wonderfully until I’m in a romantic relationship and suddenly I’m expected to mind-read, silences included, and check in constantly, without manipulating based on all that expert “management.”

### Reverting to babyhood

Relationship may activate wounds that were already there. Sometimes it may open a level of the heart that is simply very young and vulnerable, whether you knew there was a wound there or not. You become like a baby again. There’s a reason lovers call each other “baby.” And after all, Christ said "unless you change and become like little children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 18:3). ### Don’t make your partner your whole world

When I’m in love, my partner is my everything. Everything else drops away. I still try to make some time for friends, but really all I want to do is be with her.

That little paradise is wonderful, but it contains the seed of its own destruction. Part of why it feels like a magical wonderland is that you can suddenly stop parenting yourself now that you have someone who’s fully got your back. This makes you soft and weak, kinda like a sweet treat or heroin. It is wonderful to have their love and support, but don’t let that bring you away from your own self-love, reparenting, meditation, yoga, and other healing practices that keep you independently healthy. Otherwise, you’ll end up blaming them when you feel bad.

So don’t let your world get so small. Don’t abandon your friends unless they really aren’t good for the relationship. And if they aren’t, ask why. Are they bad friends? Are they slyly interested in one of you romantically? Or is the relationship so weak that it can’t handle anybody with an outside view?

You don’t need to spend as much time with friends as you did before the romance. But keep some room to explore life on your own.

For me, that room is where I keep a mission of my own, separate interests and some connection to community. I also get outside therapy when I need it, and I make sure I can still be alone.

Toft also points out that a man can get feminine energy from nature, music, poetry, art, flowers, food, and other parts of life. I agree, it's not fair to myself or to my girlfriend/wife if she has to be my only source of beauty, softness, sensuality, and inspiration.

When I’m already overwhelmed and suddenly announce, “I need time for myself!” it can easily sound like I need time away from her. It's always better to have some balance from the get-go rather than as a reaction after you see something is unsustainable, so make alone time normal from early on.

And bring each other out into the world. Meet other folks together and explore new places, don’t just disappear into each other.

#### Disconnect to connect

Self-reflection is a good place to start, but it can’t see what it can’t see. Your partner is also usually trying to keep you, so they have an ulterior motive, as sweet and expected as it may be.

That’s why I recommend [peer counseling](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/hearthwork-an-integrated-peer-counseling) outside the couple. If my partner is the only person I bring all my pain to, she also ends up carrying whatever I do when I still don’t understand it.

I also have to deal with what happens in my body: my mind may understand that my partner is going through something difficult and that I don’t have to be dragged down with her, but my body still feels dragged down, fragile, and sensitive. My breathing gets shallow, and it becomes extremely hard to feel okay while she is in pain.

Crying helps a lot, but it may actually be harder for men once they are in a relationship because they are expected to be the shoulder everybody else cries on. That is another reason to have a peer counselor.

Crying also contains a type of automatic erratic breathwork. And [breathwork](https://breathwork.u-dont-exist.com/) is one of the easiest ways I know to calm down. Reduced breathing or just continuous/circular [Systema breathing](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/Nar2-nvomAk) is especially useful to me when my partner is getting me upset and flustered with drama. Sometimes she’s deliberately trying to do that, and other times it’s hard to tell, although if I'm stuck thinking about what's on purpose I'm already lost. I explain more of the body-based therapy options in my [Somatic Modalities guide](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/somatic-modalities-strategic-sequencing).

Meditation is also helpful, but for most people, I would still put inner-child reparenting, peer counseling, and body-based work first or make them primary meditation targets. Many people say they cannot meditate or have not gotten to that stage yet, and meditation without grounding in those approaches can easily be a road into [spiritual bypassing](http://goenka.u-dont-exist.com/).

### Babies growing up again

Nobody has to be completely healed before entering a relationship. And I think that as we learn more about ourselves through the crucible, we should naturally raise our standards. Maybe at first we need the drama to access our primal emotions, but after years of that, we’re exhausted by what we previously hoped to find.

At the very least, there needs to be honesty, real effort at improving, and some ability to stop blaming everybody else for your own problems.

I already said before dating that the present-day adult has to be able to parent whatever intimacy wakes up inside you. Once you’re together, more of that stuff comes out. [Inner-child reparenting](https://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/) means parenting the child part instead of surreptitiously hiring the partner for the job.

It can get pretty entitled when you expect your partner to know what you need without being told. You may barely see your own pattern, and then deny it instead of taking responsibility when they point it out.

### Some babies grow up faster

While different maturity levels are to be expected to some degree, it can get out of hand at the extremes. What I noticed in all of my most intimate relationships was that when one person was always the child and the other is always the therapist, guru, doctor, or dad, then it’s too unequal, and eventually there will be some kind of pendulum swing or backlash. When I became consistently the responsible one, that also made me responsible for everything that went wrong, not to mention the unhealthy clinginess, jealousy, and all the rest which went along with that dynamic.

Then the questions change for me: if the romance changes, which parts are still supposed to remain? Are we promising actions (or low-key pretending to promise permanent feelings)? Which needs are we still going to meet outside the relationship?

And the biggie: what happens if one of us falls in love with someone else? Is that, in and of itself, a betrayal, since it's not under our control? If our lives change, can we compassionately sit down and rethink the agreement? And if we split up, what do we still owe each other (and our kids, if we have any)?

These are just a few questions I could think of to start the ball rolling, but be creative and see what you come up with! You can make a [card game](https://innerself.love/blog/romance-card-game/) out of this and have some fun with it. If there’s real love, it will survive a bit of honest curiosity. And again, community can make all of this a lot less scary.

## Which marriage vows are honest?

Conventional marriage vows are dishonest and inept, and most people know it. You can’t promise how you’ll feel in the future, but you can promise actions if you really believe you’re capable of delivering them. So for example, you could promise to tell the truth, to keep showing up emotionally, to support the children, or whatever else you genuinely believe you can do even if your feelings change. More importantly, though, if you do keep those promises, it's quite likely that your connection will deepen, so it's not like we truly have no control of future feelings, but just not completely, directly or unilaterally.

We also find something like my warning in the New & Old Testaments. Ecclesiastes 5:4–6 says it is better not to make a vow than to make one and fail to keep it. If you tell the priest you didn’t mean what you promised, the passage warns that God will be angry and destroy your work.

Matthew 5:33–37 and James 5:12 make the wedding vow itself look even more suspicious (to me, anyway): let your “Yes” be “Yes” and your “No” be “No,” and anything beyond that comes from evil.

Where did the idea of promising to have romantic interest in your partner come from, anyway? It turns out it never existed in any culture I’m aware of until the 19th century, when people in England began citing romance as their reason for marriage. That trend has continued evolving like a snowball until today. But what our ancestors knew was not wrong: You can sign a contract about what you’ll do, but feelings aren’t contractual.

### Attraction and exclusivity

It’s hard to find sexually monogamous animals; have you ever looked? And as it turns out, we humans aren’t a natural exception to the rule, either. Plenty of tribal cultures were (and some still are) much looser about this, while still generally retaining the primary-partner “social monogamy.” Sexclusivity started gaining sway around the time we started planting crops and owning land. That’s when it made sense to keep track of how to keep our land in the family bloodline.

Ironically, sexclusivity, which we treat as the basis of romance, started out as that kind of bureaucratic record-keeping. By the 17th century, when England began mass urbanization by forcing peasants off their land, it became the legal definition of marriage. As for what comes naturally to us underneath that law, I’m not trying to say we’re just like bonobos, but the academic consensus is that humans tend toward flexible pair-bonding, with a propensity for occasional infidelity.

### Jealousy on trial

These days, even the brief inkling of attraction to another person becomes almost a sure sign that sexual infidelity is next. That’s why I felt I had no choice but to address the issue head on when B. wanted to marry me. I told her, “I can’t fully commit to you if I’m still attracted to other women, so before I do, let’s see if I can fix that.”

I meditated for a while and surprised my own self to find something like a switch in my mind. It felt like evolutionary programming around whether I had found my life mate and true love. I asked myself, “Is she the one?”

The inner Magic 8 Ball came back YES, and I flipped off the mate-finding switch.

It worked about 99% of the time. I was no longer interested in other women, aside from one extremely beautiful girl who grabbed my attention once.

That amazed me, although B. never quite believed the switch was real. She even started hallucinating that I was looking at or talking to other women when I wasn’t. So her jealousy wasn't about what I was doing then and there. More likely it was insecure attachment from childhood trying to justify its continued existence.

Jealousy is always insecurity, but sometimes that insecurity can be an important indicator that the relationship actually is not secure, rather than just a remembered script from childhood. Although, either way, it can become a self-fulfilling prophecy.

Instead of stewing in jealousy and throwing out microaggressions now and then, speak your heart without hiding the insecurity behind anger, as in the above [video I found on IG](https://www.instagram.com/reel/Dc9hwfziBn8/?stkn=MzRlODBiNWFlZA==). In a monogamous relationship, you might say:

“I notice you pay attention to this other girl more than me, and it makes me feel unloved and insecure. Can we talk about it? Do you really want to explore things with her? If so that’s fine, but don’t make the decision lightly, because it’s irreversible.”

Maybe he really is neglecting you or wanting to be with someone else. Maybe the “attention” was one glance and your baseline insecurity inflated it into a threat that it wasn’t. That part should not be projected onto your partner. Every couple has to work out for themselves what actually feels healthy.

I do believe two people can choose sexual exclusivity honestly. They can admit that attraction to other people exists and decide not to act on it each day. And even better than simply suppressing it, they can put it to work. I agree with Esther Perel, who writes that this sexual energy felt outside the relationship can be used and redirected into the relationship to make it more alive.

Imagine if you and your partner both saw those outside attractions as beneficial energy sources rather than a threat to your relationship. It sure would beat beating around the bush because of fear and jealousy. You can start playing around with opening up about the minor, momentary attractions, rather than wait in hiding until a serious crush is dramatically confessed.

Lots of couples promise forever to each other and nobody else. Look around at how many actually live that way. On the other hand, in a polyamorous relationship, the boundaries might be more negotiable, but then somebody has to sit down and negotiate them, down to who gets to stay the night. That can end up sometimes creating more drama than it's worth.

Polyamory can also be a way of escaping deeper intimacy, like the college friend with four girlfriends I mentioned earlier. Or it can be a way of escaping guilt, like when someone keeps cheating until they finally "come out of the closet" as poly, as if that solves their prior dishonesty. But they are right to finally come clean and hopefully avoid that dishonesty in future.

# Doing it consciously 🧭

I learned most of this by screwing it up. Some of it I just thought through, and I'm still not sure about all of it.

## Imagination as a discernment tool

We all fantasize about a new exciting person we just met, right? The useful thing about the fantasy is that it shows me what I want. But I usually make the mistake of playing out just the first part of the fantasy. So I might start with the regular lovey sexy stuff, and that’s fine because that’s what’s drawing me in. Then after that, when I have a bit of wisdom available, I continue onto the mundane and difficult scenes. I’m not trying to force anything or oppose anything. I just try to open my eyes when they want to close, ignore, look away. Here’s my checklist:

- What’s it feel like after we have sex? Do we feel comfortable and happy about that, or do we feel guilty that we gave in to temptation too quickly and now there’s some weird feelings? Name the feelings and explore.

- How about a week into the relationship? What’s starting to get annoying perhaps? What are we trying not to say and not to notice?

- Get in the ring before the ring gets on you. Let’s imagine we have a real fight about money, jealousy, or something else important. What do we do to handle the conflict?

- Fast forward. Now we’ve got kids. What might be the values conflicts in how we raise them? Schools, screen time, vaxxes, religion, helicoptering vs freedom, food, gender & sexual orientation issues.

- Rewind. What if we disagree on having kids?

- Fast forward five years. What has each of us gained or given up, and do we have any resentment about it?

This isn’t to make predictions and we’re not consulting the magic 8 ball nor doing Istikhara rituals here. I mean you can do that too if you want. Distinguish between fears and what really seems likely just as you do between wishes and reality.

If every scene is beautiful, consider that you may be, and almost certainly are, still rehearsing the relationship you want. There’s nothing wrong with expecting the best, but it takes work to get there in a wise way.

Psychedelics can make this same problem of idealization much stronger.

## Psychedelic Timing

This section will be relevant to some readers and easy for others to skip.

I’m putting the warning before anything good I say about psychedelics for relationships, because people need to hear it before they take anything. I don’t think sexual consent should be negotiated for the first time while high, when the drug is changing how safe, loving, open, and connected everything feels. And even if the two of you plan it all in advance, getting high and starting a relationship is still a risk.

Starting a relationship through psychedelics has another danger. Key and I met and then spent a few days taking iboga with friends. We got to know each other very deeply that way, and that was great. But it was especially her higher self I was getting to know. I tend to idealize people even sober, and that gets worse when I’m high. It was still beautiful to see the real higher self potential in her, I’m not denying that. But that information didn’t help me understand how we would get along in normal life.

I’ve also had friends get together at MDMA parties and stuff. It’s rarely a good idea. The intimacy can be completely real without telling you whether the two of you actually work together sober. Or you might even sometimes have the opposite problem, like I had with H., where the connection continues to deepen while sober, and it becomes something that one person might cling to desperately, never imagining they could find that again.

The obvious advice is to wait before making large promises and spend time together sober, around other people, and in ordinary situations. Write down what each of you learned, do some peer counseling, check with friends to see if you’re too pie-in-the-sky. But the problem is, you won’t necessarily be interested in sober advice when you’re high. You’re “beyond all that.” And the high can continue after sobriety because intimacy itself is like rose-colored glasses.

### Returning to the heart to get unstuck

Psychedelics, especially certain types of shrooms, very pure LSD, very pure therapeutic-grade [MDMA](http://mdmaket.u-dont-exist.com/), ayahuasca, [loveyhuasca haoma](http://soma.u-dont-exist.com/), and [iboga](http://iboga.u-dont-exist.com/), can be extremely useful when a couple is trapped in defensive stories.

With Bee and Key, there were many times when we could argue forever sober, then take something and finally understand each other. It could feel like we were telepathically synced. Instead of each person defending their version, we directly understood what the other was perceiving, feeling, misunderstanding.

But the problem is most people are lazy about integrating what they learn during this window of clarity. We enjoyed the understanding while it lasted, and then the same defensive story would resurface later. In neuroscience, this is called state-dependent learning. What you learn in altered states doesn’t automatically translate into your normal sober mind state without the work to do that.

### Measuring the benefits

Probably one of the most important benefits we can hope to get long term from any therapy is the ability to express what we feel in a non-combative way. Otherwise, people tend to ping pong between not expressing or downplaying their feelings and expressing them combatively. MDMA can make this temporarily much easier because all the problems can look so small and silly. The question is what happens when you’re sober again.

So I would say the therapy worked if a week or two later, you and your partner can comfortably say, “Honey, I’m feeling unappreciated right now. I know you’re not trying to make me feel that way, and maybe you feel like I’m making you feel that way, but I’d like us to take some time soon to discuss the issue beneath this in a kinder way. What do you say?”

If you don’t have a partner, you can do the same thing with whoever you tend to have trouble relating with, in a less lovey way but still with compassion.

It’s not always possible in that moment to change a defensive posture into non-defensive loving communication. That’s why I suggest setting a time soon with a nice intention, like a ritual. Sober is fine. You can put some rose petals around you or something to build the feeling, or play some song you both like.

## Why all of this sounds artificial

By now this probably sounds terribly artificial. Is anyone actually going to do scheduled check-ins, delayed cohabitation, conversations about hypothetical disasters, imagination exercises, not to mention resisting the heat-of-the-moment stuff and continuing your own reparenting practices when a warm body is available to do some of the soothing?

Well, yeah. It is painfully artificial. In a healthy communal society, the couple would have access to witnesses, elders, friends, and other emotional outlets and resources. Most of us these days don’t have that, so we’re trying to recreate consciously and clumsily some of what the wider field would have done without a checklist.

# If you’re already in it

Most of this article is about what to do before jumping head first into head over shoulders. But if you’re already married with children, advice about slowing down arrives a little late.

I wish I had a special method for fixing that. Mostly I learned the obvious thing the hard way: one person can’t fix the relationship (unless they’re the main problem in it).

With Bee and Key, the biggest thing sitting underneath everything was trust. They wanted to be with me and would say they trusted me, but really they didn’t trust me, themself, or anyone else. It was all or nothing: blind trust, or none. I kept saying, “It’s not possible to have a relationship without trust.” I think that rigidity came from trauma and never really growing up.

Even dishonesty was sometimes hard for me to define in these relationships. Bee once opened a door and, a second later, said she hadn’t opened it. That drove me crazy! I mean, why would she lie like that? On the other hand, although she was technically lying, there was no strategy behind it, and she was usually quite honest. Non-strategic lying is literally defined as "pathological lying," so I should have seen it more like an illness than a failure of virtue.

Some things were less mysterious: they were acting as if the trust problem wasn’t there because they still wanted the relationship. Attractions to other people sometimes went unsaid too, and sometimes got imagined from nowhere. Some people keep a relationship like this going for a long time, just sweeping everything under the rug.

<!-- IMAGE https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!UllG!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F791c76a8-a333-4b27-839e-b1a4b019124d_1079x1356.png : a picture in the post, lying to avoid conflict -->

On the other hand, sometimes I was more honest than my partner could handle, which was its own kind of damage. Ask whether both of you actually want to prioritize radical honesty, first. But the other person also can’t keep saying “not now” forever and think that’s going to save the relationship.

This is the time for agape to shine, because the best thing for both of you may not be holding onto the relationship pattern you originally committed to or dreamt of.

When did you two last dance? And not the “we dance around our problems” joke (LOL).. If that’s where things are at, the trust is out the window. Ouch. I know that one from experience with my first 2 wives and K, too. That generally marks the point of no return, so try not to wait for that. Because once you’re there, as my dad explained a thousand times whenever we had a visitor, unconscious resentment begins to snowball, and we feel colder together than alone. Pretty soon, old Romeo & Juliette might get wandering eye syndrome.

## Outside help can sometimes break the loop fast

Bee and I were once desperately misunderstanding each other, so we called my friend Eshwar, the only friend of mine whom she respected. He told a related story about something that had happened with his sister. Somehow, Bee immediately understood what I had been trying to say. It was like magic.

So, don’t think of outside help as a formal last resort. I think of it as a part of building community when we learn to rely on each other for this kind of intimate, real-life support. It could be calling up a mutual friend, peer counselor, couples therapist, doing Imago dialogue, Restorative Circle, and/or joining a men’s or women’s group. The key is that both people need to respect the person or process enough to hear something new.

What they say is also partly true: “The only one you can change is yourself.” Not fully true, because we do have influence, and changing ourself does ripple out. Restart whatever you stopped doing because the relationship was soothing you: inner-child reparenting, meditation, body practices, peer counseling.

## What will honesty produce?

Many people ask this question of honesty, but it’s unfair. Honesty doesn’t produce a certain outcome. It is its own reward. It might repair the relationship, open it, turn the romance into friendship, preserve the family through separation, or show you that ending it is better. Of course, if the other person won’t look at the truth, you can’t make honesty a mutual value. Then you have to decide what you can live with. Temporary separation helped Bee and I, although it became less temporary as time went on, but I’ve always thought maybe we would get back together in a few decades.

# Children

I think everyone generally agrees that parents have more responsibility to children than they do to each other, since children can’t take care of themselves.

Before having or adopting children, sit down for an imagination session to figure out whether you two lovebirds can preserve some civil co-parenting capacity after a terrible breakup. That’s the actual long-term commitment.

When it comes to stepchildren, even if they aren’t technically your children, they still become attached. If you’ve been acting as their parent for years, it’s not right to vanish from their life just because the marriage ended. You don’t have legal parental rights, but you can stay a mentor or close friend, and hopefully your partner would agree with this. When children have parental figures come and go, it can damage their ability to have securely attached relationships as adults.

It’s standard advice to never recruit children into the adult war. They get their own relationship with the other parent. However, this was particularly difficult during my first marriage, because Ann was very much unfit at the time to care for our son, Bear. I did fight for custody, but, as my lawyer told me, there was no point, because men generally lose. Eventually I exhausted my options for fighting, and I was left with weekly supervised visits.

Ann was brainwashing Bear into believing that I was “breaking his bones” at these visits even though he didn’t know what bones were. That’s when I told him I had to stop the visits before I end up in jail. So it was for my protection, but his as well, because if he realizes one day his dad is in jail because his mom manipulated him into telling a dangerous story, that could really wreck him.

As it is now, he may miss me, but he is well cared for (I think) by his grandmother, and probably soon he’ll seek me out when he gets his own phone.

I know I sound like a broken record, like my dad, but still: It takes a village. The nuclear family, built on parents’ romance, can often be the worst arrangement for children. If there’s trouble in paradise, parents will try to protect the kids by hiding their tension. But the truth is, children absorb that unresolved tension and integrate it into their very being. In a community, romance is one bond among several, and adult stability for children doesn’t rise and fall with it.

# Ending consciously

<!-- EMBED https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SitT9ojqV1U : a YouTube video -->

Before you think about how to leave, settle whether you’re leaving, and whether counseling is worth trying first.

You don’t owe it to them to try counseling unless you also owe it to yourself. If you actually don’t want a good relationship with them anymore, go. No amount of counseling will fix something that has nothing at the base of it.

If you love them and wish it could work, ask if they feel the same, and then go to counseling. On the other hand, even when you love each other, sometimes you already know the issue is beyond what counseling could fix.

### Gaslighting in the dark

Do you have irreconcilable memories of key events? I did with both my first and second wives. I took my first wife, Ann, to two counselors. The male counselor eventually told us, “I’ve never told anyone this, but you guys need to split up for at least six months.”

There’s no way to resolve a situation where one person remembers that you ruined her life on purpose, while you remember that she was having a manic episode and had to be committed for her own safety. We also didn’t have mutual friends who could corroborate what happened—only friends on each side who were partial.

Many say it’s best to save face and work things out privately, but I’d disagree. If you don’t have friends who can help with the conflict, then you’re really stuck (and you might wanna find some new friends). But do it fairly, with the goal of helping the conflict, not just getting approval for your own interpretation. Both people should get a chance to speak, and good friends should hear both sides before validating either.

I can believe a friend’s experience and still tell them that my ability to know what is really going on is extremely limited. If someone says, “I don’t have all the evidence,” I trust their thinking more. Living in community would help because your friends would not only hear two stories afterward. Sometimes they would actually see how the two of you treat each other when you argue.

If you’ve been there for someone, don’t abandon them in a crisis unless it’s unsafe for you to stay or they’re creating the crisis to keep you, or they aren’t willing to help themself. You may need to arrange other care, but that’s no reason to pretend the romance still has a future.

## After leaving

Often times the breakup is hard, but the aftermath is worse. Public demonization should be avoided, but I don’t think that means hiding the truth about the relationship to save face. Sometimes, the truth is that you were abused, coerced, or seriously deceived. So how much you say, and to whom, really depends on what happened and why you’re telling people.

Once you’re out, you can see things you genuinely couldn’t see while you were bonded. Look at what you contributed to how things turned out. And don’t paint your ex as a cartoon villain: try to understand whatever was true in their perspective too, including the conflicts they were carrying inside themselves.

Sometimes the easiest story is that they were fake all along, because it explains everything. Grab it only if it really is the simplest explanation of what happened. People who aren’t heavily invested in seeing your innocence can help you tell, and a therapist, pastor, or even a stranger may see the evidence more clearly than a lifelong friend. Even the curiosity itself can be therapeutic for you.

Spiritual practice during an ending is critical, but don’t use it to get around the feelings. Pray for your ex’s wellbeing. Remember what was real and good, and don’t use your pain as permission to hate them. Avoid the New Age belief that everyone is simply a mirror of you. Of course, we do partly mirror one another, but each person was formed by plenty that had nothing to do with you. No matter how enlightened the Buddha was, his cousin Devadatta still kept trying to kill him: looking honestly at your part doesn’t erase theirs. Sometimes it helps to ask, “What would Mr. Rogers do?” (if you’re old enough to remember that show).

I also learned that public demonization doesn’t always have to be the last chapter, even if things ended on that bitter note. If you avoid contact forever, they might never apologize and learn honesty. But it can help to keep distance for a while until cooler heads prevail. On the other hand, it can be tempting to let them back into your life after a private apology, and especially after a public one, but keep in mind you deserve a stable love. You’re not a yo-yo.

## What I gained from loss

At this point, I can say I’m better off because of what happened with Bee, Key, and H., without pretending every part of those relationships was beneficial or destined. Loss taught me things love alone never would, or to paraphrase Rumi, “You must keep breaking your heart until it stays open.” (The [real quote](https://school.danaa.app/rumi-wound-with-its-meaning/): “The reed must be torn from its bed, cut, hollowed out, and pierced with holes (wounded) before it can channel the breath of the musician into music.”)

From Bee, I learned what it means for me to be really in love. I learned the kind of woman I actually want: deeply spiritual, sensitive, creative, unique, somebody who inspires me and makes me question everything.

Key repeated a lot of that, which is why I fell in love with her. I learned some of the same harsh lessons from both of them: don’t take openness and curiosity to be fully good. They can also indicate that someone hasn’t developed their own sense of self or opinions, or enough inner adult.

From H., I learned that I can be extremely close to someone and feel completely understood and supported without actually falling in love. I still treasure the support we gave/give each other.

Too many people judge a relationship only by whether they stayed together. Relationships are a process of growth in many dimensions, so start measuring what you actually learned from yours.

# Tough Love

Romantic love is not easy, and the things that actually make it work are not very marketable. I can’t sell you the fix with one click or one monthly subscription to my love class. (Just kidding, click here!)

A relationship like this is rare, and that’s probably why romantic love has been seen with suspicion as a destabilizing force throughout history. One of the best books that makes that point today might be the one I was reading with Key: The Spirit of Intimacy, by Sobonfu Somé. Somé came from the Dagara tribe in Burkina Faso. To her people, marriage is an arranged, ritualized spiritual and communal responsibility. They’re suspicious of romance. But one of her ideas fits what I’m trying to say: the relationship is bigger and more important than the egos in it. She calls it the spirit of the relationship.

That spirit is like the universe living its desire to know itself through us, which might be why we see ourselves in ways we can’t imagine prior. Nobody can command the spirit to remain. At our best, we serve it and listen for what Spirit wants to change. When we start thinking we own it or deserve it, that presumptuousness tends to push it away.

My parents agreed with Somé that community matters and that we serve Spirit. They were also not romantic, though they were loving and poly. I actually wished they were in love with each other, I guess, although I couldn’t have articulated that since I didn’t even have that concept at the time. Also reminiscent of Somé, they also wore rose-colored glasses and taught me that everybody was basically good. So I learned something about love and community, but not how hard romance is without a community around it, or how much damage basically good people can do when their fears, trauma, and undeveloped parts take over.

It took me many decades to figure out what probably everyone else already knows: real private pathology exists. But pathology festers when the environment is perfect for it. The dominant culture hermetically seals two people together and calls that a luxurious privacy. It tells them that sealed room should provide all the fresh air you’d get from a lover, family, community, therapist, and so on. Then it treats the predictable failure as privately caused as well, like mold that grows out of nowhere.

The myths around romantic love become real because we co-create them. We act as though jealousy proves love, permanent feelings can be promised, conflict should stay hidden, and the relationship failed if it ended. Then we suffer inside ideas that were never natural laws, and we walk blindly into our first, second, third, fourth, and fifth marriages!

It might be that I wrote this whole article for my son, Bear, who sadly, I last saw when he was about five, right before he was supposed to get the Rosenblum sex talk.

Bear, sex can be what you do when you’re older and find a friend you love in a super special way that makes you want to raise children together. Don’t imagine you love each other just because you want each other. Don’t feel pressure to promise what your heart will feel. Let the love and bond it produces blossom genuinely as your hearts sync. Learn about each other before getting too close, and make sure to keep friends and community around who know both of you.

I believe Rumi was right: A sacred relationship will open and purify your hearts regardless of whether it ends.
