At this point, I can say I’m better off because of what happened with Bee, Key, and H., without pretending every part of those relationships was beneficial or destined. Loss taught me things love alone never would, or to paraphrase Rumi, “You must keep breaking your heart until it stays open.” (The real quote: “The reed must be torn from its bed, cut, hollowed out, and pierced with holes (wounded) before it can channel the breath of the musician into music.”)

From Bee, I learned what it means for me to be really in love. I learned the kind of woman I actually want: deeply spiritual, sensitive, creative, unique, somebody who inspires me and makes me question everything.

Key repeated a lot of that, which is why I fell in love with her. I learned some of the same harsh lessons from both of them: don’t take openness and curiosity to be fully good. They can also indicate that someone hasn’t developed their own sense of self or opinions, or enough inner adult.

From H., I learned that I can be extremely close to someone and feel completely understood and supported without actually falling in love. I still treasure the support we gave/give each other.

Too many people judge a relationship only by the result, so start measuring what you actually learned from it instead. Relationships are a process of growth in many dimensions. The result is not only whether you stayed together.

Romantic love is not easy, and the things that actually make it work are not very marketable. I can’t sell you the fix with one click or one monthly subscription to my love class. (Just kidding, click here!)

A relationship like this is rare, and that’s probably why romantic love has been seen with suspicion as a destabilizing force throughout history. One of the best books that makes that point today might be the one I was reading with Key: The Spirit of Intimacy, by Sobonfu Somé. Somé came from the Dagara tribe in Burkina Faso. To her people, marriage is an arranged, ritualized spiritual and communal responsibility. They’re suspicious of romance. But one of her ideas fits what I’m trying to say: the relationship is bigger and more important than the egos in it. She calls it the spirit of the relationship.

That spirit is like the universe living its desire to know itself through us, which might be why we see ourselves in ways we can’t imagine prior. Nobody can command the spirit to remain. At our best, we serve it and listen for what Spirit wants to change. When we start thinking we own it or deserve it, that presumptuousness tends to push it away.

My parents agreed with Somé that community matters and that we serve Spirit. They were also not romantic, though they were loving and poly. I actually wished they were in love with each other, I guess, although I couldn’t have articulated that since I didn’t even have that concept at the time. Also reminiscent of Somé, they also wore rose-colored glasses and taught me that everybody was basically good. So I learned something about love and community, but not how hard romance is without a community around it, or how much damage basically good people can do when their fears, trauma, and undeveloped parts take over.

It took me many decades to figure out what probably everyone else already knows: real private pathology exists. But pathology festers when the environment is perfect for it. The dominant culture hermetically seals two people together and calls that a luxurious privacy. It tells them that sealed room should provide all the fresh air you’d get from a lover, family, community, therapist, and so on. Then it treats the predictable failure as privately caused as well, like mold that grows out of nowhere.

The myths around romantic love become real because we co-create them. We act as though jealousy proves love, permanent feelings can be promised, conflict should stay hidden, and the relationship failed if it ended. Then we suffer inside ideas that were never natural laws, and we walk blindly into our first, second, third, fourth, and fifth marriages!

It might be that I wrote this whole article for my son, Bear, who sadly, I last saw when he was about five, right before he was supposed to get the Rosenblum sex talk.

Bear, sex can be what you do when you’re older and find a friend you love in a super special way that makes you want to raise children together. Don’t imagine you love each other just because you want each other. Don’t feel pressure to promise what your heart will feel. Let the love and bond it produces blossom honestly as your hearts sync. Learn about each other before getting too close, and make sure to keep friends and community around who know both of you.

I believe Rumi was right: A sacred relationship will open and purify your hearts regardless of whether it ends.
