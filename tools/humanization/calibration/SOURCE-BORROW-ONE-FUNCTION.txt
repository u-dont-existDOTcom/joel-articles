Borrow One Function at a TimeA complete ideal parent may be impossible to imagine. Borrow one function at a time from a figure who embodies it: a therapist, grandmother, teacher, friend, older sibling, spiritual figure, fictional character, future self, or another presence whose care does not require you to disappear.

Borrowed Nurturer: “How would this person sit beside the child?” Begin with non-judgement if love feels inaccessible: no insults, no rushing, no impossible standards, no demand that the child perform gratitude for the session. Non-judgement is enough to begin establishing the adult position, but not necessarily enough to ask the child to trust, open, or receive care. The next borrowed nurturer step is to find a nurturer in your life that you can remember fondly. If there was none, you can look to examples like Mr. Rogers, or spiritual love you’ve felt, if you have.



Borrowed Protector: “What would a minimally competent adult do in the next ten minutes?” Eat, lock the door, stop replying, leave the unsafe room, ask for help, or go to sleep.



Borrowed Guide: “What did I decide when I was calm? What would the wise person I respect ask me to practice today?” Direction can come from a written plan, value, teacher, or tradition until you can hold it yourself.



Borrowing can also help the part trying to be the adult. When the child answers with sarcasm, distrust, or contempt, ask how a decent adult would hear it without defending their goodness, retaliating, collapsing, or leaving. That may be the only function you borrow for the moment.

You may still feel completely like the child while doing the action. That is fine. Adult identity can form after the behavior: “I did that for us. That was the Protector.”

Related research gives this idea some support without proving the whole method. A small pilot of the Ideal Parent Figure method used imagined ideal caregivers during the stabilization phase for adults with childhood-trauma-related complex PTSD. In one experiment, compassion imagery evoked threat responses in highly self-critical participants; another found that a secure-attachment prime reduced some of that response. So “this feels fake” may mean the person needs a bridge rather than a more forceful performance of love.
