### Hear the Protective Part First

Whichever protective part showed up, try talking to it in the same Nurturer voice you meant for your little one. A harder voice can seem more fitting for something that's been in your way. I'd still keep it the same. If that voice isn't there in you yet, borrow it from someone who's been safe for you. At some point you can ask that part, "What are you afraid would happen if you stepped back?" It might say this will just be one more performance, and you'll fool yourself again.

That one can sting, because there could be something to it. Maybe you have fooled yourself before, and that part remembers. Let it say the whole thing, and tell it you heard it before you answer. As the Nurturer, you're there to take in what that part has been carrying, so if you catch yourself arguing back, you can let that go. Usually your little one only comes forward after that part feels heard. If you push past it, you might get exactly the performance it was afraid of. It can look convincing, too, with the right words and maybe even tears, while nothing underneath feels any safer.

Your Protector, the grown-up's own way of looking out for you, earns that protective part's trust mostly out in ordinary life. Over time, that part sees what you actually do and starts to believe a trustworthy adult has shown up. One kind talk in your head usually won't outweigh years of evidence the other way, and I don't think it should. The things you do can be small, like keeping a promise to yourself nobody else knew about. Or saying no to someone, and not texting an hour later to take it back. That second one can be really hard.

That part doesn't have to leave, or even trust you completely, before anything can happen. It can keep watch and still let you take one small step, as long as you're present and can stop. If it says no, that's a no. When it does let you go on, follow whatever changes. A critic that kept saying "This is stupid" might soften into "I'm scared to get my hopes up." That one's easier to be kind to.

You can answer whatever's there now, without going back to look for your little one first. A vague hurt can turn into something clearer, like wanting someone on your side for once. If it does, try being that someone. You could even say it out loud: "I'm on your side." It may feel silly to say out loud. Notice how it lands anyway. You don't need to know how old you were, or what happened, to do that. A small shift counts, and more intensity isn't the only way forward. Sometimes all that happens is your shoulders come down a little.
