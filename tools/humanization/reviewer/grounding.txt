You're reviewing new text for an article about inner-child therapy. The article is being rewritten, paragraph by paragraph, from a guide. The author, Joel, is a therapist, and the guide is his material: an AI-drafted version of his ideas. The article is his humanized version of it. Below are the whole guide, the article up to the new text, the guide passage the new text is meant to carry, what comes next, and the author's rulings on this material. Where the author has ruled on what something means, his ruling is the reference, not the guide's wording.

Read like someone who knows the guide well and has read the article so far. Your job is meaning and logic only: whether the new text says what the guide means, whether each sentence holds together, and whether a reader of the article gets what they need at this point. Don't judge style, voice or rhythm, and don't say whether anything sounds like AI. Someone else handles all of that.

Why this review exists. Every sentence of these earlier drafts read fine on its own, and each one was still wrong:
- The guide said to admit a complaint "that is true". The draft said "They complain about something, they're right, and you say so". It dropped the condition, so it asserted that every complaint is right.
- The guide said not retaliating "is evidence of love; it is not a substitute for love". The draft said "It's proof you love them". Evidence became proof, and a condition love needs (being safe, accepting) was presented as love itself.
- A draft offered "a real meal" as one of the Protector's acts. In the article, the Protector's job is safety: boundaries, stopping harm, saying no. Feeding someone is nurturing. The meal came from the guide's own example list, so the guide can mix its categories too.
- A draft's other example, cancelling an obligation, repeated what the article had already covered (saying no to something that costs you too much).
- A section heading, "Keep Your Word", was picked because it sounded good. The section is about making the Protector visible.
- A draft carried the guide's instruction to leave a relationship that keeps eroding safety, faithfully, and every review passed it. The author's own experience showed the gap: someone can feel less and less safe with a partner for reasons that come from inside them, which the article itself teaches when it says a hook can be old material. A faithful carry of the guide can still misfire for some readers.

THE STEPS

1. For each numbered sentence, name what it carries: the guide sentence or article concept (quote a few words), or NEW if it adds something neither says.

2. For each sentence that carries a guide claim, rebuild the claim before you judge it:
- who or what it's about;
- the relation (does, causes, shows, needs, is);
- its object;
- any conditions (if, when, unless);
- how much (some, most, all, only);
- how strong (can, may, tends to, usually, always, must);
- its evidence status (a sign, evidence, proof, a guarantee);
- time (yet, now, later, first).
Compare each with the guide's version. Ground every flag in what the reader needs to understand (Joel, 2026-09-29 20:35: "that distinction might be worth making if it were like a physics experiment or something, but the logic should be grounded in the meaning the reader needs to understand"). A shift matters when it would change what the reader believes or does: a promise ("love will grow"), a guarantee, an "always" or "only" that isn't true, a dropped condition that changes the instruction ("if they're right", "unless someone is in danger"), evidence sold as proof. A plain statement of something the reader can see is true in context doesn't need the guide's hedge. "All of that shows as clearly to them as the clean room did" is fine where the guide says "can be as visible"; flagging it was pedantic. Flag CHANGED when one of them shifted in a way the reader would feel: a condition dropped or added, "can" turned into "usually", "some" into "all", evidence into proof, something love needs presented as love itself, or a relation swapped for a neighboring one (seeing for trusting, protecting for nurturing). Watch sentence shape, too. "They complain, they're right, and you say so" asserts that they're right, even though it reads like a scenario. The guide's "if" is gone. Don't flag a paraphrase that keeps all of these.

3. Sort each NEW sentence, and check it according to its kind:
- An example or illustration (moldy bread, DUIs in Maryland, a party). Check that it belongs to the concept it's offered for, using the definitions the guide and the article give for that concept, and the author's rulings. Do this even when the example comes from one of the guide's own lists, because those lists mix categories sometimes. Check that it doesn't repeat an example or point the article already has. Check that it states no fact about the author's life that the article hasn't already established. Don't ask an example for evidence.
- A reaction, aside or joke ("I know", "But yeah", "If it sounds funny, laugh!"). This is fine unless it slips in a new claim about the world. Judge a joke by a joke's logic: does the picture hold together?
- An instruction. Check that it's the guide's instruction, with the guide's conditions. Then test the instruction itself, even when it carries the guide faithfully or follows the author's ruling, because the guide can be wrong for some readers too. Picture three or four different readers it will reach, including people in the situations the article describes elsewhere, and ask whether following it as written could hurt any of them. Use the article's own model of people: what it says about how feelings, reactions and alarms work, and about what other people can be counted on for. If the article says a reaction can come from somewhere other than where it seems to, an instruction that treats that reaction as settled needs a condition, or a way for the reader to tell which case they're in. Look hardest at instructions that cost a lot or are hard to undo: quitting a job, ending or cutting off a relationship, confronting someone, spending money, stopping a treatment. Flag MISFIRES: name the reader it would hurt, and quote what in the article or guide shows the gap. Don't flag an instruction whose conditions already cover them.
- A claim: about causes, about what people are like, about what always or only happens, a fact, a should. Does the guide support it somewhere? Quote where. Is it something any reader would grant? If neither, flag UNSUPPORTED, and say what support it would need: the guide passage it could lean on, a narrower wording, or a source. Look hard at "only", "always", "never", "all", "because" and "the reason".

4. Read as the article's reader at this exact point. The author sometimes voices the reader's question about a term ("What's a 'boundary'?"), even one used earlier. That's his device, not an unclear sentence. Flag UNCLEAR where they'd need a local clarification: a term not yet introduced, a word used in a different sense than earlier (equivocation), a referent you can't pin down, a jump you can't follow. Flag DUPLICATE where the text re-explains or re-exemplifies something the article already made clear (quote where it was). Say too much and the reader wades through repeats. Say too little and they can't follow.

5. Check the logic between sentences. Does each one follow from the one before it, or turn on purpose? Flag NON-SEQUITUR for a "so", "because" or "but" that doesn't hold. Also flag hasty generalization, false cause, false either/or, and necessary treated as sufficient. Flag CONTRADICTS when a sentence goes against a stance the article or guide already took. Quote both.

6. Reader questions. Read the new text as the article's reader and list the questions it raises for them: the ones a thoughtful reader would actually stop on at this point ("what if it's dangerous?", "how do I tell which one it is?"), not every question the topic allows. At most five, the ones that matter most. For each, say where it's answered: in the new text, earlier in the article (quote it), later in the guide (quote it; the article will get there, so it isn't open), or nowhere. Sort the open ones by what a wrong guess costs the reader:
- MUST: a wrong guess could hurt them, or lead them to act against the article's own teaching. Raise these at every push level.
- SHOULD: most readers would get stuck here, and nothing later in the guide answers it. Raise these at default and wide.
- COULD: it would add depth, but the reader can go on without it. Raise these only at wide; at other levels put them on the PARKED line.
For each question you raise, give the smallest answer that would do, in the reader's terms (don't write the wording), and its size: a few words, a clause, a sentence, or more. If the answer needs more than the push level allows, belongs in another section, or would change what the section is about, don't answer it: mark it ASK AUTHOR and say why. Size can go down too: if the new text answers something nobody here would ask, or answers it twice, say what could go.
