The author's standing bans (Joel, from 2026-09-28 19:51 on, his words in quotes). Never write the first three or the last three, and treat them as AI tells when you see them. The rest are cautions and tells to look for:
- "doesn't get to decide" and its family: a feeling, urge, part or thing that "doesn't get to", "gets to" or "gets a vote". Joel: "one of the phrases AI completely colonized", especially about something that isn't a person; "an optimal efficiency quippy formulation which humans just rarely use for non-humans".
- "Fine," "Good," "Great," and the like as a clause or a sentence of their own ("Fine, they're nice boots."). Joel: "super AI".
- Wry humor. Joel: "the wry humor is also an AI tell. it's wry in a strange way". His own humor is goofy and glad, not dry.
- Too many made-up scenes. This is a caution, not a ban. Joel, 20:13: "i'm not saying to ban made-up scenes, they can be useful, but you're overusing them here" (and 19:51: "you're making way too many example scenarios"). Carry the guide's own examples first.
- Over-explaining listicles. A list is fine when the list carries the meaning, and don't cut a list down if that strips something important. Joel: "I have human and humanized paras with instructions and lists that DO pass".
- A feeling or an abstract idea as the one doing something a person does: going somewhere, trying, wanting, deciding, refusing, arriving. This is one of the top tells to look for, not a ban: better writers use it, and Joel finds it "rhetorically quite nice many times", but it reads as AI "especially when it's looking super high polished or overused" (2026-10-02). Joel, 2026-10-02: "the usage of abstract concepts or feelings as agents is one AI tell because it permits high efficiency of words." He changed "so the anger goes there, trying to get some justice" to "so the angry communard goes there, trying to get some justice": the action goes back to a person, with as few words changed as possible. An everyday idiom ("money won't fix it") can stay.
- A balanced "X enough to A and Y enough to B" pair. Joel, 2026-10-02, on "devoted enough to practice them and secure enough to disagree": "looks super highly polished".
- Chat acronyms (IMO, TBH and the like) where the article doesn't already use them: say it in words. Joel, 2026-10-01: "for articles where the acronyms are used, you'd use those, but for other articles where they aren't used, you wouldn't put them in unless the user asks for informal internet slang style".
- A list of three or more strung through one sentence ("A, or B, or C"; "A, B, and C"). Joel, 2026-10-03: "lists of 3 in general are an ai pattern". A paragraph with two of them failed (66% AI); his fix kept two items in each list and gave the rest a sentence of its own, and it passed. Never put two in one paragraph. And at 01:06: "when there's no actual need for 3 items you can remove one of them, the one that matters the least. when you need all 3 items to be there for meaning, then split it up like i did."
- A marching order: every sentence takes the next step of a procedure (when it happens, what happens, a caution, what to do, what to do next), so the paragraph reads like code instructions put into English. Joel, 2026-10-03: "it's the marching order that's why it's AI"; and of his fix, which breaks the march with the writer reacting in the moment ("All of a sudden your little one is right there! Oh my gosh!"): "i'm not saying to make oh my gosh into a rule but just see how it can break up the marching order of code instructions translated to english". Changing the words inside the steps doesn't fix it. Break the sequence where a person talking would react, or add something as it occurs to them. Joel, 21:18, on his own instruction paragraphs: an aside that loosens the instruction ("or however you'd actually say that" is a break), a pause or a permission ("Wait and listen. If nothing comes, that's fine for now..."), stopping "with considering what something might feel like", and stopping the instructions at "just listen", so "it's really not one long instruction text".
- Smuggling talk: "not smuggled into one sentence here", "rather than smuggling it in" and the like. Joel, 2026-10-03: "ai is always saying something like 'not smuggle in' or something about smuggling in." Give the plain reason instead, as he did: "My reasons are given in those articles, respectively, since they need more space."
- A "not Y" tail on a finished claim ("…in those articles, not smuggled into one sentence here"). Joel, 2026-10-03: "it always wants to add a 'not Y' part." Say the claim plainly; keep a contrast only when the paragraph needs the other side said.
- Changing a logical relation. "Or" isn't "also", "and" isn't "or", "may" isn't "will". Joel, 2026-10-03: "OR is not ALSO." Keep every connective, negation, quantifier and modal the original has, unless the new one says exactly the same thing.
