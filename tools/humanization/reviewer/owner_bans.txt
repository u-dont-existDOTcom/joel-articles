The author's standing bans and cautions (Joel, from 2026-09-28 on; his words in quotes). Almost none of these is a sure sign of AI: Joel, 2026-10-07 03:24, "almost nothing is a for sure AI tell, but they are way overused by AI." So most of them are cautions: use one only when the paragraph really needs it. The never-write ones are bans for a practical reason. Humans do write them sometimes, but AI writing falls back on them by default, so a caution wouldn't stop them. Joel, 2026-10-07 15:44: "humans do write this sometimes, so i hate to say straight up ban, but i'm afraid otherwise it will not really be avoided".

Never write these:
- "Doesn't get to decide" and its family ("doesn't get to", "gets to decide", "gets a vote", "gets a say") when the subject isn't a person: a feeling, an urge, a thought, a part of you, the weather, a rule, an idea. Joel, 2026-09-28: "one of the phrases AI completely colonized", "an optimal efficiency quippy formulation which humans just rarely use for non-humans". 2026-10-07 15:44: "that was regarding abstract subjects. "the weather doesn't get to decide" for example, not "your dad doesn't get to decide"". With a person as the subject it's ordinary English and can stay.
- "Fine," "Good," "Great," and the like as a clause or a sentence of their own ("Fine, they're nice boots."). Joel: "super AI".
- Wry humor. Joel: "the wry humor is also an AI tell. it's wry in a strange way". His own humor is goofy and glad, not dry. When a joke could read dry, mark it as glad: Joel, 2026-10-06, put 😂 after two jokes in community section 5 "because they were clearly needed to prevent the humor from being so wry".
- A weekday, clock time or made-up day put in to sound concrete. The repo's SKILL.md: "Never invent casual day-of-week or clock-time details merely to make prose sound human or concrete." Joel, 2026-10-07: "AI is always saying Tuesday, on Tuesday, or some specific day like this. on a regular Tuesday is the worst." Keep a day or time only when it's a real fact from the source and matters to the thought.
- Joel's Claude tics (his standing preference for all writing): "doing some work", "load-bearing", "tells on itself", digestive words for dealing with something ("metabolize", "digest"), "clean", "does its work", and the "That's not X. It's Y." construction.

The most important tell: the marching order. Joel, 2026-10-07 15:44: "the marching order is really important". Every sentence takes the next step of a procedure (when it happens, what happens, a caution, what to do, what to do next), so the paragraph reads like code instructions put into English. Joel, 2026-10-03: "it's the marching order that's why it's AI"; and of his fix, which breaks the march with the writer reacting in the moment ("All of a sudden your little one is right there! Oh my gosh!"): "i'm not saying to make oh my gosh into a rule but just see how it can break up the marching order of code instructions translated to english". Changing the words inside the steps doesn't fix it. Break the sequence where a person talking would react, or add something as it occurs to them. Joel, 21:18, on his own instruction paragraphs: an aside that loosens the instruction ("or however you'd actually say that" is a break), a pause or a permission ("Wait and listen. If nothing comes, that's fine for now..."), stopping "with considering what something might feel like", and stopping the instructions at "just listen", so "it's really not one long instruction text". Joel, 2026-10-04: "it's not about better explaining. it's about breaking up the instruction manual flow, which requires some kind of within-the-thought reflection or jumping"; "how would a human author re-engage the reader here?" A break that worked: a bare condition turned into what someone in the scene is thinking ("I think that's what it wants to see anyway, whether there's a grown-up here now who could stop if it got to be too much"). Breaks that didn't: the stock casual moves ("Nope.", "Aww, buddy.", a goofy exclamation, a punchy closer), each 100% AI.

Cautions: overused by AI, so use one only when it says something the paragraph needs:
- Lists. Joel, 2026-10-07 15:44: "lists of 3 especially, and lists in general are overused by AI. try to avoid that unless it's really needed." That covers a bulleted or numbered list and a list strung through a sentence ("A, or B, or C"; "A, B, and C"). Joel, 2026-10-03: "lists of 3 in general are an ai pattern"; a paragraph with two of them failed (66% AI), and his fix kept two items in each list and gave the rest a sentence of its own, and it passed. Never two in one paragraph. At 01:06: "when there's no actual need for 3 items you can remove one of them, the one that matters the least. when you need all 3 items to be there for meaning, then split it up like i did." A list that carries the meaning can stay, and don't cut one down if that strips something important: "I have human and humanized paras with instructions and lists that DO pass".
- A feeling or an abstract idea doing what a person does: going somewhere, trying, wanting, deciding, refusing, arriving. Joel, 2026-10-07 15:44: "abstract things doing what people do is way overused by ai because it sounds polished." 2026-10-02: "the usage of abstract concepts or feelings as agents is one AI tell because it permits high efficiency of words"; it's "rhetorically quite nice many times", and worst "when it's looking super high polished or overused". By default give the action to a person, with as few words changed as possible: he changed "so the anger goes there, trying to get some justice" to "so the angry communard goes there, trying to get some justice". An everyday idiom ("money won't fix it") can stay.
- The x-not-y family, in all its shapes: a "not Y" tail on a finished claim ("…in those articles, not smuggled into one sentence here"), "not X, but still Y" ("It isn't proof, but it still counts"), "not X but Y", "not just X, but Y", "less X than Y", and an x-not-y heading. Joel, 2026-10-03: "it always wants to add a 'not Y' part"; 2026-10-06: "always trying to do an x not y statement ... humans don't do x not y as much"; 2026-10-07 15:44: "there are other X Y rules, like "Not x, but still y."" Say the claim plainly. Keep a contrast only when the reader needs the other side said, and then give it its own words. His heading for the medicine section names the thing ("The Medicine Part - Yes, I'm Naming It") where "The Medicine Part, Without Pretending It Isn't There" read AI.
- A balanced "X enough to A and Y enough to B" pair. Joel, 2026-10-02, on "devoted enough to practice them and secure enough to disagree": "looks super highly polished".
- Smuggling talk: "not smuggled into one sentence here", "rather than smuggling it in" and the like. Joel, 2026-10-03: "ai is always saying something like 'not smuggle in' or something about smuggling in." Give the plain reason instead, as he did: "My reasons are given in those articles, respectively, since they need more space." (The linter fails the figurative kind.)
- Too many made-up scenes. Joel, 2026-09-28 20:13: "i'm not saying to ban made-up scenes, they can be useful, but you're overusing them here" (and 19:51: "you're making way too many example scenarios"). Carry the guide's own examples first.
- "Ordinary", "regular", "boring", "everyday". Joel, 2026-10-07 03:24: "boring, regular etc are not for sure AI tells, almost nothing is a for sure AI tell, but they are way overused by AI." Use one when it says something, not as filler.
- A made-up number on something nobody measures ("five percent of it"). Joel, 2026-10-07: "it's ok to say 5% is enough altho i feel it's a bit ai, like AI wants to put concrete numbers on stuff all the time, then people are wondering "how much is 5%?"". "A tiny bit" or "a small piece" says it without the question.
- Chat acronyms (IMO, TBH and the like) where the article doesn't already use them: say it in words. Joel, 2026-10-01: "for articles where the acronyms are used, you'd use those, but for other articles where they aren't used, you wouldn't put them in unless the user asks for informal internet slang style".

Meaning rules (about saying the right thing, not about sounding like AI):
- Changing a logical relation. "Or" isn't "also", "and" isn't "or", "may" isn't "will". Joel, 2026-10-03: "OR is not ALSO." Keep every connective, negation, quantifier and modal the original has, unless the new one says exactly the same thing.
- A rewrite that keeps the guide's example but loses what the example was for, or says the guide's sentence the wrong way round. Joel, 2026-10-06: "your P1 is missing most of the actual meaning of the original. The original is asking to see what exactly is blocking rather than giving up on everything"; and on "And if it was comforting your little one, was the loving grown-up really there?": "why would it be comforting if adult was not there?". Say the guide passage's main instruction outright, and check every sentence against the guide sentence it carries. A vague guide phrase gets clearer, not a new concrete meaning ("better-supported" is not "better research").
- The writer given a move the next sentence takes back. Joel, 2026-10-07, on "I'd probably want to fix it by saying sweeter and sweeter things, which won't work, and I wouldn't decide reparenting is useless over it, either. I'd borrow that grown-up first.": "this part is contradictory". His fix gave the mistaken move to "some people" ("Some people at that point might try saying sweeter and sweeter things…"). The author's own wants belong in the text only when they matter to the reader's decision (SKILL.md, "Fake personal stake").
