So my childhood was a lot of bouncing around between different communes like Twin Oaks, Acorn, East Wind, Padanaram, Zendik, etc. Later I lived with the Love Israel family in Seattle as we escaped from “Y2K” if you remember that! I was never quite the commune kid; I was the perpetual visitor’s kid, which turned out to be rarer and stranger: I got the comparison view. Dozens of experiments, side by side, decades apart.

Thirty years later I went back and checked on the communities of my childhood, and the honest report is: they haven’t changed much. Acorn was too small then and is too small now. Twin Oaks was too big in the ways that matter. East Wind was about the right size. And the two bigger ones shared the same two diseases, which no amount of size can fix: no communal therapy, and bureaucratic politics filling the space where the healing should have been.

That’s the pattern this whole article turns on, but rather than the communal movement evolving, what happened, AFAICT, is it basically stagnated for a few decades, and now….

Suddenly Communes Are Hot, Again
In 2023, the U.S. Surgeon General’s report was centered on the “Epidemic of Loneliness” and encouraging people to build social connections. Then last year, suddenly, interest has been surging. Intentional community interest is back. APNews also reported on the surging movements to increase social connection. They highlight that 1 in 4 young adults feels lonely most or all of the time now. USAToday shows that loneliness in older Americans doubled, from 2018 to 2020, going up to 56%. This epidemic is real, and official statistics show it’s currently killing more people than Covid (not that I ever believed those stats, since flu mysteriously vanished).

Pull up Google Trends and search the topic “ecovillage” or “intentional community,” worldwide, from 2004 to 2026. People had been losing interest since Google began measuring things.



The line doesn’t rise gently out of COVID loneliness. It stays dead flat through 2022, 2023, 2024 — and then changes state, right around the moment AI went from tech-press curiosity to something reorganizing everyone’s work, feeds, and sense of the future.

It’s poppin’ too on Instagram! Back in March, Chantress Seba — my favorite light-language love influencer — was recently collecting applications to start a commune. I sent one in myself, but I don’t think she’s coming to Africa. 11k people liked her post, expressing genuine interest, and many also commented that they sent an email. I can imagine she and her partner realized they didn’t have the ability to respond to many thousands of emails!


Just a week ago, a carnivore-diet influencer with a million followers has once again floated the idea of a commune in an Instagram post, and 155k people so far have liked this post. Thousands replied, and many are organizing communes themselves, recruiting directly in the IG comments! Nearly everyone I talk to, on three continents, is some version of tired of modern society and wondering aloud about the exit.


So the desire is arriving. What isn’t arriving with it is the knowledge, because the knowledge is buried in out-of-print sociology, in the memories of people now in their seventies, and in the wreckage of a few thousand communities that failed for the same five reasons in a row.

This article is that knowledge, or as much of it as I can carry. It is not a pitch for my community. I’m not currently founding one, and the article is more useful that way — I’m not recruiting you, I’m equipping you. If I ever do help found something, every warning in here will apply to me, and I’d want you to hold me to it. However, I do plan to buy some land soon, and I assume people will probably come to live with me naturally over time. Community is almost inescapable.

Why communities fail: the two deaths
Strip away the details, and intentional communities die in two ways.

The secular death. The egalitarian communities — the FEC world, the income-sharing farms — solved ownership and labor and then hit a wall they didn’t have a name for. They had no way to metabolize what people actually carry: the jealousy, the childhood wounds, the resentments that compound at 3% weekly interest. So procedure moved in where healing should have lived. Meetings metastasized. The politics of the committee replaced the politics of the heart. People didn’t explode; they eroded. They left quietly, one processed-to-death conflict at a time, and what remained was a functioning economy inhabited by people carefully not talking about the things that mattered.

The spiritual death. The communities that did put inner transformation at the center almost always routed it through one person. A teacher, a founder, a channel. And concentrated spiritual authority is the cult mechanism itself — it doesn’t matter how good the teaching is. The Farm in Tennessee, founded by Stephen Gaskin, had genuinely beautiful values and fifteen hundred people. But with all those people, Gaskin’s doctrines still reached into every bedroom: sex meant engagement, pregnancy meant marriage. When his system cracked in 1983, families cracked with it. The teaching wasn’t the problem. The plumbing was — everything flowed through him.

So the secular communities have distributed power and no depth, and the spiritual communities have depth and no distributed power. Two half-communities, failing in mirror image, for sixty years.

The thing that’s missing is spiritual community without the cult plumbing: depth work and play (pl/ork) that is peer-distributed — held by everyone, owed to no one. That’s what the rest of this article is about.

The conversation problem
Here’s something I have to say before we go further, and I’ll say it straight because dressing it up would be dishonest.

Most people are not currently in a condition to have this conversation. I don’t mean they’re stupid. I mean modern life has damaged them — the isolation, the screens, the childhood nobody helped them digest — to the point where the topics this article requires (jealousy, death, God, what was done to you as a child, what you’ve done to others) trigger defense before they reach comprehension. I can’t hold a decent conversation about sensitive things with most people I meet, and I’ve spent my life practicing exactly that.

That’s just the system that we’re in. Its job is to squeeze us all into little boxes and suffocate us while extracting whatever little energy we have.

So this article is for the people who can sit with the next several thousand words without flinching. Some of you can. The damage is unevenly distributed — some people have done years of work, or got lucky in childhood, or both, and walk around with attention to spare. Others are still bleeding from wounds that run their whole lives. Most sit somewhere between, and where you sit decides what you can build and with whom. There's math about this later in the article. Whoever joins your community arrives somewhere on that line too, and the community needs machinery for moving people along it — machinery that heals faster than daily life wounds. Everything below is that machinery.

The four domains
The work divides into four domains. I’ve published detailed guides for the first three — free, no funnel, no retreat to buy — and the fourth is what this article mostly is. Underneath all four runs one principle: nothing suppressed, everything processed, authority stays with the practitioner, and the community provides the container and the ever-evolving answers — fluid and organic, never frozen into doctrine.

The cult failure comes from freezing the answers, not from having them. A community that can’t keep revising its own practice from lived experience goes one of two ways: dogmatic (answers locked) or empty (a container with nothing in it — a surface performance, a survival program). The living third way is answers continually emerging from collective practice, held firmly enough to act on and loosely enough to outgrow.

1. Inner work. Reparenting the child structures. Learning to love the child you were and become the adult that child needed. Somatic regulation, so the body stops vetoing what the mind decides. Contemplative practice — mine is grounded in the early Buddhist suttas, taken critically: most of the suttas are treasure, some are obviously late additions, and the abhidhamma is a scholastic wrong turn. I say that openly because a community’s relationship to its own tradition should be exactly that — devoted, and unafraid to disagree.

2. Relational work. Peer counseling — my Hearthwork manual is published and free. Counseling that flows peer to peer, in pairs and small groups, so the healing capacity of the community lives in everyone rather than in one professional or one guru. Jealousy processing. Honesty practices. Conflict metabolized in dyads and triads as it arises, instead of stockpiled for the monthly meeting where it arrives wearing a suit and calling itself politics. I also have a Romance Guide which helps the dyads that would be making up the community.

3. Medicine work. Careful, peer-guided use of the serious plant medicines. This gets its own section below, because it’s the controversial one and I refuse to hide it.

4. Container design. Membership, land, money, governance, children, exit. The boring architecture that decides whether the first three domains get room to work or get crushed under a mortgage dispute. Most of the rest of this article.

These four stand together. Communities that take the container without the inner work die the secular death. Communities that take the depth without the container die the spiritual one. Skip a domain and the community falls apart along exactly that seam.

The medicine work, said out loud
Almost no commune that uses psychedelics talks about it publicly. The exceptions are actual tribes and the Brazilian ayahuasca churches — Santo Daime’s Mapiá, the União do Vegetal — where a legal carve-out or an unbroken tradition provides cover. Everyone else stays quiet, for the obvious reason that these medicines are illegal or socially radioactive across most of the world.

I’m going to talk about it, because you’re reading my Substack, which means you’re probably already with me on this, having read my psychedelic guides— and because leaving it out would falsify the whole model. So, both sides, honestly.

The case against is real. These medicines are chaotic and unpredictable, especially when used carelessly (as you can see from the Global Ayahuasca Survey). They can open doors in a person before the person is ready for what’s behind them. They attract seekers who want the experience without the discipline — the ceremony junkies, collecting openings the way other people collect passport stamps. A community that builds around them without structure gets the chaos and misses the depth.

The case for is also real, and in my experience decisive: worked with properly, these medicines let people do in months what otherwise takes decades. They dissolve the exact defenses that make the conversation problem above so universal. And they bond people — a group that has walked through iboga nights together, that has held each other through the dismantling and the reassembly, is bound in a way no committee meeting will ever produce. Without something at that depth, “community” tends to remain a surface performance or a survival program. With it, the thing actually comes alive.

I won’t idealize them. They aren’t beneficial for everyone, and the world’s current wave of enthusiasm will produce its own casualties. But the world really is waking up to what they can do for deep inner work and play — and sometimes they open spiritual territory the person didn’t know existed, ready or not. That, too, has to be held by the community rather than by a stranger with a feather.

The design principles, then:
No shamans. This is the same anti-authority principle as everywhere else in the model. The traditional world mostly disagrees with me here — many ayahuasca lineages hold that nobody should drink outside their guidance, and the Bwiti hold the same about iboga. I respect what those traditions have preserved and I’ve learned from them. But handing your deepest openings to a charismatic stranger you barely know is exactly the plumbing that turns spiritual community into cult, and the retreat-circuit shaman — unvetted, unaccountable, economically incentivized to keep you coming back — takes your power away at the precise moment you’re most open. People need to learn to guide themselves and hold each other. I’ve written elsewhere about how to run ceremonies on that basis, and a cautionary tale about the best psychopath shaman I ever met.

The medicine is gated behind the other work. Inner-child work and peer counseling first. The medicine confirms and deepens work already underway; it can’t substitute for it, and a community that lets it substitute has just invented a faster way to fall apart. The gate also quietly solves the selection problem — the experience-collectors won’t do six months of unglamorous reparenting first, and that’s the point of the gate.

Integration is the practice; the ceremony is the doorway. Most of the work happens in the weeks after — in counseling pairs, in the body, in changed daily behavior. A community that ceremonies weekly and integrates never is doing spiritual entertainment with extra steps.

Supports matter. The traditional medicines come embedded in nutritional and preparatory context that the retreat circuit strips away. I’ve written about the supports I use in my Altered States Triage guide.

Legality is a real constraint and lying about it helps no one. Where I live, in Senegal, iboga and ayahuasca aren’t explicitly illegal — nobody here has heard of them; weed, meanwhile, is very illegal. Gabon protects iboga inside Bwiti tradition; Brazil and neighboring countries protect ayahuasca inside tribes & churches. Most everywhere else, this work is underground, and every community doing it is making a legal bet it should make with open eyes, in writing, before the first ceremony. This is a coupling decision — next section explains what I mean.

The closing window, and selecting your couplings
Why now? Loneliness is only half of it. Something is converging.

The technologies arriving now — programmable medicine down to the cell, gene editing in the clinic, brain-computer interfaces in human trials, AI mediating a growing share of all human attention — point toward a merger of the biological and the digital that the people building it describe openly and enthusiastically. You don’t need the conspiracy version; the published version is plenty. Watch the World Economic Forum’s old exec, Klaus Schwab on the Fourth Industrial Revolution: the stated program is fusion of the physical, digital, and biological.

Meanwhile, the perimeter tightens in ordinary, documentable ways — border agents scan social media, dozens of central banks are building programmable currency, biometric ID covers billions of people, and our collective ability to hold a thought for three unbroken minutes decays measurably year over year.

I won’t predict dates. Tech prophecy has a terrible record and I refuse to add to it. What I’ll tell you is what I’m doing: I practice to end rebirth, in the Buddha’s sense, and I want to do that work while a human nervous system with sovereign attention is still an available thing to have. The window in which one can live at a distance from the machine — think your own thoughts, feel your own body, raise children whose minds weren’t auctioned to an algorithm before age ten — appears to be narrowing. Maybe it narrows slower than I fear. I’d rather build the exit and not need it than need it and not have built it. If the stakes are what they appear to be, this is an almost Armageddon-shaped choice about which side of the merger you stand on — and I’d rather spend my last free decades in practice among people I love than in a feed.

For community design, this replaces the tired question “are you pro-technology or anti-technology?” with the right one: which couplings to the technosphere do you keep, and which do you cut?

Every connection to the dominant system — medical, financial, communicative, legal, alimentary, energetic — is a separate decision with real costs in both directions. Full embrace dissolves the community into the thing it left. Full rejection is a slower dissolution plus dysentery. The failure mode is drifting into whatever couplings the world installs by default instead of choosing. The Amish, whatever else you think of them, have run the alternative for a century: every technology gets evaluated by the community against one criterion — does this strengthen us or dissolve us? — then adopted, rejected, or fenced. That’s the most sophisticated technology policy on Earth, and your community needs its own version, in writing.

The hardest coupling is medicine, so look at it directly. Modern medicine is about to get very good — possibly nanobot-and-gene-edit good — and almost everything it offers will require full enrollment in the system: your genome, your biometrics, your compliance. A community at real distance from the technosphere will face the day when a beloved member has a condition the system could fix and the community’s chosen distance can’t reach. Decide now what you keep (trauma surgery, antibiotics, midwifery, dentistry, the pharmaceuticals that clearly serve) and what you’ve released, and write down the protocol for catastrophic illness — including the honest clause that a member who chooses full re-enrollment for treatment is making a reasonable choice, not committing betrayal. Communities have shattered on exactly this rock, mid-grief, improvising.

And take the consolation, because it’s empirical: community itself is the strongest medicine most people will ever take. The chronic diseases eating the modern world run on isolation, despair, ultra-processed food, sedentary confinement, and sleep wrecked by screens — every one of which a real community treats around the clock, for free. Belonging won’t cure cancer. It will prevent an enormous share of what kills people slowly.

Jealousy is the enemy — monogamy and free love are decoys
Every community that lasts longer than a season gets reorganized by romance, so this domain gets designed consciously or it designs you.

The usual debate is monogamy versus free love, and the debate is a decoy. I’ve written a full article on romance, so here’s just the spine. Strict, property-style monogamy — the kind that arrived with inheritance and land title — institutionalizes jealousy, builds a fence around it, and calls the fence sacred. Free love without inner work distributes the same shrapnel across more people. The tribal record, for what it’s worth, shows human pair-bonding as real but flexible nearly everywhere private property hasn’t rigidified it — couples form, couples change, and the village holds the children and the continuity either way.

So the non-negotiable sits elsewhere: jealousy cannot run the community. Nothing suppressed — suppression is just scheduling the explosion. Everything processed — in counseling, in the body, in ceremony when needed — and genuinely released, for the person’s own emotional and spiritual liberation, not merely managed like a chronic condition.

Look at the communities that have actually run this experiment for decades. At ZEGG and Tamera, primary partnerships form; some couples choose sexual monogamy; others don’t; structures shift as people grow. The community decides for nobody — it can’t, and it shouldn’t. What it provides is the support and the freedom to experiment within bounds, with real help on hand when the old wounds surface. That’s the model: the community holds the container; individuals choose the form; the work makes both forms possible. Without the work, both forms fail identically — the post-mortems just use different vocabulary.

What the children will say about you
My own childhood broke most of the mainstream rules about what a child is supposed to see and do. I won’t detail it here, aside from thanking my parents for not vaccinating me, and thanking my mom for nursing me until age 5. What I’ll say is that I’m grateful for nearly all of it — and that gratitude, the grown child looking back and saying yes, that was good for me, is the only audit that counts. Not the parents’ intentions. Not the ideology. The verdict of the child, thirty years on. Design for that verdict.

A friend reviewing this draft grew up in a Christian commune in Colorado where her father was a pastor. She calls her childhood “a treasure and a burden” — the treasure of belonging, of everyone caring for everyone; the burden of a strict religious life she never chose. Every commune childhood produces its own version of that sentence. The design question is what goes in each half.

Some positions I hold as fixed:

The mother is primary. The village is real. Neither replaces the other. Mothers raise their own children — within communal norms, with the workload genuinely distributed across the community, and in a web where fathers may not always even be known and the fathering is carried communally. What the community may not do is take the child from the mother in the name of freeing it. Tamera moves children into a Children’s Place, apart from parents, from age four; the Israeli kibbutzim ran that experiment at scale for two generations, and it was the kibbutz-raised children themselves who shut it down. I side with the kibbutz children over the kibbutz theorists.

Children need their own commune inside the commune. A territory that belongs to them, where adults enter rarely and by invitation, where a society of children learns relationship on its own terms while adults hold the outer container. I tasted this at a Unitarian youth camp as a kid and it remains one of the freest memories I have. ZEGG’s Kinderhaus works this way. Every functional tribal culture I know of works this way: the child-pack raises the child half the day, the village the other half, the parents the core.

The door must open outward at adulthood. The Amish send their youth into the world at age 16, free to stay out. Roughly 85 percent come back and kneel for baptism — above 90 in the old settlements — partly because the outside world is genuinely crazy, partly because nothing out there matches what they grew up inside. But the return is a choice, and the choice changes the whole life built on it. A community whose children never leave never learns whether anyone would stay. The Zapatistas are living this problem right now: their young people leach away toward the phones and the cities, because when the whole world seems to move at light speed, staying home feels like being left behind. No design fully solves this. There is only raising children whose home was so alive that the lit-up world looks dim by comparison — and then opening the door anyway.

And maybe the ones who leave should go start their own communities, with their own evolved understandings, rather than waiting decades to inherit the founders’. That’s how a movement reproduces instead of merely aging.

One more hard truth: childrearing values split couples who love each other — two people, one culture, one bed, and they still divorce over screens, vaccines, and discipline. Now multiply by twenty adults from twenty backgrounds. This is one of the biggest stumbling blocks in community life, and goodwill can’t resolve it after the fact. It has to be resolved before the fact, which brings me to:

Choose by values, not by view
People choose communities the way they choose vacation rentals — the land is beautiful, everyone was warm at dinner. Two years later they collide with the community’s real positions on the things that actually run a life: children, money, sex, medicine, food, God. And they discover their own positions at the same moment, mid-fight, about all of them at once.

Do it in the right order. Before you visit anywhere, sit down and find out what your values actually are — on children, on money, on relationship structure, on medicine, on practice, on the couplings you’d keep and cut. Write them down. Where you’re unsure, write that down; “willing to experiment” is a legitimate value, but it has to be declared, because the person beside you may not be experimenting. Then choose or build the community that matches the list. The garden is beautiful, but the values are what you’ll be living with.

The gold standard: what I saw in Chiapas
In 2005, I and a few college friends visited Oventik, one of the Zapatista caracoles in Chiapas. Twenty years later it’s still the closest model to my ideal of how federated communities should work.

The Zapatistas hold territory across the most resource-rich state in Mexico. They’ve held it for thirty years against a state that functions as a cartel, with paramilitaries burning villages at intervals the whole time. They have their own military, the EZLN, which largely renounced violence for anything but self-defense — and that capacity for self-defense is part of why they still exist. They govern through a principle they call mandar obedeciendo — to lead by obeying. Leaders are more like spokespeople than deciders, and they rotate monthly. Big decisions are made by consensus. In 2023 they reorganized to push authority even further down, dissolving larger councils into smaller local ones. Most movements consolidate power over time. They keep dissolving it.

If you’re serious about building community, go visit them. Study the system directly, not through academic filters. Almost nobody writing about intentional community in the West has bothered, which tells you whose examples get counted.

Now the caveat, and it’s the size of a mountain: their system works because it grew from centuries of shared Mayan governance culture. The 1994 uprising didn’t invent their way of deciding — it defended a way that already existed. You can copy the forms of governance. You can’t copy the cultural depth that makes people trust the forms. A new community adopting Zapatista structures without that depth gets a thin parody. Depth is grown, not installed, and growing it takes years of living together and processing everything that comes up. Anyone selling a shortcut is selling.

(Sociocracy, arrived at strikingly similar shapes — circles, consent, distributed authority, double links — from a completely different starting point: Dutch organizational engineering with Quaker roots, decades before the West had heard of the EZLN. When Mayan tradition and European engineers independently land on the same structures, the structures are probably tracking something true.)

Founderism: the four questions
Someone (or some small group) founds every community. Someone writes its methods, holds its early vision, filters its early members. Those founder fingerprints are on everything — and there begins the oldest failure in the book.

I know this tension from the inside, because the methods in this article are mine. The founder writes the methodology; the founder then decides who has understood the methodology; the founder is therefore filtering the community through their own mind no matter how anti-authoritarian the content of the teaching. No structure fully dissolves this. There’s a permanent tension between echo chamber and chaos, and it can only be held consciously, never resolved.

What helps: the methodology should reject external authority in its own content, so a founder grasping for control is visibly violating their own teaching, and everyone trained in the work can see it. The practice should be judged by outcomes anyone can observe — are people actually flourishing, are conflicts actually metabolizing, do the children actually thrive — rather than by fidelity to the founder’s interpretation. And there should be many parallel implementations, like Linux, so no single community is “the authentic one.” Fork freely. Forks are the immune system.

And here’s the test — four questions, answerable by anyone from outside in an afternoon:

Could the community respectfully override the founder? Replace the founder’s role? Revise the founder’s vision? Survive the founder’s absence?

If any answer is no, founderism is still baked into the structure — whatever the bylaws say, whatever the founder says, however sincerely everyone denies it. Ask these four questions of any community you’re considering. Ask them of any teacher whose methods you adopt. Ask them of me, if I end up with people living on my land. The people most likely to capture a community are precisely the ones who assure you the risk doesn’t apply to them.

The everyday warning signs, while you’re at it: the founder grows more central over time instead of less. Deviation gets framed as failure rather than legitimate variation. When an implementation fails, the explanation is always the implementers, never the method. The gap between insider knowledge and public knowledge keeps widening. And measurement of outcomes gets quietly resisted. Any two of these together, and you should already be re-reading the exit clause.

The math of absorption, and who this is not for
Yes — in this model, the community is the therapist. That’s the design. Peer counseling means the healing capacity lives in everyone rather than in one professional or one guru. But there’s math underneath, and communities that ignore it drown.

Every person arrives with some ratio of free resource to need. Free resource — I’m borrowing the term from Re-evaluation Counseling — is the attention you have left over after your own material is handled well enough. Need is everything still bleeding. Everyone carries both, in wildly different proportions.

A community can absorb only as much need as its members have free resource to meet. That’s the whole equation. Eight people with two in deep crisis is a community spending everything on triage. Eighty people can carry several members through their worst year and barely feel it. The capacity to say yes to wounded people is a resource level, not a moral quality, and pretending otherwise sinks the boat for everyone — the wounded first.

And here’s the cruel paradox: small communities need new members most and can absorb them least. Small communities also can’t afford members without practical skills — eight people need the well fixed and the harvest in just as urgently as eighty do. Large communities could afford openness, but the large ones aren’t the ones desperately recruiting. So if you’re forming a group, your first members must arrive with more free resource than need, and with hands that know how to do something. That sounds harsh. It’s arithmetic.

So, plainly — this is not for you right now if:

You’re fleeing something rather than building toward something. The community will receive whatever you’re fleeing, at full strength, within six months.

You want the feeling of village life without the governance burden of village life. There’s no version where someone else handles the boring parts.

You’re unwilling to be seen — actually seen, in conflict, in jealousy, at your worst. The methods only work on what’s visible.

Or your need currently exceeds your free resource by a wide margin. This is no verdict on your worth — it’s been true of me at points in my life. It means the sequence is: do the inner work (and play) first, wherever you are, until you have something to bring besides the wound. The community you eventually join will be the better for it — and so will you, because you’ll enter as a builder instead of a patient.

The forming group is the community
The community doesn’t begin when you buy land. It begins years earlier, with the small group that decides to try. And that group has to pass one test almost no group knows is the test:

Before any money mixes, process one real conflict all the way through.

Not smoothed over. Not agreed-to-disagree. Processed — the jealousy or the resentment or the old wound actually named, felt, and released, using the methods the group has committed to. A group that has never done this together is a circle of strangers holding hands. A group that has done it once knows something about itself that no vision statement can tell it.

If your forming group can’t metabolize a conflict in a rented living room, it won’t survive a shared mortgage. The living room is cheap. Find out there.

Money and land: where the bodies are buried
I’ll be blunt, because this is where more communities die than anywhere else: whoever owns the land owns the community.

If the land is in the founder’s name, you have a landlord with a philosophy. If it’s in three names out of twelve members, you have a two-tier society, and everyone knows which tier they’re in even if nobody says it. The legal form — trust, cooperative, association, whatever your country offers — has to be decided before purchase. After is too late: after, the people on the deed have no reason to come off it, and the first serious dispute will crack along exactly the line the ownership papers drew.

The same goes for departure. People will leave — good people, for good reasons: a dying parent, a marriage, a changed heart. If leaving means losing everything they put in, staying stops being a choice and becomes a trap, and a community held together by traps is a hostage situation with a garden. Write the exit terms while everyone still loves each other. That’s the only time it’s possible.

None of this is romantic. Exactly. Romance is what everyone brings in abundance; the boring paperwork is what kills them. For the full sequencing — vision before membership before money before land — read Diana Leafe Christian’s Creating a Life Together, still the best single manual, built from interviews with founders of communities that made it and the far larger number that didn’t.

From visitor to member
Open doors helped kill The Farm commune in TN: fifteen hundred people, half children, most arriving with nothing but need, and no filter because filtering felt unspiritual. A membership process protects everyone already inside — including the newest member, who deserves to join something that won’t collapse on them.

A skeleton any group can adapt:

Correspond first. Has this person read the materials? Started the work? Can they say why this community and not another? Letters are cheap, and they separate fantasy from intent.

Short visit. Two weeks, working alongside people. Everyone’s charming for two weeks — which is exactly why this is only the first gate.

Long visit. Months. Long enough for a real conflict to arrive, because one will, and how a person moves through conflict is the only information that matters. It can’t be faked at this duration except by a professional faker.

Provisional membership. A defined period, a defined evaluation, decided by the membership or its delegated circle — never by one person, and especially never by whoever recruited them.

Full membership. With everything that means for land, money, and voice, under the founding agreements.

At every gate, the prerequisite is the work, not the vibe. Vibes are what con artists do best. Six months of unglamorous inner work is what they can’t fake.

Plan the funeral at the wedding
Nobody designs for their community’s death, which is why so many die badly.

Write the dissolution terms at founding. If it ends — and most do — what happens to the land? To each member’s stake? To the children’s schooling and stability while the adults dismantle their dream? A community that has answered these questions can fail with dignity: people leave whole, friendships outlive the institution, the children experience a move instead of a shipwreck. A community that can’t imagine its own end will fight over the corpse, and the lawyers will eat what’s left.

And take the true consolation. The Farm collapsed as a communal economy in 1983. Thirty years later, one of its grown children was diagnosed with cancer while making a documentary about the place — and former members she hadn’t seen in decades sent letters and checks. The institution failed. The relational field it built was still alive, still functioning, still hers. That’s what you’re actually building, and it can outlive every legal structure you wrap around it.

A note on Africa, since I write from it
I live in Senegal, and I should tell you what “intentional community” looks like from here: alien. Almost nobody has heard of it, and the reason turns the whole Western project inside out.

Africa never fully lost what the communities movement is trying to rebuild. Most land here has no title — it’s held by ancestral memory and covenant, not deeds. The villages sit on a spectrum that still touches living tribal culture: communal land, communal childcare, the village as the unit of survival. Westerners pay thousands of dollars for workshops that simulate, badly, what a Senegalese village does before breakfast.

But it’s dissolving. The women and children emigrate to the cities for jobs, phones, pizza — the whole catalog of modern wanting. The villages that don’t use money are nearly impossible to find now. The same solvent eating Western community from below is eating African community from above; one reaction, two stages. And the Africans who do resonate with intentional community are mostly urbanites — already Westernized, sick of the city, no longer at home in a tribe that either wouldn’t recognize them or no longer coheres. They arrive at the same homelessness Westerners start from, just by a shorter road.

Which means this movement is a global response to one dissolution, hitting everyone from different directions. And it means the remaining traditional communities are the last living reference implementations of everything in this article — and both the knowledge and the window are closing. If you’re serious about this work, a living village will teach you more than my whole reading list. Go respectfully. Go soon.

(Practical note, since people ask: Senegalese residency costs about $200 and is straightforward. English isn’t spoken — French among the educated, Wolof among much of the rest — and the Sufi culture that makes this country warm and safe is also watchful in ways a community here would need to design around. Every location is a bundle of trade-offs. This one’s mine.)

If you can’t move yet
Most people reading this are locked in — the job, the kids, the mortgage, the sick parent. Good. I mean that: the most important work needs no land, and the people who think it does are the ones who buy forty acres and import every kilo of their unprocessed material to a beautiful hillside.

The inner work starts now, in the apartment you already live in. Peer counseling needs two people and a room. The jealousy processing, the reparenting, the practice — none of it waits for a deed. Do the work in place, and when the move becomes possible you arrive as free resource instead of as need.

You may find something stranger: doing the work where you are creates a pocket of community where you are. A practice group that meets weekly for two years is already a small commune of the evenings. Some stay that way forever, and that’s a whole life. The land is just where the point gets room to grow.

What to do this month
Most people who read something like this feel a door open, close the tab, and change nothing — not from insincerity, but because nobody names the first step. So:

Start the inner work alone. The peer counseling manual and the reparenting guide are free. Or begin with whatever serious practice you already carry. Begin before you have company, because whoever won’t do the work alone won’t magically do it in a group.

Find two or three people and practice. Not a community — a practice group. A living room and a commitment to meet. Every community that works began as a few people learning to hear each other before they owned anything together. Where to find them? Unprogrammed Quaker meetings, where clearness committees have been doing proto–peer counseling for three centuries. Open-hearted congregations. Restorative-circle people. Recovered twelve-steppers with real spiritual depth. Anyone who’s done long retreats and come back kinder. Trust conduct over vocabulary — the fluent ones are often the emptiest.

Visit before you buy anything. Twin Oaks, East Wind, and Acorn take visitors. So do the European ecovillages. So, for the serious, do the Zapatistas. You won’t know what level of togetherness you can tolerate until you’ve lived inside several versions. Everyone wants the village until the village is in their kitchen.

Inner work, practice group, visits. Everything else — land, money, membership, ceremony — comes after, and comes easier.

The close
I won’t end this warmly.

The window is closing. The search line stood up because millions of nervous systems can feel it closing, even the ones that couldn’t tell you what “it” is. Most of the people reading this will do nothing, and most of the ones who do something will do it in the wrong order — land first, vision on a napkin, jealousy unprocessed, deed in one name — and add their wreckage to sixty years of identical wreckage, and the wreckage will be blamed, as always, on human nature rather than on skipped work.

A few of you will do the work. You’ll spend the unglamorous year in the living room before the beautiful year on the land. You’ll process the conflict before you sign the mortgage. You’ll write the exit terms and the dissolution terms while everyone still loves each other. You’ll gate the medicine behind the healing, keep jealousy off the throne, hold your founders to the four questions, and raise children who leave at eighteen with your blessing and come back — if they come back — because nothing out there was as alive as home.

I have no application form for you. There’s no retreat to buy. The manuals are free, the watch list is below, and the door of this work opens from the inside only.

As for me — I’m in Senegal, and I’ll be buying land soon. I’m not founding anything; I’ve just noticed that community is almost inescapable, and people tend to arrive wherever the work is real. Residency here costs $200.

If you build something real, we’ll hear about each other.

Watch these, then read these
Films:

Where’s Utopia? (1988) — my father’s film, the first of its kind, made with Jerry Mintz; online now for the first time. [Link to your Substack post.]

American Commune (2013) — The Farm through the eyes of two sisters raised there. The children’s audit, on film. If you watch only one, watch this.

Commune (2006) — Black Bear Ranch: what happens after the escape, when the bodies, the jealousy, and the children are still there. Freedom without relational architecture becoming an unacknowledged hierarchy of the bold.

A New We — a tour of European ecovillages; the antidote to thinking “commune” is one thing.

Within Reach — a couple bicycles 6,500 miles through a hundred communities; watch it to learn that choosing a community is itself an apprenticeship.

Seeking the Good Life in America — Twin Oaks, Acorn, and Light Morning compared; different communities solve different longings, and no community should try to satisfy them all.

Voices of Cohousing — the partial-community option: high neighborliness, moderate obligation, preserved autonomy. Less radical, far more robust; for many people, the honest right answer.

Inside the Bruderhof — high-commitment religious communalism working, and the autonomy price it charges. Don’t copy its sacrifices without its legitimacy.

Living in the Future / Lammas — community as land-use battle with the state; your ethics don’t exempt you from zoning.

A Place Called Chiapas (1998) — the Zapatistas in their first years.

Books and papers, in reading order: Diana Leafe Christian, Creating a Life Together (start here — the sequencing manual). Yana Ludwig, Building Belonging. Then choose a governance path: Rau & Koch-Gonzalez, Many Voices One Song for sociocracy, or Schaub et al., Building United Judgment for consensus. Ludwig & Gimnig, The Cooperative Culture Handbook for the skill stack. Then the deeper layer: Kanter, Commitment and Community; Sosis & Bressler on commune longevity; Rubin, Willis & Ludwig on why longevity isn’t success; Ostrom’s core design principles; and Jo Freeman’s The Tyranny of Structurelessness — the short, brutal essay every “we don’t believe in hierarchy” group should read twice. For the children: Hrdy, Mothers and Others. For the Zapatistas: Gloria Muñoz Ramírez, The Fire and the Word.
