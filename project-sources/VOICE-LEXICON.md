# Joel Voice Lexicon — Catchphrases, Idiolect, and Spoken-First Devices

This is a controlled lexicon, not a phrase bank. Joel's exact phrases may be preserved when he supplies them or used when the present context genuinely calls for them. They must never be sprinkled merely to simulate voice.

## Exact approved phrases

| Phrase | Function | Best register/context | Text rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **“Lovey bunnies DON'T know!”** | Inner-child voice: frustration at ignorance softened by affection rather than annoyance | Playful/tender or light polemical aside after a concrete example | Preserve the exact capitalization and exclamation. Rare. Do not use in evidence-dense, child-safety, legal, or acute-risk prose. |
| **“Can you believe it?”** | Childlike incredulity and shared amusement | After a genuinely surprising or absurd detail | The humor partly depends on vocal intonation. In text, use sparingly and only where the preceding detail carries the tone. It may work as a short standalone sentence. |
| **“my lovey bunnies” / “Hey Lovey Bunny 🐰”** | Affectionate direct address and emotional contact | Inner-child, tender, playful, or personally addressed guidance | Use only when the relationship and article world support it. Never treat it as a universal byline signature. |

## Recurring expressive moves — not mandatory catchphrases

These appear naturally in the corpus. They are useful evidence of cadence, but deliberate sprinkling will sound synthetic.

- **“Keep in mind…”** — practical caution and reader guidance; frequent in guides.
- **“Isn't that interesting?” / “Isn't that crazy?”** — spoken wonder or incredulity; rarer in finished prose.
- **“Good Lord.”** — brief self-implicating pressure release beside serious material.
- **“Anyway…”** — informal pivot after a digression; useful only when there was an actual digression.
- **“Remember:”** — mnemonic direct address in practical or spiritual explanation.
- **“This world is funny.”** — amused philosophical resignation; more legacy- or context-specific than a current default.
- **“What on earth…?”** — comic incredulity; context-specific.

## Grammatical-person provenance

Do not convert a general claim, recommendation, mechanism, or reader-facing explanation into autobiographical first person merely to make prose sound natural or human. `I`, `my`, `for me`, `I do`, `I feel`, and similar constructions assert something about Joel's own life or experience unless the context clearly marks them as authorial judgment rather than autobiography.

Use first-person experience only when the current source actually attributes that experience to Joel, Joel supplies it in the current task, or Joel explicitly approves the attribution. If a rewrite would newly make a claim autobiographical and the source does not resolve that, ask before assigning it to Joel. Preserve grammatical person as part of provenance, not as a style variable.

First-person authorial judgment can still be legitimate when the judgment itself is sourced to Joel, but do not use `I want...`, `what matters to me...`, `my reason for...`, or similar preference/significance staging as a cheap way to introduce a general point. State the substantive point directly unless Joel's personal preference is itself the subject.

## AI-frequency-risk architectural and mechanical language

Models overuse architectural and mechanical abstractions as generic connective tissue: `leverage`, `move`, `job`, `load`, `load-bearing`, `framework`, `scaffold`, `mechanism`, and related language can make ordinary human material sound engineered even when each word is technically defensible.

These are not absolute bans. Use them when they are literally the right technical term or when Joel himself uses them for the thing being described. Otherwise prefer the concrete relation, action, consequence, or ordinary verb instead of turning thought into an engineered system. In particular, do not use architectural/mechanical wording merely to make transitions, describe what a paragraph is doing, label an idea's role, or make a rewrite sound analytically precise.

Also watch the larger rhythm around such language. Replacing `framework` with a plainer synonym does not humanize prose if the passage still advances in predictable model units such as setup → qualification → tidy conclusion, balanced true/false clauses, equalized examples, or repeated miniature taxonomies.

## Legacy or narrow-context phrases

Do not revive automatically: “Nobody, aka Joel,” “I double dare you,” “Are you ready?”, spiritual greetings such as “Sukhino bhava,” or old Blogspot-era intensity. They may be appropriate only when Joel explicitly restores them or the current piece clearly belongs to that register.

## Catchphrase protocol

1. Record exact spelling, punctuation, capitalization, emotional function, register, and whether the phrase is spoken-first.
2. Distinguish a real catchphrase from a frequent connector.
3. Preserve a user-supplied catchphrase during editing unless Joel authorizes replacement.
4. Do not paraphrase a coined or catch phrase into a smoother substitute.
5. Use the current article's own material first. A catchphrase should sharpen or release something already present.
6. Audit density across the full piece. One earned appearance can feel intimate; several strategic appearances can feel branded.
7. When intonation carries most of the humor, flag text use as uncertain rather than forcing it.
