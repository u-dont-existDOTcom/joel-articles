# Introduction

Trauma can be over in the outside world and still be happening in the body. I can know perfectly well that I am safe while some other layer of me is bracing, scanning, going numb, collapsing, freezing, or getting suddenly flooded. That leftover bodily reality is what I mean here when I talk about somatic work.

With complex or developmental trauma, I am not especially interested in proving my courage by starting with the deepest memory. The questions I care about first are simpler. When something comes up, can I stay here? Can I stop if I want to? When it is over, am I more or less myself? If the honest answer is no, overwhelming myself does not become deep processing just because I call it that.

What I need depends on what is actually happening. Sometimes I need regulation. Sometimes there is old survival activation still running through my body, and reasoning with it is not going to convince a body that thinks the danger is still here. And sometimes I really am stable enough to go straight into EMDR or another focused trauma method. I don't think of those as higher and lower rungs of one ladder. They solve different problems.

This matters especially in the way I use [inner-child therapy](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/). I can understand exactly what a younger part needs and still be unable to reach it because everything in me is clenched around the trauma. There are times when my body has to become a slightly easier place to live before inner-child work stops feeling like the child-state is swallowing me whole.

## Your Physical State Can Change What Therapy Does

I don't separate “therapy” from the physical nervous system that has to do the therapy. A session happens inside whatever state I am actually in that day: how I slept, whether I am in pain, substances or medication, nutrition, fear learning, inflammation, dissociation, glutamate/GABA balance, autonomic tone, and my general physiological stability. The same therapy can land very differently depending on that ground.

I take this seriously partly because I have watched friends get almost nothing out of these therapies until what I understood as physical neuroinflammation was addressed with things like low-dose naltrexone, phosphatidylserine, DMSO, and so on. That is me describing the sequence I observed in my friends. It is not me claiming that I established the mechanism of what happened to them.

My own version is considerably less neat. If I feel like I have toxins in my gut, I can't do any real healing work. Ozonated zeolite helps me a lot with that. Ozonated charcoal water also helps me a lot when I feel surrounded by chaotic energy fields, whether I understand those as EMF or auric.

I think of this whole layer as neurological de-armoring. It is not another trauma exercise. It is the condition of the ground I am asking the trauma exercise to work on. I have separate guides on [sleep](https://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/) and [neurological de-armoring](http://dearmor.u-dont-exist.com/) for exactly that reason: sometimes the ground is the problem.

# Where I Would Start

I don't have a favorite sequence that everybody needs to follow. If one event is plainly the problem and I can touch it without disappearing into it, I may go directly to EMDR. If touching trauma makes me flood, vanish, or lose the rest of the day to the aftermath, I am not going to insist on doing the hottest possible thing first.

But there is an opposite trap in gentler work. “Building capacity” can quietly turn into an avoidance project where I learn to regulate beautifully and nobody ever treats the thing that actually hurts. I want to know what is happening away from the therapy too. Am I sleeping? Functioning? Relating better? Becoming freer? Sometimes sleep itself is the bottleneck, which is one reason I made a separate [sleep guide](http://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/).

The method name alone also tells me surprisingly little. Intensity matters, and so does what I am actually doing with the method. Present-focused self-hypnosis is not the same thing as immersive memory work. A little shaking is not forced catharsis. Brainspotting can be used to orient and resource, or it can take me straight into much hotter material. Same label. Very different experience.

# Somatic Work and Inner-Child Reparenting

One reason I care about gentler body work is that it can make reparenting possible in the first place. Regulation is not the grand finale, but neither does relaxation somehow solve every problem between me and the child.

Sometimes access to love is not even the issue. The love itself feels unsafe. The inner child is looking at the grown-up self and basically saying, “Yeah, that doesn't look like things went well for us.” Meanwhile the grown-up can start resenting the younger version for not doing whatever they were supposedly meant to do. Now I have an internal war: the older version is making vows about protection and nurturing while the child is sitting there thinking, big fuckity whoopty doo, what are you going to do for me?

And the child has a point.

The Protector is the part that creates safety in the actual world. It says no. It leaves the situation. It finishes the thing I keep avoiding, gets sleep, cleans the room, keeps one small promise. One real action can communicate more safety than another speech inside my head.

The problem is that when I am completely flooded, I am basically the child-state. Those adult capacities may not be available yet. In my inner-child work I call them the Nurturer and Protector, but I do not need a fully functioning inner adult before I can begin. A neutral witness is already something. I can notice the room, notice my body, notice the younger state. The fact that something can notice is enough adult presence for a first step.

Sometimes I borrow more than that. I can take one sentence, one boundary, or one direction from somebody safe and carry that one piece myself. That is what I mean by borrowed adulthood.

Gentle [self-hypnosis](http://hypnosis.u-dont-exist.com/) can support regulation, dialogue, reparenting, clarity, and present safety. What I do not mean is using self-hypnosis to force memories, establish what supposedly happened, or drag myself deep into child dialogue when I cannot stay present.

At other times the most immediate obstruction is simply tension in the body. I feel this especially around the abdomen and solar plexus. EFT, Somatic Experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, gentle shaking or qigong, a longer exhale that stays passive and unforced, and gentle Buteyko-style breathing have all helped me soften that area. A little mantra or meditation can help too. I am talking about something quite different from an intensive retreat or deep hypnosis. Sometimes the body work comes first; sometimes it keeps running alongside the inner-child work.

The heart-to-solar-plexus practice is important enough to me that I do not treat it like an optional flourish. I put one hand at the center of my chest, the heart chakra, and use that to connect with love. The other goes on the solar plexus just under the chest, where I feel the vulnerable inner child. The solar plexus really is vulnerable—one slap there hurts quite a lot—so it makes sense to me that the vulnerable child would be felt there.

If I cannot simply produce love on command, I use somebody real. A pet, a baby, a friend, a partner, anyone I actually love. I connect with that feeling, send some of it from the heart toward the inner child, receive love back from the child, and send it into the heart again. An infinite beautiful loop!

Brainspotting can enter the reparenting work too, but dose matters. Using a gaze position as a resource, or entering carefully, is very different from opening highly charged developmental material. With diffuse developmental or relational trauma, I tend to wait on EMDR until at least some Nurturer and Protector are available to hold what gets opened. I would never make that a universal law. Somebody stable who has one discrete event may be ready much earlier.

Deep memory work, immersive child dialogue, deep hypnosis, suggestive exploration: those are things I save for a time when staying present, stopping voluntarily, and coming back are abilities I actually have.

# Building Enough Safety to Stay Present

## Somatic Experiencing

[Somatic Experiencing](http://traumahealing.org/) is one of the things I reach for when even approaching the material sends my body over the edge. I can work with sensation, orientation, impulse, defensive response, and pacing without needing the whole story first.

Titration and pendulation make intuitive sense to me because there is no trophy for beating trauma in a fight. I touch the amount of activation I can honestly tolerate, see what happens, and return toward something steadier. Some people spend three to six months building that kind of capacity. Severe developmental trauma can take longer. I treat those time frames as orientation, not a countdown timer, and I see no reason for somebody who is already stable and has a clear target to sit through arbitrary months of preparation before adding focused trauma work.

## Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

When I say yoga here, I am not talking about power yoga, hot yoga, or somebody declaring, “Open your hips and release your trauma.” I mean slow, choice-based practice: trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, possibly very gentle yin. With yin, I want props, shorter holds, and a very easy way out.

The language should leave choice in my hands:

* “You might try…”
* “You can keep your eyes open.”
* “Come out of the shape whenever you want.”
* “Is this useful, neutral, or too much?”
* “There can be several right versions. Pick the one that fits your body.”

I am not recommending intense stretching, breath retention, forced breathwork, aggressive hip opening, long passive holds, hands-on adjustments without clear consent, or pushing through because somebody has decided suffering must be therapeutic. Once I cannot choose anymore, it is no longer the practice I am talking about.

## Gentle Shaking / TRE

Here I mean a little shaking, not an attempt to blast trauma out of my body.

Basic [TRE](https://traumaprevention.com/frequently-asked-questions/), very light neurogenic tremoring, gentle standing shakes, or even a short spell of playful movement may be enough. The line for me is fairly simple: if it starts feeling compulsive, frightening, dissociative, or hard to stop, it has stopped being what I mean by gentle.

There is one very unglamorous safety point worth making. If the tremor reaches your neck while you are lying down, do not let an unsupported head whip rapidly from side to side. # What I Use Between Deeper Sessions

A wonderful therapy session is not worth that much if I spend the next week rebuilding exactly the same pressure. I want at least one practice that still works when ordinary life has resumed.

## EFT / Tapping

EFT is very nice for regulation. Part of what I like is that it is a distraction: my hands and my attention are doing something besides riding the reaction all the way down.

I think of the different tapping points as activating different parts of the brain. That is my own thought, not a neuroscience result I am claiming to have established. I move from point to point and pay attention to how each one feels. There is also the simple fact that I am giving myself a little massage. Actually, shaving my head and massaging it works extremely well for me—possibly even better than EFT.

EFT is useful because I can do it almost anywhere. Before a difficult conversation. Right after somebody triggers me. In the middle of a mental loop after my body has joined the argument. It can take some of the pressure off without pretending the deeper trauma has therefore disappeared.

## [Shaking Qigong](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) / Shaking Medicine

My friend Louka is the reason I link this class. He had already tried many healing and somatic practices, including basic TRE. TRE did nothing for him. In fact, not much helped until he got into this particular class, which is $10 a month on Skool.

It is much broader than TRE. There are all kinds of movements and positions, and I think that may matter for some people. They may need a particular position or movement together with the tremor before they finally locate where the blockage is.

When I say blockage, I mean energy that is stuck in you. I often get the more natural kind of shaking animals seem to have rather than noticing much from deliberate therapeutic techniques. Psychedelics make this especially obvious for me. It can feel as though my body knows exactly which area needs to shake and exactly how to do it. That is why I do not reduce the whole idea to “shaking = mental health.” When the right thing is shaken in the right way, things loosen the way they should and the energy flows.

TRE is a system designed to be taught en masse, so of course it has to be distilled into something that seems to work reasonably often for reasonably many people. My suspicion is that some people have a better chance of finding the stuck place if they can approach it from more angles. “Just try lots of stuff till it works” is how my expertise runs on this—and also how you get less chance of being called an expert, lol.

I did not personally try the Shaking Qigong class. My firsthand experience is with qigong, and qigong has helped me a great deal with moving energy. So the combination makes intuitive sense to me. Guided shaking plus qigong also seems like an interesting middle ground between a highly predictable TRE structure and completely unstructured shaking.

There is a social piece as well. Plenty of people do better when they can see other people getting results instead of doing every part of the practice alone.

This is not the same dose as the tiny exploratory shaking I described earlier. I could imagine something like 10–45 minutes as a regular practice for chronic freeze, non-verbal emotional processing, stress accumulating between therapy sessions, or activation still hanging around after deeper work. That range did not come from the linked class. Whatever amount I use, the important boundary remains the same: I need to be able to stop and settle afterward.

As for what actually deserves the credit, I don't know. Movement may let muscles and unfinished defensive impulses finally do something they never completed. Maybe moving while closely attending to the body changes arousal. Qigong would say that stuck qi moved. Those are different explanations, and I do not need to make one explanation disprove all the others.

What makes me stop trusting the practice is when it becomes a catharsis contest, or when people start presenting it as a complete treatment for severe PTSD. For somebody who is highly dissociative or unstable, experimentation does not replace skilled help.

## A Discharge → Settle Sequence

Sometimes I do not want shaking to end with the system still revved up. I want a discharge that actually arrives somewhere. One sequence I like is:

1. [**Shaking Qigong**](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) **/ TRE** — let some of the activation move.
2. **Restorative or gentle yin yoga** — soften, settle, rebalance energy flow.
3. **EFT tapping** — bring emotional calm and cognitive orientation back if activation is still high.

The same yin rules apply here: props, choice, and no endurance contest. Long passive holds can become much more evocative than they look, so especially in the beginning I prefer shorter holds over heroic five-minute shapes. Yoga can also make sense before shaking, or after deeper processing. This is simply a sequence I use. It is not a law.

# When the Body Knows More Than the Story

Not every trauma arrives as a clean memory with a beginning, middle, and end. Sometimes what I have is a reaction in the body, an image, an impulse, an emotion, a fragment—something happens, but no coherent explanation comes attached. People sometimes call this *subcortical* processing. All I mean by that here is that the material showed up before the story did. I am not claiming that trauma lives in one precisely identified brain region.

## Brainspotting

My own entry into Brainspotting was very simple. I used my finger to guide my gaze slowly around my visual field and paid attention to what changed. How did a memory feel at one angle versus another? Did different memories appear in different places? It felt intuitive enough that I quickly figured out I could experiment with a basic version myself.

In my system, looking up-left often feels more like remembering, while up-right often feels more like constructing or thinking. NLP turned patterns like that into a general rule. Controlled research did not support the fixed rule, so I do not watch somebody else's eyes and imagine I can infer what their mind is doing. I keep using the pattern because it is consistent for me. There is a broader real relationship between gaze, eye movement, and memory retrieval, but that is not the same thing as claiming a universal fixed map.

What I especially like is the edging quality of it. I can approach the uncomfortable place, barely touch the boundary, and move away again instead of throwing myself into the deepest part. Maybe edging is not technically the right word. In somatic language, what I am doing is close to titration and pendulation. For me it feels both better and more effective than hammering distress until something gives.

Brainspotting also gets a lot of points from me for the same reason EFT does: it is democratic. I do not need an expensive expert every time I want to try a simple version. It is available in the moment. A formal session can of course add therapist attunement, focused attention, and body awareness around the gaze position.

Being able to experiment on my own does not mean I would casually go hunting through my deepest trauma by myself. If I cannot tell that I am becoming overwhelmed, cannot remain present, or cannot stop when I need to, that is the limit.

I do not necessarily need a perfectly defined target before something starts moving.

That is also why it fits naturally with inner-child reparenting for me. Something younger may first show itself through the body rather than through a tidy memory, and then the Nurturer or Protector can enter the work when it appears. # When I Can Point to the Memory

## EMDR

EMDR feels more like: there it is. That is the memory. The target is clearer, and the recall procedure is clearer. Brainspotting is easier for me to feel my way into when I do not know exactly what I am looking for. That is the main distinction I make between them.

An accident, an assault memory, military trauma, a distinct flashback, or a specific trigger network is the kind of thing that naturally makes me think of EMDR. It can be highly effective. If somebody is stable and there is one clearly identifiable event, I see no reason to make them complete months of preliminary body work just to satisfy a tidy sequence.

I become more cautious when what I am dealing with is diffuse or developmental, mainly bodily or pre-verbal, relational, or happening inside a nervous system that is still quite unstable. In those cases I want at least some access to a Nurturer and Protector before opening very charged material, and Brainspotting may fit better earlier.

After EMDR, I like the rest of the day to become almost comically uneventful. Walk. Eat. Hydrate. Sleep early if I am tired. Restorative yoga if it feels good. Journal only if journaling actually grounds me. Maybe a little EFT if activation is still hanging around. I would not casually pile Brainspotting, EMDR, intense shaking, and deep yin into the same day unless I already knew my system could handle that much and I had skilled support.

Sometimes the most useful thing I can do after processing is stop processing.

# Making Sense of What Changed

## Light CBT / Narrative Integration

Talking to a belief becomes much more useful to me once my body has stopped screaming, “this is happening now.” Before that, I can win the argument intellectually and still lose it physiologically.

Once there is more room, I can revisit old beliefs, make the story of my life more coherent, separate past danger from present reality, clarify values and boundaries, and recover some agency. From there it can feed directly into [inner-child reparenting](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/). The adult is easier to access. I can distinguish the adult from the child-states with less confusion. Forgiveness—where forgiveness is actually relevant—and questions about life direction become less abstract too.

# How I Know Whether It Actually Helped

My outcome measure is very boring: later, does it still hit as hard?

When I use the word *restimulation*, I mean what happens when I remember the event again. Does the same charge rush back into my mind and body, or has something actually changed?

While I was working on this article, I had a convenient real-life example. The day before, I had been washing all of my housemate's moldy clothes for her. One of her dresses got discolored and she yelled at me. I was still stewing about the way she treated me.

So I tried a quick bit of self-Brainspotting. While thinking about what happened, I slowly moved my finger and eyes around my visual field. When my gaze moved farther to either side, deeper into peripheral vision, I noticed blissful tingles. There is a popular simple claim that peripheral vision activates the parasympathetic nervous system. I do not treat what I felt as proof of that; the actual research is more complicated.

What I could do subjectively was move my gaze between a position where I felt settled and noticed those blissful tingles and another position where I felt the distress. Moment by moment, I could kind of move the bliss into the distress while continuing to think about what happened. When I finished, the event seemed to contain less negative energy, or less blocking energy, in my mind.

But that only told me what had happened right then.

The version of me that matters more is the one an hour later, not the one enjoying the final minute of the exercise. If I am still stewing just as intensely, maybe the effect was brief and I should try again. If I can bring the incident to mind and there is less restimulation, that looks more durable. I do not expect a single treatment to erase everything. One EMDR session is often not enough either.

Larger interventions also leave a wake, and I may not understand that wake until the next day. What happened to my sleep? Am I more irritable? More dissociated? In more pain? Do I suddenly feel compelled to keep processing? How am I treating other people? Ten peaceful minutes at the end of an exercise do not prove the dose was right.

Research matters to me. I just do not use the size of the literature as a ranking algorithm for my nervous system.

A shaking, qigong, breathwork, or somatic community may say that a practice released decades of trauma, awakened kundalini, cleared stored emotion, or rapidly healed the entire nervous system. My own result cannot establish all of those explanations. What I can do is pay attention to people whose patterns resemble mine, change one thing at a time when I can, and keep coming back to the least glamorous question of all: am I actually doing better?

# Sky Hypnosis and Vagal Blitz

[Sky Hypnosis](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/try-this-10-second-sky-hypnosis-bodymind) and [Vagal Blitz](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/vagal-blitz-breathwork-gives-best) are different animals from most of what I have been describing. They can shift state hard. I leave them outside the main route because neither is a prerequisite for healing, although somebody stable may still find them useful much earlier.

Sky Hypnosis can make me lightheaded. Vagal Blitz is for lying down only and has cardiovascular and mental-health cautions. I use the complete guide, begin with the least intense version that creates a useful change, and stop when balance, orientation, or recovery starts getting unreliable. Fainting and loss of consciousness are not goals.

I also refuse to count “wow” as an outcome. A huge release can feel profound. So can bliss. So can an altered state. None of that guarantees that the actual problem moved at all.

The question comes later. Am I any better at pain, boundaries, conflict, or ordinary life? And if the extraordinary practice mostly gives me a spectacular way to dodge grief, conflict, practical problems, or deeper relational healing, then the fact that it is intense does not save it.
