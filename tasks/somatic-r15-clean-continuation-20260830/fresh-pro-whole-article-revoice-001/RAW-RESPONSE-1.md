# Introduction

Trauma can be over in real life and still be happening in the body. I can know I'm safe while my body keeps bracing, scanning, going numb, collapsing, freezing, or suddenly flooding. That leftover bodily reality is what I mean by somatic here.

With complex or developmental trauma, I'm not eager to start with the deepest memory. First I want to know: can I stay here when something comes up? Can I stop? Am I more or less myself afterward? If the honest answer is no, overwhelming myself doesn't turn into deep processing just because I call it that.

Sometimes what I need is regulation. Sometimes old survival activation needs to move, and I'm not going to reason my way out of a body that still believes the danger is present. Other times I really am stable enough to go straight into EMDR or another focused trauma method. I don't think of these as rungs on a ladder. They answer different problems.

This matters especially in [inner-child therapy](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/). I may understand perfectly well what a younger part needs and still be unable to reach it because everything in me is clenched around the trauma. Sometimes the body has to become a slightly easier place to live before inner-child work stops feeling as though I'm being swallowed by the child-state.

## Your Physical State Can Change What Therapy Does

I don't put “therapy” on one side and the physical state of the nervous system doing the therapy on the other. Sleep, pain, substances or medication, nutrition, fear learning, inflammation, dissociation, glutamate/GABA balance, autonomic tone, and how physiologically stable I am that day can all change how the same session lands.

This matters to me because I've watched friends get almost nothing from these therapies until what I understood as physical neuroinflammation was addressed with things such as low-dose naltrexone, phosphatidylserine, DMSO, and so on. That's what I observed in my friends. I'm not claiming that I established the mechanism of their treatment.

My own version is messier. I can't do any real healing work when I feel like I have toxins in my gut. Ozonated zeolite helps me a lot with that. Ozonated charcoal water also helps me a lot when I feel surrounded by chaotic energy fields, whether I interpret them as EMF or auric.

I call this neurological de-armoring. It isn't one more trauma exercise. It's the ground I am asking the exercise to work on. I wrote separate guides on [sleep](https://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/) and [neurological de-armoring](http://dearmor.u-dont-exist.com/) for times when the ground itself is the problem.

# Where I Would Start

I don't have a preferred ladder. When one event is plainly the problem and I can touch it without disappearing, I may go straight to EMDR. If touching trauma makes me flood or vanish or spend the rest of the day wrecked, I'm not going to force myself into the hottest thing first.

The gentle side has its own trap. “Building capacity” can turn into an avoidance project in which I regulate forever because nobody wants to treat the thing that actually hurts. Away from the therapy, I ask what's changing in life. Am I sleeping? Functioning? Relating better? Getting freer? Sometimes sleep is the entire bottleneck, which is why I have a separate [sleep guide](http://sleep.u-dont-exist.com/).

A method's name tells me less than its intensity and how I'm using it. Present-focused self-hypnosis isn't immersive memory work. A little shaking isn't forced catharsis. Brainspotting can help me orient and resource, or it can carry me into much hotter material. The label may be the same while the experience is completely different.

# Somatic Work and Inner-Child Reparenting

Gentler body work matters to me because it can make reparenting possible. Regulation isn't the grand finale, and relaxation doesn't magically solve every problem between me and the child.

Sometimes the problem isn't that I can't reach love. The love itself seems unsafe. The inner child looks at the grown-up self and basically says, “Yeah, that doesn't look like things went well for us.” Meanwhile, the grown-up can become resentful toward the younger version for not doing what they were supposedly meant to do. Now there's an internal war: the older version is making vows about protection and nurturing, while the child is thinking, big fuckity whoopty doo, what are you going to do for me?

That child has a point. The Protector creates real-world safety. It says no, leaves the situation, finishes the avoided task, gets some sleep, cleans the room, and keeps a small promise. One real action can say more than another internal speech about safety.

When I'm completely flooded, though, I'm basically the child-state, and those adult functions may not be available yet. In my inner-child work I call them the Nurturer and Protector. A neutral witness still counts. I can notice the room, the body, and the younger state; the fact that something can notice is enough adult presence for a first step. I can also borrow one sentence, boundary, or direction from somebody safe and carry one small piece of it myself. That's what I mean by borrowed adulthood.

Gentle [self-hypnosis](http://hypnosis.u-dont-exist.com/) can support regulation, dialogue, reparenting, clarity, and present safety. I don't mean using it to force memories, prove what happened, or pull myself deep into child dialogue when I can't stay here.

Sometimes body tension is the most immediate blockage. I notice it especially around the abdomen or solar plexus. EFT, Somatic Experiencing, trauma-sensitive yoga, gentle shaking or qigong, a longer exhale that remains passive and unforced, and gentle Buteyko-style breathing have all helped me soften there. A little mantra or meditation can help too. I'm not talking about an intensive retreat or deep hypnosis. The body work can come before inner-child work, or it can continue alongside it.

The heart-to-solar-plexus practice isn't an afterthought for me. I put one hand on the center of the chest, the heart chakra, to connect with love. The other goes on the solar plexus just under the chest, where I feel the vulnerable inner child. The solar plexus really is vulnerable—one slap there hurts quite a lot—so it makes sense to me that I would feel the vulnerable child there.

When love is hard to summon on command, I think of somebody real: a pet, a baby, a friend, a partner, anyone I genuinely love. I send some of that love from the heart to the inner child, receive love back from the child, and send it back to the heart. An infinite beautiful loop!

Brainspotting can join the reparenting too. Using it as a resource or entering carefully is nothing like using it to go straight into highly charged developmental material. With diffuse developmental or relational trauma, I tend to wait on EMDR until at least some Nurturer and Protector are available to hold whatever gets opened. I wouldn't make that a universal rule. A stable person with one discrete event may be ready much sooner.

Deep memory work, immersive child dialogue, deep hypnosis, suggestive exploration: I save those for a time when staying present, stopping voluntarily, and coming back are things I can actually do.

# Building Enough Safety to Stay Present

## Somatic Experiencing

I reach for [Somatic Experiencing](http://traumahealing.org/) when simply approaching the material sends my body over the edge. There is sensation, orientation, impulse, defensive response, and pacing to work with. I don't have to begin with the whole story.

Titration and pendulation make sense to me because there's no prize for beating trauma in a fight. I touch only as much activation as I can honestly tolerate, notice what happens, and move back toward something steadier. Some people spend three to six months building that capacity. Severe developmental trauma can take longer. I use that as orientation, not a countdown, and I see no reason for someone who is already stable and has a clear target to wait through arbitrary months before adding focused trauma work.

## Trauma-Sensitive / Restorative Yoga

When I say yoga here, I don't mean power yoga, hot yoga, or somebody declaring, “Open your hips and release your trauma.” I mean slow and choice-based: trauma-sensitive yoga, restorative yoga, gentle somatic yoga, and perhaps very gentle yin. With yin, I want props, shorter holds, and an easy way out.

The instructions should sound more like:

* “You might try…”
* “You can keep your eyes open.”
* “Come out of the shape whenever you want.”
* “Is this useful, neutral, or too much?”
* “There can be several right versions. Pick the one that fits your body.”

Intense stretching, breath retention, forced breathwork, aggressive hip opening, long passive holds, hands-on adjustments without clear consent, or pushing through because suffering is supposedly therapeutic—I'm not recommending any of that. Once I can't choose, it isn't the practice I mean.

## Gentle Shaking / TRE

Here I mean a little shaking. Not an attempt to blast trauma out of my body.

Basic [TRE](https://traumaprevention.com/frequently-asked-questions/), very light neurogenic tremoring, gentle shaking while standing, or even a brief bit of playful movement may be enough. Once it feels compulsive, frightening, dissociative, or hard to stop, it isn't what I mean by gentle.

# What I Use Between Deeper Sessions

A wonderful therapy session isn't worth much if I spend the next week rebuilding all the pressure. I need at least one thing that can still help while ordinary life is happening.

## EFT / Tapping

EFT is very nice for regulation. It's a distraction, and it gives my hands and attention something to do besides riding the reaction all the way down.

I think of the different tapping points as activating different parts of the brain. That's my own thought, not a neuroscience finding I am claiming to have proved. I move through the points and notice how each one feels. It's also a little massage. Actually, shaving my head and massaging it works really well for me—possibly even better than EFT.

I can use EFT almost anywhere: before a hard conversation, right after somebody triggers me, or when my mind is looping and my body has joined in. It takes some of the pressure off. The deeper trauma can still be sitting there.

## [Shaking Qigong](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) / Shaking Medicine

My friend Louka is the reason I link this class. He had tried many healing and somatic practices, including basic TRE. TRE did nothing for him. Nothing helped much until he got into this class, which costs $10 a month on Skool.

The class goes far beyond TRE. It includes all kinds of movements and positions, and I think that may matter because some people need the right movement or position together with the tremor before they can find where the blockage is.

By blockage, I mean energy that is stuck in you. I often have the natural kind of shaking animals have, while intentional therapeutic techniques don't seem to do much for me. Especially on psychedelics, it can feel as though my body knows exactly which part needs to shake and exactly how. It isn't simply “shaking = mental health.” When the right thing is shaken in the right way, things loosen as they should and the energy flows.

TRE is a system meant to be taught en masse. Of course it had to be distilled into something that seems to work most of the time for most people. I suspect some people would have a better chance if they could approach the stuck place from more angles. “Just try lots of stuff till it works” is how my expertise runs on this—and also how you get less chance of being called an expert, lol.

I didn't try the Shaking Qigong class myself. My firsthand experience is with qigong, and qigong has helped me a great deal with moving energy. So I can understand why the two would naturally go together. Guided shaking plus qigong also seems like a middle ground between the fully predictable structure of TRE and completely unstructured shaking.

It is social too. Many people do better when they can see other people getting results rather than doing the entire practice alone.

This is a different dose from the tiny exploratory shaking I described above. I could imagine 10–45 minutes as a regular practice for chronic freeze, non-verbal emotional processing, stress that builds between therapy sessions, or activation left over after deeper work. I didn't learn that range from the linked class. Whatever dose I use, I still need to be able to stop and settle afterward.

What deserves the credit? I don't know. Maybe movement lets muscles and defensive impulses do something they never finished. Maybe it changes arousal because I'm moving and paying close attention to the body. Qigong would say that stuck qi moved. Those are different explanations, and I don't need to force one of them to prove the others.

I stop trusting the practice when it turns into a catharsis competition or becomes a supposed complete treatment for severe PTSD. For somebody who is highly dissociative or unstable, experimentation isn't a substitute for skilled help.

## A Discharge → Settle Sequence

Sometimes I want shaking to end in settling rather than leaving the system revved up. One combination I like is:

1. [**Shaking Qigong**](http://shakingclass.innersignalselfhypnosis.com/) **/ TRE** — let some of the activation move.
2. **Restorative or gentle yin yoga** — soften, settle, rebalance energy flow.
3. **EFT tapping** — bring emotional calm and cognitive orientation back if activation is still high.

The same yin rules apply: props, choice, and no endurance contest. Long passive holds can become surprisingly evocative, so especially at first I prefer shorter holds over ambitious five-minute shapes. Yoga can also make sense before shaking, or after deeper processing. This is a sequence I use, not a law.

# When the Body Knows More Than the Story

Sometimes there is no clean memory to point to. There may only be a body reaction, an image, an impulse, an emotion, or a fragment without a coherent explanation attached. People call this *subcortical* processing. I only mean that the material arrived before the story. I'm not claiming that one precise brain region contains the trauma.

## Brainspotting

I used my finger to direct my gaze slowly around my field of vision. I wanted to see how different memories felt, or what memories came up, at different angles. It was intuitive, and I figured out fairly quickly that I could do a simple version on my own.

In my own system, looking up-left often feels more like remembering, while up-right feels more like constructing or thinking. People have made that into a general NLP rule, and the rule did not survive controlled research, so I don't watch somebody else's eyes and pretend I know what they're doing. I use the pattern because it stays consistent for me. There is a broader, real relationship among gaze, eye movement, and memory retrieval, but that isn't the same claim as the fixed map.

What I like is the edging. I move toward the uncomfortable place, feather-touch its boundary, and then come back out instead of throwing myself into the deep end. Maybe edging isn't the right word. In somatic language, it is close to titration and pendulation. For me, it feels both better and more effective than hammering at the distress.

Brainspotting also gets a lot of points from me for being democratic, like EFT. I don't need an expensive expert just to experiment with a simple version, and it's available at any moment. A formal session can still bring therapist attunement, focused attention, and body awareness around the gaze position.

I don't necessarily need to know exactly what I am targeting before the session begins to move. # When I Can Point to the Memory

## EMDR

EMDR is more like: there it is; that is the memory. The recall procedure is clearer, and so is the target. Brainspotting is easier for me to feel my way into when I don't know exactly what I am looking for. That is the main distinction in my mind.

An accident, an assault memory, military trauma, a distinct flashback, or a specific trigger network is what I naturally think of for EMDR. It can be highly effective. When somebody is stable and there is one clear event, I'm not going to make them complete months of preliminary body work merely because a tidy sequence says they should.

I become more cautious when the trauma is diffuse, developmental, mostly bodily or pre-verbal, relational, or when the nervous system is still unstable. In those cases, I want at least some access to a Nurturer and Protector before opening highly charged material, and Brainspotting may fit better earlier.

After EMDR, I want the rest of the day to be almost boring. Walk. Eat. Hydrate. Sleep early if you are tired. Do restorative yoga if it feels good. Journal only if it grounds you. Maybe use a little EFT if activation remains. I wouldn't casually stack Brainspotting, EMDR, intense shaking, and deep yin on the same day unless I already knew my system could handle that much and I had skilled support.

Sometimes the useful thing after processing is simply to stop processing.

# Making Sense of What Changed

## Light CBT / Narrative Integration

When my body is no longer insisting “this is happening now,” it becomes much more useful to talk to the belief. Before that, I can win the argument intellectually and still lose it physiologically.

Then I can revisit old beliefs, make a more coherent life story, separate past danger from present reality, clarify values and boundaries, and get some agency back. This can feed directly into [inner-child reparenting](http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/). The adult is more available. I can tell the adult and child-states more easily. Forgiveness—when forgiveness is actually relevant—and life direction stop being quite so abstract.

# How I Know Whether It Actually Helped

My outcome measure is boring. Does it still hit as hard later? By *restimulation*, I mean what happens when I remember the event again. Does the same charge come back into my body and mind?

While I was working on this article, I was still affected by something that happened with my housemate the day before. I was washing all her moldy clothes for her. One of the dresses got discolored, and she yelled at me. I was still stewing over how she treated me.

I did a quick bit of self-Brainspotting. While thinking about the event, I moved my finger and eyes slowly around. As I moved farther to either side and into more peripheral vision, I noticed blissful tingles. There is a popular, simple claim that peripheral vision activates the parasympathetic nervous system. I don't take my experience as proof of that; the research is more complicated.

Subjectively, I could move my gaze between a position where I felt settled and noticed blissful tingles and a position where I felt distressed, moment by moment. While I thought about the event, I could kind of move the bliss into the distress. Afterward, the event seemed to hold less negative energy, or less blocking energy, in my mind. That only tells me what happened right then.

The important version of me is the one an hour later, not the one enjoying the final minute of the exercise. If I'm still stewing just as hard, perhaps the effect was temporary and I should try again. If I can remember what happened and feel less restimulated, that is more durable. I don't expect one treatment to erase everything; one EMDR session is often not enough either.

A bigger intervention leaves a wake, and sometimes I don't notice it until the next day. What happened to my sleep? Am I more irritable, more dissociated, or in more pain? Do I feel compelled to keep processing? How am I treating people? A calm final ten minutes doesn't prove that the dose was right.

Research matters to me. I just don't use the size of a literature as a ranking algorithm for my nervous system. A shaking, qigong, breathwork, or somatic community may say a practice releases decades of trauma, awakens kundalini, clears stored emotion, or rapidly heals the whole nervous system. My result can't prove all of those explanations. I look at people whose patterns resemble mine, change one thing at a time when I can, and keep coming back to the unglamorous question: am I actually doing better?

# Sky Hypnosis and Vagal Blitz

[Sky Hypnosis](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/try-this-10-second-sky-hypnosis-bodymind) and [Vagal Blitz](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/vagal-blitz-breathwork-gives-best) are different animals. They shift state hard. I've put them outside the main route because they're not prerequisites for healing, although a stable person may still find them useful much earlier.

Sky Hypnosis can make me lightheaded. Vagal Blitz is only for lying down and comes with cardiovascular and mental-health cautions. I use the complete guide, start with the least intense version that produces a useful change, and stop if balance, orientation, or recovery starts to become unreliable. Fainting and loss of consciousness are not goals.

I don't count “wow” as a result either. A huge release, bliss, an altered state—all of it can feel profound while the actual problem sits there untouched. Later, can I handle pain, boundaries, conflict, and ordinary life any better? If the extraordinary practice is mainly helping me dodge grief, conflict, practical problems, or deeper relational healing, intensity doesn't rescue it.
