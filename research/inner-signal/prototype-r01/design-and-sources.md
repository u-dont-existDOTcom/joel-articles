# Design decisions, voice adaptation, and source notes

**10 September 2026 · Prototype r01 · Proposed design, not implemented software or an approved clinical protocol**

## What this prototype is testing

Can a person learn a genuinely hypnotic, internally interactive practice without needing an app, a memorized therapeutic monologue, or continuous analytical self-report?

The chosen approach is adaptation and composition: Ericksonian pacing/utilization and sensory training; Levine’s titration, pendulation, and attention to experience across SIBAM; and Joel’s communion, inner-adult functions, optional spiritual framing, reality-testing, and practical follow-through. The whole combination has not been tested. Existing component traditions are not efficacy evidence for this combination.

A single induction supplies continuity. The middle is a learned conversational skill, with brief examples rather than another script to recite. The induction is suggestive about attention and receptivity, but does not dictate a childhood cause, a protective motive, a bodily response, or the truth of an emerging story. No pause means agreement, and no lack of response means hidden success.

Rich language can remain useful during the middle. It should develop from the actual response instead of restarting the entire induction. Metaphor, sensory detail, and warmth are available; they are not compulsory channels. An already absorbed and oriented person may need little additional induction. Absorption, directiveness, intensity of emotional content, and amount of narration are different dimensions; increasing one does not require increasing the others.

The training text is intentionally more explicit than the eventual internal practice. After learning it, a person can reduce it to the cue card below. Shortening the guide need not shorten every session or remove hypnotic richness.

## A self-led cue card

**Before:** What am I meeting? What am I leaving alone? What kind of encouragement is welcome? Leave time to return.

**Enter:** Chosen anchor → familiar induction → chosen opening phrase.

**Listen:** What am I noticing? Name it provisionally in my own terms.

**Respond:** Stay, ask one fitting question, or try one act of care. Wait. Let the actual result change the next move.

**Adjust:** More time, less intensity, different support, fewer words, firmer encouragement, or finish. No duty to intensify or to feel good.

**Return:** End the imagery and deepening; body contact, room, day, voluntary movement, eyes open, alertness. Say the session is over. Check rather than assume reorientation.

**Learn:** Before / what I tried / after / uncertain / any real-life action. Include later functioning, not only the immediate feeling.

## A fictional demonstration of responsiveness

This is an authored illustration, not Joel’s history, a clinical case, or a user-test result. Assume the participant completed the preparation and induction, knows the return, and has chosen to explore the difficulty of asking for space. In solo practice, the guiding lines can be spoken inwardly.

**Participant:** “There’s a tight band across my chest.”

**Guiding voice:** “Let attention stay with that band for a moment. What do you notice about it?”

**Participant:** “I want to tell someone to leave me alone. But I don’t want you to talk me into relaxing.”

**Guiding voice:** “Then I’ll leave relaxation out of it. Would you like to stay with that wish for space?”

**Participant:** “Yes. I’m sad, but I’m here. I want to feel it.”

**Guiding voice:** “Take some time with the sadness. You don’t have to make it smaller for this conversation.”

*Silence of the length agreed before starting; no demand for an explanation.*

**Participant:** “I keep planning what to say instead of feeling anything.”

**Guiding voice:** “You asked for a little encouragement when that happens. Let the planning wait for a moment. As you say ‘I need some space,’ what do you notice?”

**Participant:** “My hand wants to move away from me.”

**Guiding voice:** “Would you like to explore a comfortable amount of that movement, or just notice the impulse?”

**Participant:** “I moved it a little. The band is still there. But I feel less trapped.”

**Guiding voice:** “The tightness is still there, and you feel less trapped. Stay with the difference for a moment.”

*No claim of nervous-system discharge, recovered history, or completed healing.*

**Participant:** “I think I have enough. I want to make the request later.”

**Guiding voice:** “Then let’s finish here.”

*Complete the entire return from the prototype before discussing wording for the real-world request. The participant might decide to rest, seek counsel, or make no request; rehearsal is not an instruction to act immediately.*

Changing one detail changes the appropriate response. If the participant instead says “I don’t know where I am; I can’t stop it,” the exploration ends and the voice switches to present orientation and support. If the participant says “I’m fine, I just don’t want this,” the refusal is sufficient. The same sensation or topic does not select the same intervention for every person.

## What the app should be

These are requirements for a later implementation, not claims about the current app.

### Teaching and preparation

Text is suitable for explaining methods, designing and reviewing scripts, reflecting after sessions, and helping a user articulate preferences. The learning goal is usable self-hypnosis and better-informed choices about outside help. A person may prefer self-hypnosis indefinitely.

Joel’s view that trained self-hypnosis can outperform another hypnotist for many people is preserved as his position. His personal account remains in its existing location. Neither the books nor this prototype establish how often self-hypnosis outperforms skilled heterohypnosis, for which populations, or on which outcomes. The app must not silently replace his position with a universal hierarchy in which clinician-led hypnosis is always the endpoint.

### Optional user-directed voice guidance

Voice would provide the external narration and interaction the user chooses. The person could specify target, vocabulary, spiritual or secular framing, warmth, directness, silence, imagery, and unacceptable methods. The app would acknowledge that it cannot feel the person’s body, share embodied therapeutic resonance, or reliably know an unreported response. It must not claim intuitive access to the subconscious.

Use brief reports at meaningful transitions, with minimal interruption during a developing experience. Before starting, agree how long silence should ordinarily last, how the user requests more time, and what happens at the check-in. Silence is unknown information, not evidence of comfort, consent, dissociation, or a successful trance.

A future implementation would need working interruption, an accessible stop control independent of speech recognition, and a locally available return sequence. These must be tested on the actual device before live exploratory use. Microphone, network, transcription, and playback failures must stop new exploratory prompts. Do not simply resume deepening after a disconnection. A brief failure notice should direct the user to the known return. Do not claim the app can summon help or monitor safety unless that capability exists and is explicitly configured.

The voice should not require the user to verbalize every bodily event. Nor should it claim to infer autonomic states, concealed trauma, or readiness from prosody, breathing sounds, or confidence. Those are not substitutes for a clinical assessment. Brief spoken observations can be useful without being sufficient.

Do not clone Nimja’s voice or imply his endorsement. This prototype uses original language informed by general indirect-hypnosis techniques.

### Method consultation and preparation for a human hypnotist

The knowledge available for explanation should be broader than the procedures the app will conduct. A user can ask about regression, affect bridges, renurturing, abreaction, distancing, touch, or an unusual experience without triggering a refusal to discuss the subject.

For “My hypnotist did this; was it trauma-informed?”, ask about the actual wording, aim, consent, preparation, choices, response, how the therapist adjusted, and what happened afterward. Assess those features rather than certifying or condemning a practitioner from a method name. Uncomfortable is not automatically harmful; impressive or comforting is not automatically sound.

Example: “An affect bridge is intended to link a present feeling with related experience. That name alone doesn’t tell us whether its use was appropriate. Did the therapist invite associations or insist that a specific event must have occurred? Could you pause? How did they respond when the experience changed?” This is an explanation and a context check, not an affect-bridge induction.

Help users prepare a short preferences note for a prospective practitioner: what has helped, what has not, preferred directness, acceptable touch/imagery, how to stop, reactions to prior work, and questions about trauma and dissociation training. Do not diagnose their therapist, tell them that disagreement proves pathology, or pressure them into unnecessary dependence on either a practitioner or the app.

## A negotiated scope with stated service limitations

Tailor the working scope to the person and the session. Consider what they choose, how well they can retain present orientation and agency, previous adverse responses, current sleep and functioning, available support, and the method under consideration. Do not treat “has trauma,” a diagnostic label, crying, anger, or discussion of an old memory as a universal stop condition. Do not turn these factors into an unvalidated readiness score.

A preference for strong encouragement is not permission to ignore refusal. A preference for quiet is not permission to disappear indefinitely. Trance depth is not a proxy for capacity to process traumatic content. Comfortable feeling, confident speech, and “I accept the risk” do not give an app missing clinical capabilities.

When the current process is tolerable, chosen, and within the service’s competence, the app should be able to remain with difficult material rather than automatically redirecting to relaxation. When information is insufficient, ask the smallest useful process question. When its competence is the limiting factor, explain that fact without calling the user fragile, incapable, or unready as a person.

Some procedures can remain in the guide as educational material with method-specific labels: **“For work with a trauma-informed clinician present who is trained in this method.”** Examples for later editorial review include deliberate trauma-focused regression, affect-bridge exploration of unknown traumatic origins, and management of substantial flashback or dissociative responses. The label should say what requires support and why; it should not imply that any person calling themselves a hypnotist can provide it.

A clinician’s presence also does not make hypnosis a truth test, justify coercion, or validate recovered imagery. Such claims need correction in discussion, not an “advanced use” warning that quietly endorses them. No topic has to be erased from the knowledge base to maintain these distinctions. Preserve the user’s opportunity to explain and ask questions even when the app will not enact a procedure.

If current orientation, agency, or immediate safety is failing, stop inward exploration and support a return to the present and appropriate human help. In contrast, the fact that a user becomes sad while remaining oriented and deliberately choosing to continue should not by itself force an exit. These are proposed service boundaries, not a claim that the prototype can perform reliable clinical triage.

## What further reading changed

Additional close reading focused on *Healing the Divided Self*, printed pp.118–120, 222–225, and 229–233 (PDF pages are printed pages +28). Related retrieved passages on stage-II initiative, restabilization, body awareness, and Levine’s containment were also reviewed. This remains targeted comparative reading, not completion of every chapter of all six books. No missing companion audio was reconstructed or heard.

The Kristi case, pp.231–232, is especially relevant. Phillips describes an impasse in which her non-intrusive approach was experienced as distance. After discussion, she used more active encouragement at points the patient signaled, while carefully monitoring the response. The authors report improvement. This is a clinical case, not a trial of an app or permission to push any user. It directly argues against equating trauma-informed practice with one universally quiet, minimally directive style.

The interpersonal-trance discussion, pp.222–225, also limits the app analogy. The authors describe embodied observation and resonance together with analytical judgment, rather than trusting intuition alone. The technology design must not claim an equivalent relationship.

The memory guidance, pp.118–120, combines useful warnings about uncertainty with historical assumptions about reconstructing hidden origins. We retain the cautions without turning every sensation into a puzzle piece of a presumed forgotten event. Levine’s later *Trauma and Memory*, chapter 7, remains the principal source for separating compelling experience from historical verification.

## Review status and what remains unknown

The draft received a literal same-context editorial review for preservation of owner goals, non-leading content, a usable solo route, genuine continuation with chosen discomfort, uncertain/no-response handling, and a complete exit. Static artifact checks are recorded separately. No independent clinical reviewer, human participant, or voice runtime has tested it.

The main unresolved question is experiential: does the induction create the absorbing, receptive contact Joel values, while the learned middle stays intuitive rather than becoming a task of monitoring oneself? Test that on the actual wording before redesigning the full guide. If the method feels flat or overly managerial, revise the entry and the handoff rather than adding more screening questions or prohibitions. No result is inferred from the fictional demonstration.
