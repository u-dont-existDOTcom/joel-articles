# Inner Signal: learning to guide your own hypnosis

**Practice prototype r01 · 10 September 2026**

A complete practice for owner review, developed from Joel’s guide and the supplied books. This is an original, provisional synthesis, not a validated treatment or a script by Levine, Phillips, Frederick, or Nimja. No audio or app implementation is included. Source notes are in `design-and-sources.md`.

## Read this before practicing

Begin with the self-led version. Read the practice once while fully awake, including the return. Then put the screen aside. You can speak inwardly, use your own words, or record the induction in your own voice and continue without playback. You do not need to remember every sentence or consult a branching menu during the session.

The short form is: **gather attention, notice, respond, wait, return.**

Choose something you genuinely want to meet: a feeling, an unresolved question, a familiar difficulty, or simply the wish for warmer contact with yourself. The subject need not be trivial. For an initial trial, choose something you can approach without losing your sense of where you are or your ability to change your mind. You are learning how to respond, not testing how much you can endure.

Allow roughly fifteen to twenty-five minutes, including several minutes to return and reflect. This is a practical scheduling suggestion, not a therapeutic dose. Finish earlier when that is enough. Set an unobtrusive reminder before your available time ends so you do not have to watch the clock.

Practice somewhere physically secure, without responsibilities requiring your attention. Do not use the induction while driving, bathing, swimming, operating equipment, or supervising someone who needs you. Postpone exploratory hypnosis when intoxicated, acutely confused, or unable to keep yourself safe; address immediate care and support instead. A history of trauma is not, by itself, a reason to exclude all self-practice. [G; B4, pp.20–28; W1]

Before beginning, settle two questions:

**What am I choosing to work with, and what am I leaving alone today?**

**What kind of help is welcome: spacious listening, warm companionship, or some direct encouragement?**

A simple answer is enough: “I want to explore the bracing that comes with asking for space. Some feeling is welcome. I don’t want to search for forgotten events. Help me stay with what I actually notice.” Another day you may choose only comfort or companionship.

Choose one starting anchor: contact with the chair, a sound, or a welcome hand on your body. You may use your familiar two-hand practice. Its meaning belongs to you; the hand positions do not prove where a feeling or part lives.

Choose your own opening phrase. “Let me meet what is here with warmth and honesty” is enough. Your familiar appeal to Higher Self, the universal heart, or the wise subconscious can serve the same purpose. Use language that helps you make contact rather than language you feel obliged to believe.

**Stopping:** “Stop” means end the exercise and use the return below. “Less” means reduce the demand or move attention outward. “Quiet” means stop adding words. You can also simply open your eyes and finish. These are explicit choices for this practice, not hidden triggers. No inner signal has to approve your decision to stop.

## 1. Let attention gather

*The following is the induction. Read or record it slowly, with a little room between paragraphs. Bracketed directions are not spoken. Keep only the starting anchor and opening phrase you selected; you do not need to read all the alternatives aloud.*

> Let your eyes settle somewhere in the room, somewhere they can rest for a little while, and notice the difference between looking for something and letting what is already there come into view.
>
> There is a surface beneath you. Notice where you meet it … the contact, the pressure, whatever you can actually feel. And while you notice that, a sound may reach you, or a breath may draw your attention, without needing you to arrange either one.
>
> You can give this a little time.
>
> A thought about the day may still be here. A thought about whether you are doing this properly may be here too. There is room to notice a thought and return to the contact you chose, without finishing the thought first.
>
> And as you return, you may find one detail that holds your interest … something about the sound, or the way your weight meets the surface … and let attention stay with that detail long enough to become curious.
>
> There is no particular sensation to produce. The contact you actually feel is where we begin.
>
> Perhaps you can give yourself the time you would give someone you care about who has begun to speak and has not yet found the words. You can remain nearby, listening, without finishing their sentence for them.
>
> [Pause.]
>
> Let your chosen phrase come to mind now. Say it as an invitation, and notice how it is received.
>
> [Pause.]
>
> As you continue, your eyes can close, soften, or stay open. Let that be a choice you make. You can allow listening to deepen while a sound from the room remains available in the background.
>
> Count slowly from five to one. Give each number its own moment.
>
> **Five.** Notice your chosen contact again.
>
> **Four.** Let attention gather around what you find there.
>
> **Three.** Leave a little space after the words.
>
> **Two.** Listen into that space, without preparing an answer.
>
> **One.** Stay here for a moment.
>
> [Pause.]
>
> Bring your chosen concern lightly to mind, or remain with your wish for contact. Let it be present without rehearsing its whole story.
>
> **What am I noticing now?**
>
> Give the response time to take its own form. It might be a sensation, a few words, an image, a feeling, or an impulse to move. There may be nothing clear yet.
>
> Let the words become less important while you listen.

## 2. Follow what actually happens

The induction hands over here. In self-hypnosis, the next words are yours. Silence is part of the practice; you are not waiting for a missing narrator to tell you what you feel.

Notice one thing accurately. Answer it with one question, an act of care, or a little more time. Then wait long enough to receive the response. You may move between sensation, emotion, imagery, words, and meaning; you do not need to populate every category. [G; B3, ch.7]

The following are examples to learn beforehand, **not a checklist to perform**. Choose only what fits the moment.

### When something is developing, let it develop

A feeling may become clearer, an image may change, or an impulse may begin to make sense. There is no need to interrupt each change with another question.

> “I can stay with this for a little while.”

A difficult feeling does not have to become pleasant before you can listen to it. Ask a process question when one is needed: “Can I still choose whether to stay or shift?” If yes, and you want to remain, you can continue within the scope you chose. Tears or discomfort alone do not tell you to push ahead or to stop. [Adaptation from B3, chs.5 and 13; B4, pp.42–44]

If you chose more active encouragement, you can use a firmer invitation when it helps: “Let the explanation wait for a moment. What do I feel as I say that?” Directness can focus the exploration without deciding what the answer must be. Change course if the invitation stops helping. [Adaptation from B4, pp.231–232]

### When the wording is wrong, correct the wording

Suppose “let yourself soften” produces bracing, or simply sounds false. Do not repeat it more persuasively.

> “That phrase doesn’t fit. I’ll leave the tightness as it is. What would be a better way to meet it?”

Wait. You may prefer firmer contact, more distance, fewer words, a different question, or no further exploration. Skepticism need not be a hidden protector. Sometimes the sentence was just wrong.

### When a younger feeling appears, offer a relationship

You may recognize vulnerability as younger, or an image of a child may appear. There is no need to assign an age, enter a past scene, or decide why this feeling exists.

> “I’m here. Would company help, or would you prefer some space?”

Respond to what comes back. If the answer is “Don’t come closer,” remain at the chosen distance. If warmth is welcome, offer it in a form you can mean. Then notice what receiving that particular response is like.

Your Nurturer supplies care; your Protector carries out a boundary; your Leader helps decide what to do afterward. None needs a speech. When those capacities feel unavailable, begin as a witness and borrow one credible sentence or action from a safe model, while keeping your own judgment. [G; B4, pp.148–153, 173–174]

### When an experiment seems useful, try one

You can intentionally introduce support rather than waiting for every resource to appear spontaneously.

> “Would it help to try a little more distance?”

Or choose a welcome hand placement, a different posture, an imagined source of support, or words you would like to hear. Try one change and notice its effect. A resource that makes things worse is not the right resource just because it sounds kind. [B1, ch.4; B6, ch.5]

When an impulse to move becomes noticeable, you may explore a small, voluntary, physically comfortable version—or imagine it without moving. Keep the movement within your physical abilities. You are finding out what it is like; you are not obliged to enact an impulse or produce a release. Stop movements that cause pain or other concerning symptoms. [Adaptation from G; B3, ch.5]

### When you need less, change the amount of contact

Let attention widen to the chair, a sound, or a part of the body that is easier to meet. You do not have to erase the feeling to include something else.

> “I can let this much be here and notice the support beside it.”

Stay with the more manageable contact. Returning to the difficult material is optional, and happens because you choose it, not because you completed a prescribed number of soothing breaths. This adapts titration and pendulation; it is not a fixed cycle you must repeat. [B3, ch.5; B4, pp.38–44]

If you begin losing track of the present, feel increasingly unreal, cannot deliberately shift attention, or no longer feel able to stop, end the inward exploration and use the return. Persistent disorientation or worsening functioning calls for human help, not a stronger induction. Immediate danger needs urgent assistance. [G; B4, pp.23–28, 154–155]

### When nothing clear happens, do not manufacture a conversation

Return to one concrete sensation or your chosen sound. You can rest with it, try a different question, or finish. A blank interval does not establish that something is hidden or that a healing process is happening invisibly.

The useful lesson may be that this way of entering does not suit you today.

## 3. Find a stopping place

When you have received enough, or your available time is ending, stop opening new questions. You do not have to make the experience resolve into a lesson.

> “What would help me leave this here for now?”

Perhaps there is a need for a note, an action, some rest, or an appointment with someone. Record the practical matter later rather than starting a second session around it.

A familiar memory can be acknowledged as something you remember. Newly arising imagery can be meaningful without establishing a historical event. Neither a bodily shift nor relief settles whether an interpretation is true. Save factual evaluation and major decisions for ordinary awareness. [B2, ch.4; B5, ch.7; B4, pp.118–119]

## 4. Come all the way back

*Use this after every waking practice, including one you ended early. You can use it yourself if a recording or future voice service stops unexpectedly.*

> “I’m finishing this practice now. I can return to unfinished questions another time. Any instructions to deepen or remain in this session are finished.
>
> “I let the imagery recede and bring attention back to my actual surroundings. Any younger feeling belongs within my present life; I return as myself, at my present age.
>
> “**One.** Feel the contact beneath the body.
>
> “**Two.** Let attention widen to the sounds around me.
>
> “**Three.** Name where I am and the day or date.
>
> “**Four.** Move my hands, feet, shoulders, or another area I can comfortably move.
>
> “**Five.** Open my eyes, look around, and return to full waking attention.
>
> “**The session has ended.**”

Check that you are oriented and alert rather than merely repeating the words. Move before standing, and stand only when steady. If you still feel significantly confused, unreal, or unable to return to usual activity, stop further hypnosis and seek appropriate support. An exit statement is an instruction, not a guarantee of recovery. [G; B4, p.27]

## Afterward: learn from this particular session

Write a few lines: **what was there before; what I tried; what happened afterward; what remains uncertain.** Add a practical action only when one is warranted. Keeping a promise, seeking support, or leaving a boundary in place can matter more than making another suggestion.

Later that day and after sleep, notice how you are functioning. A session that felt powerful but repeatedly leaves you less able to sleep, relate, or manage daily life needs a different approach or more support. Do not label deterioration “healing” simply because the session was intense. [G; B4, pp.154–155]

On a later practice, keep the induction elements that actually suited you and shorten the wording you no longer need. The skill being learned is how to enter, listen, respond, and return for yourself. There is no requirement to graduate from self-hypnosis to another hypnotist.
