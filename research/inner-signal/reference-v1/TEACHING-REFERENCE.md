# Hypnosis teaching and methods reference v1

2026-09-10. Original synthesis for a future educational/consultation layer. Not the public guide, a treatment manual, an implemented app, or proof of competence. Source IDs resolve in SOURCES-AND-READING.md. A statement under **Source account** describes that source's position; **Application** is our design decision or inference. Historical assumptions are not silently rewritten as modern consensus.

The recurring task is to answer the user's actual question: what a method is, how to learn an appropriate skill, what happened in a reported session, or whether a particular claim is supported. Do not turn every educational question into a risk interview or every practice difficulty into trauma investigation.

## Learning and hypnotic experience

### T01 — Self-hypnosis, another hypnotist, and recordings

**Source account:** Eason and Parris describe self/heterohypnosis as a difficult boundary to draw when a person uses recordings, written instructions or previously learned cues. They defend self-directed learning rather than making another hypnotist a necessary precursor. [W01]

**Application:** Describe who selects, delivers and adjusts suggestions instead of arguing over a purity label. Text can teach; voice can provide chosen external guidance; an internal practice can proceed without either. Preserve Joel's view that self-hypnosis can outperform a practitioner for many people without claiming a comparative population result this reading has not established.

### T02 — What counts as hypnosis?

**Source account:** The professional explanations emphasize focused attention and responsiveness to suggestion; B4 distinguishes hypnosis from sleep and allows open eyes. Cardena discusses minimal-suggestion and non-relaxation approaches. [W13; B4, pp.24–27; W06]

**Application:** Do not require unconsciousness, sleepiness, a blank mind, vivid pictures, amnesia or a dramatic bodily sign. The guide deliberately includes an induction because Joel values it; that design choice is different from asserting that no hypnosis can occur without a separate ritual. Do not claim a five-count proves a particular brain state.

### T03 — Theories: several accounts, not one settled mechanism

**Source account:** The ISH entries distinguish social-cognitive/integrative, dissociated-control or monitoring, higher-order/cold-control and predictive-processing accounts. These explain overlapping observations differently. [W02–W04]

**Application:** First identify which theory the user is asking about. Explain that theory in its own terms, then label the evidence and competing accounts. A useful hypnotic experience does not select a unique theory. Avoid replacing every question with either 'it is only placebo' or 'it bypasses the conscious mind.' Neither slogan answers the mechanism question.

### T04 — Predictive processing and bottom-up work

**Source account:** Jamieson's account uses interactions among expectations, incoming information and their relative weighting; proposed hypnotic applications remain theoretical. [W03]

**Application:** Deliberately evocative language can coexist with checking actual experience. This does not make every suggestion physiologically bottom-up. The practical commitment is that a response can revise the next intervention. An app cannot infer prediction-error weights or a nervous-system state from a report of warmth. Explain the proposed model without presenting it as a measurement.

### T05 — Responsiveness is something to explore, not a grade of worth

**Source account:** B4 samples different sensory and hypnotic approaches and develops skills using familiar experience. It discusses both standardized assessment and clinical variation. [B4, pp.26–27, 45–50]

**Application:** Ask what actually occurred and which instructions were understandable, welcome or difficult. Try a materially different entry when repeating one fails. Low response to one suggestion is not proof of resistance, hidden trauma or permanent inability. A clinical assessment instrument is not a casual app score, and a successful hand movement is not proof of readiness for complex trauma work.

### T06 — Felt involuntariness and retained choice

**Source account:** Polito and Arbige distinguish an experience of a response happening by itself from the disputed question of whether its production is automatic. Different theories interpret agency changes differently. [W02]

**Application:** Do not contradict a user reporting an involuntary-feeling response, or demand continuous executive monitoring as the definition of good hypnosis. Equally, do not treat that feeling as consent or proof that another person has irresistible control. Practice should make correction and ending usable; reassuring language alone does not establish that either is working.

### T07 — Learning an induction and a return

**Source account:** B4 teaches starting, deepening, stopping and reorienting, with rehearsal and recordings between sessions. [B4, pp.45–50, 83–90]

**Application:** Explain the purpose while awake, demonstrate one coherent route, let the person experience it, and discuss its actual effects afterward. Teach a complete return before shortening entry. Reduce scaffolding as the learner internalizes the skill. Keep the selected anchor consistent: sound-based practice should not silently turn into a mandatory body scan. A future app must not retain the only copy of the return.

### T08 — Imagery without compulsory pictures

**Source account:** B4 describes variation among pictures, felt impressions and inner listening, and practices sensory awareness using familiar objects or sounds. [B4, pp.48–50]

**Application:** A person who does not visualize can use language, rhythm, spatial relations, contact or another accessible modality. Do not diagnose aphantasia from one failed exercise or promise training will produce vivid pictures. A learner can describe what 'support' would be like and test that experience without seeing a cinematic scene. A nonvisual route is an alternative method, not a remedial failure.

### T09 — Direct, indirect and permissive language

**Source account:** Phillips and Frederick use direct suggestions, evocative resources and Ericksonian approaches; Nimja's tutorials describe language forms and delivery. [B4, pp.53–57, 83–100; W08]

**Application:** Match the form to the person's preference and response. Rich language remains available throughout the session. A gently phrased presupposition can still impose a cause; a direct question can leave the answer open. Indirection is not consent and directness is not coercion by definition. Teach how an invitation is constructed, not a universal superiority claim about one linguistic style.

### T10 — Pacing, leading, utilization and metaphor

**Source account:** B4 links acknowledgement of present experience with an invitation toward a useful possibility, and describes adapting to unexpected responses. [B4, pp.53–57]

**Application:** Start with something actually reported or observable. Offer one relevant possibility, then allow time for its effect. Metaphors are proposals, not concealed historical assertions. When a metaphor stops helping, change it rather than explaining why the person ought to respond. The structure of the learner's own next sentence can be simple: accurate observation, fitting invitation, silence. Eloquence is optional.

### T11 — Re-entry cues and anchors

**Source account:** Associational cues appear in B4; Nimja describes familiar effects, clarity, practice and variability in cueing. [B4, pp.97–100; W09]

**Application:** Pair a deliberately chosen cue with an already useful way of attending, then test re-entry at a suitable later time. Do not pair it with maximal emotional activation. Preserve a separate alert-calming purpose rather than using a trance cue for driving. No universal repetition count, guaranteed instant induction, secret obedience cue or instant-erasure promise is justified. Retiring a cue includes ceasing rehearsal and removing it from active recordings/settings.

### T12 — Posthypnotic suggestions and amnesia

**Source account:** B4 uses reminders and continued access to resources; Kihlstrom discusses experimental posthypnotic amnesia as suggestion-related retrieval difficulty, rather than erasure of stored experience. [B4, p.50; W05]

**Application:** State the intended later effect and its scope explicitly. Do not make forgotten instructions a learning goal. Remembering a session does not establish that hypnosis failed. An unexplained memory gap deserves contextual assessment rather than automatic classification as ordinary hypnosis or a verified dissociative disorder. A theory of laboratory amnesia does not explain every real-world loss of time.

## Responsive practice and body-oriented work

### T13 — Observation, interpretation and deliberate experiment

**Source account:** The books describe interactions among sensory, affective, imaginal and cognitive experience; their memory cautions limit what those experiences establish. [B1, ch.4; B3, ch.7; B5, ch.7]

**Application:** Distinguish what appeared before a suggestion, what was intentionally introduced, the subsequent response and the proposed interpretation. Neither spontaneity nor induced relief proves an external fact. Somatic feedback informs fit, tolerability and preference; it is not a truth test. These distinctions can be reviewed afterward rather than interrupting every developing experience with analysis.

### T14 — Bracing after a suggestion

**Source account:** B3 and B4 contain cases where the same initial response can lead to different interventions depending on the person's report and sequence. [B3, chs.7–8; B4, pp.53–57, 229–233]

**Application:** 'My shoulders tightened' and 'that wording feels wrong' are different observations. The first leaves interpretation open; the second directly communicates poor fit. Do not repeat an expressly rejected suggestion more persuasively. Nor automatically conclude that any tension proves the wording was wrong, the user is resisting healing, or a protective child has been discovered.

### T15 — SIBAM is a map, not five compulsory reports

**Source account:** Levine's SIBAM names Sensation, Image, Behavior, Affect and Meaning. B4 also discusses BASK and other ways of connecting experiences. Their categories are not exact synonyms. [B3, ch.7; B4, pp.157–159]

**Application:** Use the relevant channel and notice relationships. Do not manufacture missing images or insist that meaning must always come last. Preserve that behavior can be observed by a clinician while many other dimensions are reported. A voice app receives a narrower account and must not invent unseen posture, facial color, movement or autonomic changes.

### T16 — Titration, pendulation and fractionation

**Source account:** Titration concerns how much difficult activation is approached; pendulation concerns movement among different experiential qualities, including contraction and expansion. Hypnotic fractionation uses movement into and out of hypnotic engagement. [B1, ch.4; B2, ch.4; B3, ch.5; B4, p.27]

**Application:** Explain which process is being used rather than making the terms interchangeable. A person can change the amount of contact without ending hypnosis, or briefly reorient without claiming a completed autonomic cycle. No fixed count of breaths or shifts proves readiness to revisit material.

### T17 — Resources are individually tested

**Source account:** B1 combines bodily tracking with compassion, imagery and neutral or welcome experiences. B6 explicitly treats the usefulness of music, people or activities as individual. [B1, chs.3–4; B6, ch.5]

**Application:** Offer the resource as an experiment. A parental image, hand on the heart, prayer or relaxed posture is not automatically beneficial because it sounds caring. Neutral contact may be more usable than an intensely positive image. Keep spiritual and secular routes available. The person's report changes the choice; there is no required calming symbol.

### T18 — Breath is one possible route

**Source account:** B1 offers several breathing approaches and says some can increase anxiety; the reader is directed toward a better-fitting alternative. [B1, ch.3]

**Application:** Distinguish noticing breathing from deliberately controlling it. Do not insist on increasingly deep breathing when that is producing discomfort. A sound, external orientation or contact may be a better entry. Conversely, a poor response to one breathing method does not justify banning all attention to breath. No breathing pattern certifies that a medical symptom is psychological.

### T19 — Overcoupling and undercoupling

**Source account:** The SE account in B4 distinguishes disconnected components of experience from components rigidly linked together. Its proposed interventions sometimes connect and sometimes differentiate. [B4, pp.157–159; B3, ch.7]

**Application:** Do not assume that more association is always progress. A sensation need not keep proving the same catastrophic thought; a positive memory need not be followed through every painful association. Equally, intellectual explanation may remain detached from felt experience. These are source-derived ways of considering a pattern, not diagnoses generated from a single sentence.

### T20 — Movement and containment

**Source account:** Levine describes small defensive/orienting movements and differentiates experiencing strong affect from impulsive enactment or suppression. [B3, ch.5 and displayed pp.276–296; B1, ch.3]

**Application:** A noticed impulse may be observed, imagined or explored in a small physically comfortable way. Do not require shaking, dramatic discharge or enactment of aggression. Containment should make contact more manageable, not mean refusing every difficult feeling. A change in sensation may be useful without proving that stored trauma energy was discharged or that treatment is complete.

### T21 — Positive practice is a complete purpose

**Source account:** The books include strengthening, compassion, future possibility and generativity, not only symptom exploration. [B1, chs.4, 7; B4, pp.82–100, 316–319]

**Application:** A session may cultivate love, curiosity, play, confidence, creativity or spiritual connection without finding a wound. Rehearse a chosen action and notice its fit. A successful imagined future is neither prediction nor clinical clearance. Preserve the distinction between creating an experience and recovering information; both can be worthwhile, for different reasons.

## Parts, memory and more demanding methods

### T22 — Ego states and an inner child

**Source account:** B4 presents ego-state theory as a model with metaphorical features, not a collection of literal people or a compulsory Parent/Adult/Child inventory. It assumes adaptive origins within its theory. [B4, pp.61–66]

**Application:** Use the person's own language. Younger-feeling contact need not specify an age or historical scene. Nurturer, Protector and Leader are Joel's available functions, not a diagnostic ontology established by the book. Do not infer DID from parts language or force every symptom into a protector explanation. Explain the authors' adaptive-origin premise as their premise when discussing it.

### T23 — Renurturing and borrowed capacities

**Source account:** B4 distinguishes therapist-evoked renurturing from using the person's ego-state resources; it also develops mature capacities and positive models. [B4, pp.148–153, 171–174]

**Application:** Care can be a credible sentence, respectful distance, a boundary or an action, not a mandatory parental speech. When an internal adult feels unavailable, borrowed models may supply a limited function without surrendering judgment. Do not promise to replace childhood or create historical evidence that support occurred. The aim of internalization is greater usable capacity, not dependence on a narrator's approval.

### T24 — Integration is not automatic deletion of parts

**Source account:** B4 discusses cooperation and permeable boundaries, differentiating integration from fusion/merging. It also expresses reservations about ongoing overt separateness in its 1995 clinical model. [B4, pp.65, 157–169]

**Application:** Preserve both aspects when describing the book. Do not claim that the authors prescribe simply erasing parts, or that their full position is a contemporary consensus on identity. In this guide, track cooperation, continuity and functioning; do not impose a diagnosis, fusion goal or an expanding cast. Complex dissociative treatment is not established by a self-help metaphor.

### T25 — Ideomotor and ideosensory communication

**Source account:** B4 describes movement or sensation conventions for yes, no, uncertainty and privacy. On p.52 it explicitly limits their validity; elsewhere it uses more confident exploratory language. [B4, pp.50–52, 108–113; W10]

**Application:** Explain these as ways to notice or communicate a response, not independent access to factual truth. Clarify ambiguous question wording. Do not repeat until the desired answer is obtained, diagnose from a signal, or require an internal yes before honoring the person's stated stop. A person may experience a movement as spontaneous without its interpretation becoming infallible.

### T26 — Age regression and affect/somatic bridges

**Source account:** B4 uses regression for positive memories as well as difficult material. An affect or somatic bridge invites associations across times sharing a feeling or sensation. Some examples presuppose an original hidden cause. [B4, pp.52–60, 105–120]

**Application:** Distinguish resource recall, imagined younger-self contact, revisiting a known event, and searching for an unknown traumatic origin. The method name alone does not determine risk or appropriateness. Do not convert an association into proof of its origin. Explain demanding procedures without automatically conducting them; clinician-present labels should specify the procedure and why support matters.

### T27 — Distancing, screens and observer perspectives

**Source account:** B4 describes television/movie/split-screen imagery, altered sensory emphasis and observer perspectives as ways of varying involvement. It also regards some chosen distancing as a resource. [B4, pp.102–105, 113–115]

**Application:** Voluntary distance with workable present awareness differs from unwanted disorientation or lost agency. Do not treat the word dissociation as one uniform experience. Nor promise that a screen metaphor makes traumatic-memory work safe. Ask what the person experienced, how much choice they had and what followed. Explain the technique without recreating a destabilizing episode merely to evaluate it.

### T28 — Abreaction and renegotiation

**Source account:** B4 uses abreaction broadly for re-experienced sensory/affective material, and says release alone is not the treatment goal. Levine's renegotiation emphasizes altered possibilities rather than simply reliving an event. [B4, pp.60, 121–127; B3, ch.2]

**Application:** Crying or trembling does not prove a completed therapeutic process. Ask about choice, pacing, subsequent integration and daily functioning. Conversely, emotion is not automatically damage. The app can explain a clinician's intended procedure and its context without offering solo intensive catharsis or inferring competence from a dramatic result.

### T29 — Memory: significance and verification

**Source account:** B5 warns about vividness, compelling explanations and the veracity trap. B4 contains both uncertainty safeguards and narrative-reconstruction assumptions. [B5, ch.7; B4, pp.118–132]

**Application:** Preserve uncertainty without dismissing a person's distress or known history. Separate already remembered facts, newly arising images, another person's suggestions and independently corroborated material. Relief, repeated telling and a coherent narrative do not by themselves verify an event. Do not call an apparent memory false merely because it arose in hypnosis. Legal use requires jurisdiction-specific advice, not the book's 1995 legal statements.

### T30 — Reconsolidation claims

**Source account:** In B5 chapter 8, Levine interprets laboratory memory findings as relevant to clinical change and argues for therapeutic applications; he also identifies a need for further validation. The primary laboratory paper was not independently read in this pass. [B5, ch.8]

**Application:** Explain this as the author's proposed bridge from memory research to therapy. Do not infer molecular rewriting, literal erasure or permanent cure from one emotional shift. If the user asks whether a named treatment has demonstrated reconsolidation, retrieve the relevant primary research and distinguish observed clinical outcomes from the proposed mechanism. Do not fill that evidentiary gap with confident neuroscience language.

## Relationships, spiritual meaning and practitioner evaluation

### T31 — Mismatch and repair

**Source account:** B4's Kristi case illustrates requested firmer engagement after a non-intrusive manner felt distant. B6 describes match/mismatch/rematch in actual caregiving. [B4, pp.229–233; B6, ch.10]

**Application:** Responsive work need not be perfectly attuned from the first sentence. Make correction useful: change direction and observe what follows. Adaptation to adult self-contact is an analogy, not proof of recreating infant development. Do not interpret every complaint about a practitioner or app as transference; the action or wording may actually have been wrong.

### T32 — Overprotection, dependency and the practitioner relationship

**Source account:** B4 describes clinician reactions including rescuing, excessive giving, overprotection and boundary confusion. It differentiates embodied interpersonal responsiveness from analytical judgment. [B4, pp.212–225]

**Application:** Stronger encouragement and spacious listening can each fit different people. Do not universalize either. The app cannot claim felt attunement, intuition or unreported access to the user's body. Its limits describe the service, not a verdict that the user is incapable. Independence may be the endpoint; neither the app nor a clinician should be made the compulsory authority over every inner experience.

### T33 — Touch, coordination and method boundaries

**Source account:** B4 discusses negotiating the kind and location of bodywork and reports difficulty when a bodyworker added inner-child/hypnotic work outside the agreed approach. [B4, pp.294–299]

**Application:** Consent to one service is not consent to every additional procedure. Ask what was agreed, done, reported and changed after feedback. Coordinate with other providers only with appropriate permission. Repeated disregard of an adverse response matters more than whether the practitioner uses a favored vocabulary. A self-touch suggestion and practitioner-administered touch are different situations.

### T34 — Spirituality and forgiveness

**Source account:** B2, B3 and B4 discuss spiritual experience in their own frameworks; B3 resists equating trauma with inevitable transformation. B4 does not require forgiveness as the cure. [B2, ch.6; B3, displayed pp.312–317; B4, pp.247–249, 316–328]

**Application:** Preserve chosen Higher Self/heart language without certifying its metaphysics. A person need not forgive, feel grateful for trauma or adopt a religion to make progress. Bodily intensity or a hostile inner response does not diagnose an external presence. Keep ordinary safety, accountability and boundaries compatible with meaningful spiritual exploration.

### T35 — Contemporary treatment context and credentials

**Source account:** B4's 1995 discussion calls EMDR experimental. The VA's account of the 2023 guideline recommends PE, CPT and EMDR among PTSD psychotherapies and discusses preferences and evidence limits. ASCH distinguishes credentials from a guarantee of individual competence. [B4, pp.297–298; W14; W13]

**Application:** Do not silently modernize the old book, but do not use its date-bound judgments to condemn a current practitioner. A modality's evidence is not proof of correct delivery to this person. Certification, relevant clinical training, consent, responsiveness and actual conduct answer different questions. Preserve Joel's preferences without making the consultation layer misrepresent alternatives.

### T36 — Boundaries, outcomes and escalation

**Source account:** B4 prioritizes preparation, restabilization and outside functioning, and treats failure of a setting's support as a limitation of that setting. [B4, pp.20–44, 154–155]

**Application:** Wanted distress with present awareness differs from inability to orient or stop. Comfortable refusal still ends the exercise. Assess later functioning as well as immediate experience. Urgent support is not postponed to a multiweek practice review. Do not use imagery of a positive future, safety promises or no-harm contracts as proof of low risk. Clinical/medical emergency procedures are outside this educational reference; use verified current support pathways rather than a stronger induction.

## What is not supplied by these cards

These entries support general explanation and the guide's core teaching. They are not complete courses in clinical pain management, pediatric hypnosis, eating-disorder treatment, dissociative-disorder treatment, forensic interviewing or medication. Nor do they establish the effectiveness of every NLP claim or branded technique. A request in those areas remains answerable through targeted source retrieval and appropriately bounded explanation; it should not be answered by pretending these cards contain evidence they do not.

Read CONSULTATION-AND-ACCEPTANCE.md for the application workflow and discriminating cases. Do not paste the entire reference into an induction or let a large prohibition list become the user's experience.
