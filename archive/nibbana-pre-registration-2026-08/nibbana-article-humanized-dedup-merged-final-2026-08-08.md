# The Bāhiya Sutta

The Bāhiya Sutta, [Udāna 1.10](https://suttacentral.net/ud1.10/en/anandajoti) -- sutta means teaching -- is such a beautiful sutta that I've read and repeated it hundreds of times. I'd advise you to check it out to see if you also connect with it deeply. Basically, Bāhiya was an ascetic, not a Buddhist, who thought he was enlightened, or on the path to enlightenment. A spirit came to him and told him he was nowhere near enlightenment. Bāhiya humbly asked, "Then who is enlightened?" The spirit told him, "The Buddha is enlightened, go find him."

So, Bāhiya asked ChatGPT where to find the Buddha and used Google Maps for the 3 day walk there (just kidding, idk how people did anything back then!). When he finally found the Buddha, it was bad timing. The Buddha was on almsround begging for food and didn't want to answer any questions. Bāhiya pressed him 3 times (3 being the magic number back then), saying "life is uncertain, you never know when it may end, please teach me the Dhamma briefly." Dhamma means the Buddha's teaching and the reality it points to.

This is how the Buddha finally advised Bāhiya to practice, in the pithiest instructions ever ([Ajahn Amaro's interpretive translation](https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2008/01/ajahn-amaro-on-non-duality-and.html), also published in [*Small Boat, Great Mountain*](https://www.abhayagiri.org/media/books/amaro_small_boat_great_mountain.pdf)):

*In the seen, there is only the seen,* *in the heard, there is only the heard,* *in the sensed, there is only the sensed,* *in the cognized, there is only the cognized.* *Thus you should see that* *indeed there is no thing here;* *this, Bāhiya, is how you should train yourself.* *Since, Bāhiya, there is for you* *in the seen, only the seen,* *in the heard, only the heard,* *in the sensed, only the sensed,* *in the cognized, only the cognized,* *and you see that there is no thing here,* *you will therefore see that* *indeed there is no thing there.* *As you see that there is no thing there,* *you will see that* *you are therefore located neither in the world of this,* *nor in the world of that,* *nor in any place* *betwixt the two.* *This alone is the end of suffering.*

For about 15 years I read this as the Buddha literally saying there is no self between the different sensory streams. He didn't explicitly say that. Amaro's rendering is contemplative and interpretive; the literal translations are narrower and actually clearer by targeting the deconstruction of “things” although still without my parallel flows understanding. The full translation comparison is in the [research report](https://nibbana-research.u-dont-exist.com/). So when I say "sounds hear themselves," I'm giving a direct-experience pointer inspired by "in the heard, only the heard," while extrapolating a bit beyond the original Pāli.

So, I started branching off into Buddhist books to see if I could challenge myself & go further. Later I found out that part of the uselessness was built into the monastic rules. And a monastic who makes a false attainment claim faces [*Pārājika*](https://www.dhammatalks.org/vinaya/bmc/Section0010.html)[ 4](https://www.dhammatalks.org/vinaya/bmc/Section0010.html), a defeat rule that permanently ends his status as a member of the order. So as usual, CYA (cover your ass) applies for anyone with some status to protect. Theravāda monastic-authors have an excellent reason to repeat lists, explain techniques, and remain maddeningly vague about what they themselves have actually experienced. It's like when you go to a medical doctor and ask them if they ever tried any of the medicine they prescribe, and they tell you they studied medicine.

## Then I Found The Map Past Oneness

Then I found [Awakening to Reality](https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/) (AtR) and its main author, Soh Wei Yu. Soh repeatedly described his ordinary 24/7 experience as fully disjoint "mind-body drop:" each appearance self-aware without a knower. Hearing someone report that as ordinary daily experience gave me confidence that I might be able to experience it, too. He called this, "mind-body drop," because he did not categorize nibbāna as an experience. But his description made life without a hidden knower imaginable enough to practice toward. When I described my nibbāna experience to him, he filed it under "mind-body drop." But AtR's own explanations of mind-body drop describe ordinary body, world, intention, and function continuing as a transparent or boundless "one suchness." I now think that is closer to what I’ll describe later as **partial untying** than to the full unweaving I experienced.

I owe him a great deal. One key difference I now have with AtR theory: Soh says that unification of the senses is not required experientially for functioning in the world. This really makes no sense to me at all, and I suspected for a long time that AtR practitioners were over-claiming, especially since Soh's teacher, John Tan (aka Thusness) always scolded Soh for overclaiming. One example of overclaiming that I proved definitively was Soh's claim to be in bliss 24/7. When I met him in Singapore a few years ago, I noticed he wasn't smiling at all. I asked, "So, you're in bliss 24/7?" He said, "Yes." I said, "Even now?" He said, "Well, no, not now." 😜

So I use "unification" for the functional coordination through which a person speaks, crosses a road, recognizes a friend, and answers a message, even when no separate knower is experienced. Most of my own daily life includes that ordinary unification and often feels almost like everybody else's day (well, if they're happy).

John Tan's stage map was another major help. His [public stages post](https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2007/03/thusnesss-six-stages-of-experience.html) says it is "nothing authoritative, merely for sharing purposes," and John does not present the stages as universal nor prescriptive.

I used to think that John's experience was mostly a modern theme. But now I can't understand how I thought that. After all, I already knew from DN 1, [MN 26](https://suttacentral.net/mn26/en/sujato) and [MN 36](https://suttacentral.net/mn36/en/sujato) that Buddha learned the mind-unification meditations (*jhānas*) from his pre-Buddhist teachers. While those teachers thought they were enlightened, the Buddha saw that the jhānas were not in themselves liberating, but they were very useful to develop a stable and pure mind from which to develop penetrative insight into the nature of reality. He called that type of discerning/penetrating insight vipassanā—and no, it doesn't mean [Goenka-style body-scanning](https://goenka.u-dont-exist.com/) as many people now believe.

Then we have the famous [MN 137](https://suttacentral.net/mn137/en/sujato), in which the Buddha advises to use unification of the mind to relinquish diversity, then to use non-identification to relinquish unity. And in [MN 121](https://suttacentral.net/mn121/en/sujato), at every depth of unification the meditator audits what disturbance remains and keeps finding the same answer: "just this much — that which depends on oneness." Unity itself is the last stress.

My own journey did include all of the meditative states of absorption from the four material jhānas up through the four immaterial absorptions/attainments (which are referred to in commentaries as the 5th–8th jhānas), thanks to practicing the techniques in Ajahn Brahm's book, Mindfulness, Bliss, and Beyond. They were super amazing, psychedelic-like experiences which I personally probably *did* need to have, in order to stop thinking that psychedelics were required to produce those states. In the first jhāna, my body dissolved into a cloud of euphoric atoms that were in love with each other 😶‍🌫️💗🥰 for idk how long! But I wouldn't make those "hard/powerful" jhānas a prerequisite for insight, since they aren't made such in the suttas.

The general recommendation is collectedness: gather the mind before trying to untie the unity. You have to collect the trash before you can toss it out. That's made explicit in [AN 4.170](https://suttacentral.net/an4.170/en/sujato), where Ānanda, the Buddha's main attendant, explains that all of the monastics who achieved full liberation did so with some form of cultivation of mental unification, or at least mental serenity, although not necessarily prior to insight practice.

Most people on a spiritual path these days, as before Buddha came along, don't get beyond the unification stages. If they consider themselves to be advanced, like many of the [BatGap](https://batgap.com/) and [Liberation Unleashed](https://liberationunleashed.com/) claimers, they generally settle into oneness, a luminous witness, or cosmic consciousness. They almost never talk about the suttas, which partly explains why, although the suttas can also easily be read with bias. That's one major reason I wrote this piece. The map and suttas gave me a direction beyond oneness, but they didn't open me up by themselves.

## Desperation Did The Trick

Some people go to retreats whenever they can, hoping to experience what happened to me. I didn’t have the money for that, but luckily, be broke actually helped more (as it did for the Buddha). It was a small but aggravating part of what helped me to have a severely unstable marriage that was making me feel like I had completely failed at life!

My first wife had become unexpectedly pregnant with our son about a month before I had to commit her to a psych ward when I found out how serious her mental issues were. Imagining my life as a poor guy trying to raise a kid (or more) with a woman like this was too much. My friends & family were all starting to abandon me because they couldn’t even bear the burden of her insanity. Some even had to leave the country to escape her wrathful episodes, while others offered to help me “solve the problem” in a way that I wouldn’t even talk about. I would have killed myself if I had not believed that suicide would simply lead to a worse rebirth. I suppose this is what Buddha meant when he said you should “practice like your head is on fire” in SN 56.34, SN 2.16, AN 6.20.

Speaking of avoiding a worse rebirth, I had just watched a beautiful documentary called [*For the Benefit of All Beings*](https://garchen.net/for-the-benefit-of-all-beings/) which affected me deeply. It was the biographical documentary of a Tibetan Buddhist master named Garchen Rinpoche, who spent roughly twenty years in a Chinese prison labor camp after fighting the Chinese invasion of Tibet with CIA weapons & training. He knew he was going to hell for killing people, but he felt he had no choice. And in fact, he already was in hell in his own mind of hatred.

Ironically, it was in that brutal Chinese prison camp where he found his true master. His master taught him to love the Chinese and be happy that he was growing rice for them, since the people were not their government. That’s where his practice of loving-kindness blossomed, and he became a light of love upon the world. I thought: if he could find love there, perhaps I could stop treating my own hell as an excuse.

So anyway, back to desperate little me, in bed, next to my then-wife that I was afraid of. I had just watched this beautiful documentary. I was inspired by it, as well as by Soh Wei Yu’s experience of life, and his explanation that everything is just conditions. I began chanting the beautiful Om Mane Pemme Hum mantra from the documentary while I meditated, with the intent of experiencing what Soh Wei Yu was pointing at. Eventually, I fell asleep. Then I woke up, sat up, and my entire awareness coalesced around my navel. Then it seemed to leave my body. That part had happened before, and usually produced oneness with the room, world, or universe.

This time, my awareness dispersed, or popped like a balloon! 🎈

Awareness as one thing was gone. I was gone. What remained were the room’s color-light flows, all experiencing themselves in indescribable perfection. They were disjoint, but not isolated: the flows ran into and through one another, connected everywhere without becoming one experience or one witnessed scene. Nothing observed them from behind. There was no cosmic knower celebrating its achievement. There was also no black void. The flows were infinitely more intimate, beautiful, and alive than ordinary perception, while possessing no owner at all.

I now call this body-uncontained nibbāna. It was a full unweaving because unified consciousness and the functioning body-world were gone, but its scope was no longer limited to sensations inside the body as with the fingers nibbāna event. I was not seeing the room from everywhere. There was no “I” or single place from which it was seen. The light-flows were each presenting every other flow from every corner and angle, with no overall background experience containing them. The content may still have been conditioned by the room the body had perceived before the event, so I am not claiming that it displayed all reality everywhere, but it didn’t need to.

The closest comparison that's worth mentioning is the out-of-body perception in some near-death experiences. People sometimes report seeing things from impossible angles, and a controversial subset of those reports were verified by others in the room. Those cases had an externally verifiable target, which was not part of my room experience, since I was not floating around in the experience to notice any specific details. They aren’t proof of my own experience, but they could help explain what I mean by an experience that was no longer limited by the body's vantage point. See this [systematic analysis of near-death reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10158795/), the [AWARE-II cardiac-arrest study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37423492/), and a recent [veridical-NDE evidence scale](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12573288/).

I had already experienced the formless attainment of nothingness before the room event, and I had also experienced being everything as cosmic consciousness. Neither was the liberation nibbāna was. Both are imaginable: one is an absence, while the other is ordinary unified experience expanded until it contains everything.

Nibbāna is describable but unimaginable. A fish could hear a precise description of air—how it moves and what breathing there is like—and still have no way to imagine being outside water. If nibbāna were imaginable, many people would have already imagined and written this account, and the Buddha would be one more person who imagined something and talked about it. Many psychedelic trips would be nibbana instead of just unity.

It felt entirely different from the ayahuasca ego deaths: no cosmic orgasms and no screaming. The bliss was deeper, subtler, and vastly more peaceful. I believe the [*Dhammapada*](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/KN/Dhp/Ch15.html) is right to call unbinding the foremost ease -- the traditional line often rendered “nibbāna is the highest happiness.”

I have no idea how long this lasted. Time belonged to the unified world that was absent. Eventually the ordinary body, room, witness, and biography formed again.

I now suspect a full unweaving nibbāna can be had without causing stream entry. Nibbāna becomes path fruition when it opens the irreversible "Dhamma-eye:" identity view seen through, the Dhamma intuitively understood, and as a result, fundamental doubt about the path is forever gone. Based on the suttas, it is clear that *one fruition is what establishes stream entry.* If a later event is needed before that realization happens, the earlier event was, by definition, not stream-entry fruition. It may be possible for someone to investigate an earlier experience until its meaning finally becomes irreversible without a second event, but that is not the normal preparation-then-seeing sequence I find in the suttas. The detailed sutta case is in the [research report](https://nibbana-research.u-dont-exist.com/).

For me, since that one experience, life has never felt like a Chinese finger trap as it did before. The sense of self and “my story” went from something like infinite authority to something much smaller and harder to believe. The dhamma was no longer a mystery to me, and instead of trying to find a book to explain it, I tried to find a book that could explain it as well as I could, to avoid having to write this article. Selfishness and suffering dropped dramatically. I never wanted to kill myself again. I became angry less often (even after my first wife tried to kill my family and stole my son), although anger had not been my dominant problem; I became sad far less often and felt trapped far less. By some measures I behaved less foolishly. I had not been a habitual liar, but honesty became more serious and more natural: I was less inclined to hide or leave things unsaid.

## Nibbāna Has a Volume Knob

In my experience, nibbanic unweaving varies by **degree** and **scope**.

This part is not laid out explicitly in the suttas. [AN 11.12](https://suttacentral.net/an11.12/en/sujato) says that a noble disciple can, at any time, reach ease and joy by recollecting the Buddha, the Dhamma, the *Saṅgha* -- the community of realized disciples -- their own ethical conduct and generosity, or the qualities they share with heavenly beings. In my experience, that is true, but there can also be physical loosening of the sense of unification. My percentages are playful shorthand for how much of the binding has dissolved, or equivalently how much of nibbāna has been reached. At roughly **1%** unbinding, a person remains fully functional. By roughly **5%**, function is already barely possible. Partial untying is not limited to that range.

There’s also a strange middle ground unweaving in which I could have multiple parallel experiences that each in and of themselves are bound together and thus *not nibbana,* but hinting at it. For example, becoming 2 different solid birds flying. This experience is also mentioned in the suttas, where monastics can split themselves into a thousand of themselves to clean rooms faster (Ud 5.10) until someone touches one of them, causing all to merge back to one.

The percentage language applies only to partial nibbāna. The fingers and room events were both full (100%) unweavings; body-limited versus body-uncontained describes their scope, not their depth or authenticity.

At something I would jokingly call 5% nibbāna, I become so peaceful and blissful that doing anything feels ridiculous. Once, at a [Workaway](https://www.workaway.info/) farm in Malaysia, I remained around that level for four days. I had no interest in tasks, plans, or becoming an impressive person on a farm. Talking and interacting with people gradually brought the ordinary unification back toward 0-1%, just like in Ud 5.10.

## Are disappearing flashbacks dangerous or cool?

For roughly fifteen years I have also had semi-random micro-disappearances in which the felt "I" vanishes. Each instance includes, in varying proportions, a high-pitched nāda-like inner sound, bliss, and a dissolution of the felt self. Still not unweaving, but a potential gate into it, as with any sensation that goes direct. Nāda is a Sanskrit word for sound; some Hindu contemplative traditions use it for an inwardly perceived sound. Sometimes the sound dominates. Sometimes the dissolution & bliss does. This is the closest traditional comparison I know.

The effect often begins on one side of my head zone. By relaxing & tuning attention, I can sometimes bring the other side into it. As it deepens, the bodily sense of the head or brain area can also dissolve along with "me." I've never been able to scrounge up enough money for an MRI to check if my brain is really still there, and some would argue it's completely gone! 😂 At first I wondered if I should stop riding my bike to work. What if "I" vanished in the midst of a left turn and the bicycle didn't know what to do? But, so far everything's turned out fine, Alhamdulilah! And better than fine, I feel infinitely blessed each time, and I really do thank the universe each time. It’s become a huge gratitude practice.

# What “Nibbāna” Is Actually Saying

The original usage of the word nibbāna was to describe a fire going out. [My old nibbana book](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/nibbana-book) treated "unweaving" as the word's direct etymology. That was wrong, and it’s more commonly translated now as “extinguishment,” although that obviously sounds like the nihilism pole the Buddha was trying to avoid.

Buddhaghosa wrote a famous systematization of the Buddha's teaching in the fifth century, called the [*Visuddhimagga*](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/nanamoli/PathofPurification2011.pdf). In it, he derives nibbāna from *vāna*, "binding" or "fastening," based on the understanding that craving joins, binds, and laces one becoming to the next. [Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu](https://www.dhammatalks.org/books/MindLikeFire/Section0007.html) agrees with this explanation of nibbāna as more fitting, even if less academically strict. He traces it back to the early fire image also. A fire depends on fuel (*upādāna*, the same word used for clinging or sustenance), and it becomes free when that binding to fuel ends.

That is why Ṭhānissaro's sutta translations use "unbinding" for nibbāna. My argument for the meaning of nibbāna will be described in the next sections. Like Buddhaghosa and Ṭhānissaro, I believe the strict etymological definition is not as helpful as an interpretive one for truly understanding the word.

The suttas themselves already speak in tangles, knots, and nets. [SN 1.23](https://suttacentral.net/sn1.23/en/sujato) describes a "tangle within" and a "tangle without," then says the *arahants'* (fully enlightened ones') tangle is disentangled. [Dhammapada 90](https://suttacentral.net/dhp90/en/sujato) says the liberated person has abandoned every *gantha* (knot, tie, or bond). [Dhammapada 180](https://suttacentral.net/dhp180/en/sujato) calls craving *jālinī* (like a net, ensnaring). Unbinding and disentangling are deeply native ways to describe liberation from the binding up of the sense streams.

What came apart in my "room experience" was the fastening of separate sensory flows which ordinarily cohere into one established consciousness, one self, one world, and renewed existence. The room looked as though it were unweaving, and the suttas' tangles, knots, and nets make that visual metaphor feel less accidental.

My old "hard nibbāna / soft nibbāna" language was also my taxonomy. But here's a better breakdown of how I'd explain it now:

- **Partial nibbāna / partial untying:** some unification loosens while enough of the body-world remains for recognition, desire, memory, meaning... basically, life in the normal sense is still possible, but not as tight.
- **Full unweaving nibbāna:** the unification that creates one functioning and suffering-capable body-world disappears completely.
- **Body-limited nibbāna:** a full unweaving limited by what the living body has made available, as in the fingers experience.
- **Body-uncontained nibbāna:** a full unweaving no longer organized around the body's viewpoint or sensory aperture, as in the room experience.
- **Reassembly:** the ordinary body, scene, memory, and ability to function return.
- **Parinibbāna:** the same full unweaving without reassembly, i.e. without rebirth, because the body is dead. The living breakthrough to nibbāna is what makes the final direction knowable. [Iti 44](https://suttacentral.net/iti44/en/sujato) agrees on the difference between nibbana & parinibbana. It also shows the limit: a living arahant still has preferences and experiences pleasure and pain, but won't be reborn again. Thus, in this article, *saṃsāric* means this constructed, dukkha-capable functioning itself, not only the craving that produces another birth.

## Nibbāna is a meditative state and attainment

Many Buddhists love to tell me nibbāna isn't an experience, it's just an achievement or attainment, the loss of the fetters (Buddhist deadly sins). The suttas disagree, tho..

[AN 9.47](https://suttacentral.net/an9.47/en/sujato) is literally titled the Sandiṭṭhikanibbāna Sutta, which translates to "directly visible nibbāna." Someone asks, "How is nibbāna directly visible?", and the answer is: through meditation, step by step. As meditation progresses, when perception and feeling cease and the taints are destroyed by wisdom, that is directly visible nibbāna "in the definitive sense."

Arahants also went into nibbāna meditations, and said so. In [AN 10.7](https://suttacentral.net/an10.7/en/sujato), Sāriputta describes a samādhi meditative state where he wasn't perceiving earth, water, or any normal object at all. One perception only — "the cessation of becoming is nibbāna" — arising and ceasing like the flames of a fire. The Buddha describes the same samādhi in [AN 10.6](https://suttacentral.net/an10.6/en/sujato): perceiving "this is peaceful, this is sublime... nibbāna" and nothing else, with parallels at [AN 11.7](https://suttacentral.net/an11.7/en/sujato) and [AN 3.32](https://suttacentral.net/an3.32/en/sujato). (Bhikkhu Brahmāli thinks these describe perceiving an *idea about* nibbāna rather than nibbāna itself — [his paper is here](https://wiswo.org/books/niknib/), and I deal with that reading in the [research report](https://nibbana-research.u-dont-exist.com/).)

The contact language is further proof they're talking about an experience. In [AN 6.46](https://suttacentral.net/an6.46/en/thanissaro), monastics touch the "deathless element" with the body. In [AN 9.43-45](https://suttacentral.net/an9.43/en/sujato), the "body-witness" touches the formless liberations, and the one "liberated both ways" has had cessation-contact + destroyed taints. In [AN 9.34](https://suttacentral.net/an9.34/en/sujato), Sāriputta treats the absence of feeling in cessation as pleasure. And [Ud 8.1](https://suttacentral.net/ud8.1/en/anandajoti) calls nibbāna *tadāyatanaṃ* ("that sphere"), using *āyatana*, the same word the canon uses for the meditative spheres. You don't "touch" and "enter" a meditative sphere which is really just a philosophical abstraction.

## How Contact Builds a World

The way I understand how my "world" of coherent experience is constructed starts with my finger and room experiences.

Blissful color-flows were each present on their own, with no witness behind them. These appearances or awareness-flows meet locally, like a [MeetUp](https://www.meetup.com/) group (although MeetUp actually banned me for trying to organize a study group about this, which is why I'm just a Substack author instead of the next [Jim Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Jones), perhaps). I call those MeetUps "initial contact." That is my term, broader than the Pāli word for contact, *phassa*. Those local MeetUps can help organize a sense door and the sense-consciousness that goes with it.

[SN 12.44](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN12_44.html) gives the canonical sequence: *eye and forms* condition *eye-consciousness*; the meeting of all three is **contact**. That *contact* begets *feeling*, which triggers *craving*, then *clinging*. And clinging to experiences leads to clinging to life itself, which brings us to *becoming* and *birth.* There we have it, the whole mass of suffering, which the sutta calls "the origination of the world" -- sutta-speak for saṃsāra.

I like to use awareness to mean bare, non-reflective awareness as the appearance that it is. So the experience of blue is just that. Consciousness I think of as awareness reflecting back on itself: "I'm blue!" Pāli doesn't give me two separate nouns for this distinction, but [DN 11](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/DN/DN11.html) and [MN 49](https://suttacentral.net/mn49/pli/ms) describe *viññāṇaṃ anidassanaṃ anantaṃ sabbato pabhaṃ*—unmanifest awareness, without end and luminous all around—where the elements and mind-and-body (*nāmarūpa*, often mistranslated as name-and-form) find no footing. So the texts do distinguish that from ordinary sense-consciousness (*viññāṇaṃ*) established when one thing perceives another.

However, I think this unmanifest awareness should have been "unmanifest awareness*es*" even though that sounds very strange. Without that precision, you get Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu, who starts off right in [*The Mind Like Fire Unbound*](https://www.dhammatalks.org/books/MindLikeFire/) and [*The Wings to Awakening*](https://www.dhammatalks.org/books/Wings/), saying the mind fabricates the experienced cosmos, including time and the present. When that fabrication stops, what he says remains is exactly that copy-pasted line: “consciousness without feature, without end, luminous all around.” He can't imagine parallel flows as nibbana because the suttas say nibbana lacks “differentiation.”

I agree that nobody is separating or counting anything in nibbāna. But the light-flows weren’t one featureless consciousness either. Not because my mind was there busy counting or differentiating them, but simply because they weren't identical by nature. I explain the fuller comparison in the [research report](https://nibbana-research.u-dont-exist.com/). The one-versus-many question is ultimately what differentiates Buddhism from prior teachings and new-age spirituality. What makes it important is that unification itself is what gives the ground for suffering to land.

## Ignorance is the root

So why would already-perfect flows ever stay tied up in an ever-expanding ball of suffering yarn? I used to think multisensory binding was the root of samsara, based on the Amaro translation of the Bahiya sutta, even though that wasn’t even my actual experiential insight. Now I realize there’s nothing wrong with the natural random tangling & untangling of stringy energy flows as long as they don’t keep tangling. “Wise” (or lucky) flows tangle and release freely, the way fields, or energy strings in String Theory, bind into quantum particles which then annihilate each other back into stringy energy flows. And the level of suffering depends on the degree of the tangling. So, in the experience of first jhana, all your atoms bounce around in this super euphoric amorphous state. That’s not peace, but it’s also not suffering yet. But if they forget the beauty of peaceful release, they keep tangling for more euphoric binding. As Buddha said, at some point we emerge as blissful self-luminous, still joyous beings, but the craving for euphoric merging leads us to start munching on that yummy dark solid earth and become solid ourselves. Then we start hoarding food and having sex and eventually we develop existentialist philosophy, and now you have anti-natalists blaming their parents for bringing them into the world!

The root of the non-stop tangling problem the Buddha names is ignorance (avijjā), the first link of dependent origination. It's described in ([SN](https://suttacentral.net/sn12.2/en/sujato) 12.2 and [MN](https://suttacentral.net/mn9/en/sujato) 9): not knowing dukkha, how it's caused by craving, that it can end, nor how to end it. My plain-language version of the part people miss: ignorance is not knowing that the euphoria of merging isn't actually happiness -- not compared with the bliss and peace of release.

I’m not gonna make the free-love analogy you might expect, because that gets messy. 😘

But you can see this same process happen with money. Children are happy with no money. Then they get a little, and suddenly there's some power in their pocket, and they like it. When the money runs out, instead of just going back to being happy, they’re unhappy and want more. They grow into adults who work like hell for more and more money while getting less and less happy. The happiness was never coming from the money, but they never knew where it was coming from, so they chase the wrong condition harder and harder. Nobody chooses that ignorance; we all logically start from it.

My dad once told me he was hitchhiking, and had only a nickel left in his pocket, which was causing him terrible anxiety. Someone asked for money and he gave away his last nickel, and suddenly felt immensely free!

The Buddha said sensual pleasure itself works this way. In [MN 75](https://suttacentral.net/mn75/en/sujato) he compares it to a leper finding relief by scorching his itchy lesions over hot coals, and feeling as pleasure what a healed man would feel as burning.

The Buddha even split the accounting cleanly in [SN 22.22](https://suttacentral.net/sn22.22/en/sujato): the five aggregates are the burden; craving is the picking-it-up. The tangle is heavy by nature -- that's why even an arahant still feels the first dart -- and craving is why we keep carrying it. And physics agrees the burden is literally heavy: [quarks contribute less than 1% of a proton's mass — the other \~99% is the energy of the gluon binding itself](https://home.cern/news/news/physics/other-99). Matter weighs what its binding weighs. Light weighs nothing.

The newly formed field extends itself through time, building the "origin of the world" from [SN 12.44](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN12_44.html) toward becoming and birth.

Up til now I’ve been a bit imprecise actually about how a bunch of flows can add up to one *me*: they don't melt into a new little soul. It works like the nun Vajirā's chariot in [SN 5.10](https://suttacentral.net/sn5.10/en/bodhi): assemble the parts and "chariot" applies; assemble the aggregates and "a being" applies. The oneness is *experientially real* -- you really do experience one world -- but it's the oneness of an assembly, and the "me" it seems to happen to is a part of the assembly, not the owner standing outside it.

The main question for me and for the Buddha, is whether the capacity for suffering (dukkha) remains. Taking a look at studies on automatic action and [split-brain patients](https://nibbana-research.u-dont-exist.com/), it's clear that people can function without feeling an inner controller, yet they still feel pain. Anguish requires more reflective consciousness than pain. [SN 36.6](https://suttacentral.net/sn36.6/en/sujato) describes two darts, where the ordinary person struck by pain also sorrows, grieves, etc, while the fully liberated person (arahant) feels the bodily dart alone when not in nibbāna.

But don't get it twisted: functioning on autopilot, split-brain dissociation, or even a true no-self realization doesn't make anyone an arahant. Pain without anguish is a symptom of enlightenment, not the definition. An arahant is defined by the complete release of even the subtlest unconscious belief in or craving for unified existence -- the full loss of the fetters -- which is what ends rebirth.

And it doesn't take multiple sense streams together to suffer: one stream is enough. Even if all that remained were a single channel of pleasure/pain, that still leaves pain in there. The pain, like a plane, is able to land because there's somewhere solid to land. During full unweaving, no assembled system remains that could suffer, lack, desire, gain, lose, recognize, or do anything. Supreme bliss is what seems to be left when the capacity for suffering is released (even though you might assume neutrality would be left): as Sāriputta put it in [AN 9.34](https://suttacentral.net/an9.34/en/sujato), nibbāna is pleasant precisely where nothing is felt.

# The Myriad Things Experience Themselves

Dōgen, the 13th century Japanese founder of Soto Zen, described awakening with almost exactly this reversal. In the Tanahashi/Aitken translation of his *Genjōkōan*, he says:

“To carry yourself forward and experience myriad things is delusion. That myriad things come forth and experience themselves is awakening.”

The translation is available in full [here](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/GenjoKoan_Aitken.htm). Other legitimate translations say that myriad things “illuminate,” “verify,” or “actualize” the self. So, again with translations we can cherry pick. But still, Tanahashi’s version remains a serious interpretive translation, and it points beautifully toward the same reversal I’m describing. I'm generally suspicious of later *Mahāyāna* scriptures which came around maybe 400-700 years after the Buddha's death and put new teachings into his mouth.. Still, when Dōgen gives me one this good, I I’ll take it.

Even the old Zen riddle (*koan*) about a tree falling in the forest brings us back to the Bāhiya Sutta: without anybody there to hear it, does it make a sound? The question challenges whether sound requires a separate hearer. My answer is that auditory appearing does not require a little person behind it. “The sound hears itself” is imperfect language, but ordinary grammar is even more committed to the imaginary owner.

But then what are the appearances themselves? Here comes the most speculative part of my theory: light.

# The Theory of Light Flows

In my experience, per the Bāhiya instructions, any sensation can be a gateway into nibbāna: one apparent sound can break apart into the many waves composing it before hearing disappears entirely. As the room unweaving experience deepened, all senses disappeared, except for luminous bliss flows. Maybe this is why they call it "enlightenment" instead of "in smelly mint"? 😄

The order of disappearance of the other sensations makes sense to me. Thought goes first, since nothing is referencing anything to anything anymore. Sound, smell, taste and touch all require solid molecular interactions, but all the solidity had been unwoven.

Ultimately, in Physics 101 we learned that nothing is really solid when you look close enough, all matter is actually just electromagnetic interactions. But even before modern physics, in [SN 22.95](https://suttacentral.net/sn22.95/en/sujato), the Buddha explicitly deconstructs the idea of "solid matter" (*rūpa*):

"Suppose a person saw a great lump of foam floating down the Ganges... If they inspected it and examined it carefully, it would appear empty, hollow, and insubstantial. In the same way, whatever physical form there is... a monk inspects it and examines it carefully. To them, it appears empty, hollow, and insubstantial."

This also explains the famous quote by the founding philosopher of Mahayana Buddhism, Nāgārjuna. He famously states in *MMK* 25.19-20 "there is not the slightest difference between saṃsāra and nirvāṇa," and "the limit of the one is the limit of the other." Exactly. It's saṃsāra when the uncompounded light-flows are established as one temporal, material body-world. It's nibbāna when that establishment fully unweaves.

## Light Language Like Chantress Seba Never Seen Before!

Light language is all over the suttas. [AN 1.49-52](https://suttacentral.net/an1.49-52/en/sujato) calls the purified mind radiant or luminous (not to say the mind is nibbāna, but a purified mind is needed to dissolve into nibbāna). As the "defilements" of the mind are washed away, and as the "dust" is washed from the eyes, the light-flows start to shine through, and when unification fully unweaves, no mind remains to be luminous. Only the luminous flows. [DN 27](https://suttacentral.net/dn27/en/sujato) gives the same sequence in cosmic-story form: self-luminous beings crave the dark earth and become increasingly material. Light first, matter later.

That's my whole theory wrapped up in a bedtime story.

The DN 11/MN 49 passage is also the closest suttic description I know of nibbāna as light-flows, provided you read its "awareness" in the plural.

And the last verse of [Ud 1.10](https://suttacentral.net/ud1.10/en/anandajoti), the Bāhiya sutta, repeats the "no footing" image, then adds that stars don't shine, the sun and moon give no light, and yet there is no darkness. Well, if nibbāna is light flows, of course there'd be no darkness. No sun or moon needed, because this light isn't produced by luminous objects. Nothing finds footing, because no compounded matter has been established. Light can be perceived as the material world, but it isn't an object in the world.

## Go Into The Light

From Near Death Experiences, many people know that there's this "go into the light" theme as a transition at death. Some Christian gnostic traditions and the Tibetan Book of the Dead even try to tell you which light to go into and what to say to avoid getting reborn again.

Tibetan Dzogchen Buddhism comes at ultimate reality from a more unified lens than me, but it agrees that the essence of the elements is light, and that matter is just that light condensed. They even claim their masters' bodies actually dissolve into rainbow lights at death. There are a few 20th-century accounts of this; one was [looked into on the ground by a Catholic priest](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/252752/rainbow-body-and-resurrection-by-francis-v-tiso/), another was [written down by the dying master's own nephew](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/219616/rainbow-body-by-chogyal-namkhai-norbu/) (Namkhai Norbu Rinpoche). (The full comparison with Dzogchen is in the [research report](https://nibbana-research.u-dont-exist.com/).)

## But isn't nibbāna asaṅkhata, or "unconditioned"?

In [Ud 8.3](https://suttacentral.net/ud8.3/en/anandajoti), nibbāna is defined as *asaṅkhata*. Now, according to the sutta translations most people are reading, from Bhikkhus Sujato and Bodhi, *asaṅkhata* means "unconditioned/independent." That would trash my theory of light flows, since light is conditioned like anything else. Lucky for me & you, it turns out that is an obvious mistranslation (which some, like Thanissaro Bhikkhu, avoid using). Here's why:

In the context of various suttas, such as the defining [AN 3.47](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN3_47.html) (which says *saṅkhata* things are seen to arise, disappear, and decay while remaining), the active manufacturing of experience in [SN 22.79](https://suttacentral.net/sn22.79/en/sujato), and the structural metaphors of decay and assembly in [DN 16](https://suttacentral.net/dn16/en/sujato) and [SN 5.10](https://suttacentral.net/sn5.10/en/sujato) — *saṅkhata* could only mean "compounded," "assembled," or "fabricated" rather than just abstractly "conditioned." The etymology is also clear: the prefix *saṁ-* (together, completely) + the verbal root *kṛ* (to make, do, build), the past participle *saṅkhata* equals that which has been put together or constructed out of parts.

## If nibbāna is "unfabricated", is light also?

Light is energy in the electromagnetic (EM) field spectrum, and energy can't be created or destroyed. Individual photons do get [emitted and absorbed](https://www.energy.gov/science/doe-explainsphotons), but a photon has no parts — nothing in it can decay or be disassembled. So light isn't fabricated, even if there is a factory that made your light bulbs.

If nibbāna is uncompounded, you don't get blank nothingness. You get radiant, blissful, unestablished processes with no body-world, no observer, no timeline, and no assembled objects. Which was my experience.

## Light is unestablished, with no time, no reference point, like nibbāna

Relativity Theory hands me a serendipitous parallel here. At light speed (for a photon) [there's no time or space](https://interactivetextbooks.tudelft.nl/classical-mechanics-and-special-relativity/content/ChXX0_FourMomentum.html#rest-frame-of-a-photon). Light is the invariant that everything else measures its space and time against. My full unweaving also had no observer, viewpoint, time, nor common reference background space.

The suttas also keep calling nibbāna unestablished or unsupported. [Ud 8.1](https://suttacentral.net/ud8.1/en/anandajoti) says it has no coming, going, standing, passing away, or arising. [SN 12.38](https://suttacentral.net/sn12.38/en/bodhi) and [SN 22.53](https://suttacentral.net/sn22.53/en/bodhi) tie unestablished consciousness to the ending of renewed existence. And [SN 12.64](https://suttacentral.net/sn12.64/en/thanissaro) picks, of all possible images, a beam of light with nowhere to land! Meanwhile quantum physics says a photon's state is not established as one definite outcome until it's measured. The whole dream of saṃsāra is the unestablished becoming established as a definite, relational world.

What physics can never demonstrate from the outside, but meditation can disclose from within, is that **light sees itself**. Give light a mirror through which to see its own beauty, and then you’ve got reflective consciousness establishing a mind.

If that is what the texts were pointing to, how did the point disappear while the words survived?

# How the Experiential Key Can Fade

After 15 years of reading popular & obscure Buddhist books, plus countless hours on forums like SuttaCentral.net, I never once found a positive definition of what nibbāna actually is. Everyone says what nibbāna does (liberates), what it ends (rebirth, consciousness, defilements), what it isn't (anything), and then gives me the aggregates, sense bases, dependent origination, and no-self again. But what is the experience? What are all those lists pointing to? Why is it no Theravāda monk ever told me anything useful despite devoting their life to this?

Part of the problem: the Buddha, or those who recorded him, left the puzzle pieces scattered across several drawers. [Ud 1.10](https://suttacentral.net/ud1.10/en/anandajoti) gives the pithiest pointer to direct sensations as the gateway to nibbana. [SN 12.44](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN12_44.html) gives the contact chain that builds "the world." [SN 12.67](https://suttacentral.net/sn12.67/en/sujato) has consciousness and mind-and-body leaning on each other like two bundles of reeds -- pull either away and the other falls, like Jenga. [DN 15](https://suttacentral.net/dn15/en/sujato) lays out the full dependent-origination account, and [MN 140](https://suttacentral.net/mn140/en/sujato) walks through the formless absorption unity states and then beyond unity. It's also one more way the Buddha's teaching is a middle path: consciousness-first (Advaita, new-age) and matter-first (materialism) both get refused, because the two keep each other standing like those reeds. I put the puzzle pieces back in the box in the [research report](https://nibbana-research.u-dont-exist.com/). 😛

Those books did at least teach me that everything arises from conditions. That left two elephants in the room: does a self exist, and if choices are conditioned, what could free will mean?

[AN 3.61](https://suttacentral.net/an3.61/en/bodhi) answers the second question directly: conditioned choices still matter because they become conditions for what happens next. Most Buddhists I've spoken with didn't even understand the question.

Regarding the self, the Buddha is famous for teaching *anattā*, or non-self. But many people remain confused about whether he denied a self or merely denied that conditioned experience is the true self. In [SN 44.10](https://suttacentral.net/sn44.10/en/sujato), he refuses to answer the question directly because either answer would confuse an already confused person further. But in many other suttas he specifically states that self-view itself is the problem, and the self is just composed of changing aggregates.

And in [AN 6.61](https://suttacentral.net/an6.61/en/sujato), he was misunderstood even by many of his senior monks with regard to dependent origination, and his clarification remained cryptically terse.

The suttas clearly show that the Buddha succeeded in liberating thousands of people, and the Vinaya says [500 fully enlightened monks took part in the first council after his death](https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd21?view=normal\&lang=en). The problem is that as the people who directly understood nibbāna, no-self, and dependent origination became rarer, the words kept getting interpreted by people who were still experiencing one unified consciousness and one world, and the hints stopped landing. I'm reminded of [Ud 6.4](https://suttacentral.net/ud6.4/en/sujato), when the Buddha talks about how sectarian teachers quarrel over incomplete understandings of reality, much like blind men who each touch a different part of an elephant and argue over its true nature. And the degeneration is even visible in that first council of 500 arahants, because they excluded the female arahants and crticized Ananda for asking the Buddha to admit women to the sangha.

The canon itself says this kind of degradation was already happening in the Buddha's lifetime. In [SN 16.13](https://suttacentral.net/sn16.13/en/sujato), the arahant Mahā Kassapa asks why there are more training rules but fewer monks reaching final knowledge. The Buddha says counterfeit Dhamma gradually makes the true Dhamma disappear.

And the funniest part is that the words themselves may have survived surprisingly well. The discourses were transmitted orally for centuries, but they were transmitted communally, and the separately preserved early collections agree on an enormous amount. There are variants, rearrangements, and later material. Still, the words surviving is not the same as the meaning surviving. Even having the right pieces to the puzzle doesn't ensure you can put them together to see the picture on the box.

# A Map Can Save Years -- and Create Counterfeits

That leaves a practical problem. In [DN 3](https://suttacentral.net/dn3/en/sujato) and [MN 56](https://suttacentral.net/mn56/en/sujato), the Buddha waits until a student's mind is "ready, pliable, rid of hindrances, elated, and confident" before giving the teaching that produces the Dhamma-eye, the breakthrough vision associated with stream entry. What happens when a stage map lets anyone skip straight to the answer?

These days, somebody can possibly pull the trigger like I did, via a stage map from AtR or [Dharma Overground](http://dharmaoverground.org/), but just out of curiosity, competition, or the hope of claiming an attainment.

And nibbāna is genuinely needed as the "trigger" for stream entry. If nibbāna is never experienced, then what exactly was recognized? Where did the intuitive certainty about the Dhamma come from? Again, from [MN 56](https://suttacentral.net/mn56/en/sujato) (and others), we see that when one attains stream entry, the "stainless, immaculate vision of the Dhamma" arises in them. It's a powerful experience of liberation.

But without the "ammunition" of accumulated desire, practice, and readiness for a radical insight aimed at ending rebirth and attachment to self-view, it only makes a cool sound. The event can get filed under "awesome experience," yet the old view is never overwritten. That's why Buddha taught [loving-kindness meditation](https://love.u-dont-exist.com/) and the [Noble Eightfold Path](https://suttacentral.net/sn45.42-47/en/bodhi) -- ethical conduct, meditation, and wisdom about ending rebirth and self-view.

[Dhammapada 276](https://suttacentral.net/dhp276/en/buddharakkhita) says awakened teachers point out the path while practitioners make the effort. [MN 38](https://suttacentral.net/mn38/en/sujato) speaks of the Dhamma as something to be known, seen, and realized for oneself. [DN 16](https://suttacentral.net/dn16/en/sujato) pairs reliance on oneself with reliance on the Dhamma, and [MN 22](https://suttacentral.net/mn22/en/sujato) compares the Dhamma to a raft used for crossing rather than an identity to drag around after reaching shore.

The more detailed the map becomes, the more curiosity, honesty, and empirical self-scrutiny it demands. An unprepared reader can use the answer key to exalt a psychedelic rupture, depersonalization, derealization, trauma, or a temporary no-self state into stream entry.

Secrecy (as in [Tibetan Buddhism's secret teachings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sg-5CDOcsTM)) won't solve this. Readiness warnings, anti-counterfeit framing, ethical and relational outcome checks, and personal verification are the best protection I know.

Awakening to Reality's map saved me, but I can't recommend it to others, for a number of reasons besides their lack of clarity about nibbāna versus daily life, as mentioned before.

It seems to me that AtR was largely Soh's semi-authorized editorial creation from John's journals. John supplied much of the insight while Soh systematized it into a searchable, teachable, generalized stage map. Soh sent me his many years of chats with John, and from that I gathered that John was somewhat ambivalent about teaching in general, but maybe like me, he felt called to do so because nobody else was teaching anything of substance. As such, I think John allowed Soh to function as his mouthpiece. That's a smart arrangement: the student can promote the teacher while the teacher keeps low-key describing his own posts as "a simple sharing" or "nothing authoritative."

I don't want to caricature AtR as just a Buddhist version of Google Maps. Their blog also discusses [ethics and the Noble Eightfold Path](https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2022/06/the-awakening-to-reality-practice-guide.html), and John was often correcting Soh on [the difference between an experience, an insight, a view, and stabilization](https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2022/06/someone-asked-about-pemako-stages.html).

On the other hand, Soh did seem to fancy himself as the map king. Ironically, he became the one who would "authenticate" people's stages of awakening, despite John Tan saying it was only his own experience and that awakening was "self-authenticated by doubtless realization."

I stayed for 6 days with one of the people Soh authenticated as a stream-enterer, Frank Yang, and I can tell you for sure he didn't even understand the very basics of what Buddha taught (including the word "conditions", which Soh was also confused about when he tried to refute determinism), and Frank got angry when I questioned his understandings. Although I can't fault a YouTube star for not understanding conditions, since around that same time period, even Āyasmā Ariyadhammika, the head teacher at the [Theravāda Buddhist center](http://sasanarakkha.org/) that gives "advanced training" to monastics, didn't understand "conditions" when I met him. He thanked me when I explained that literally *everything* is conditions, and it seemed like he had a "lightbulb moment."

Frank did feel that he suffered much less after softening his identity, so that's valuable, but it doesn't equate to stream entry. He was a good, if shallow, AtR parrot, and he was becoming somewhat of an exalted guru thanks to [his beautiful, crazy YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@frankyang) and his charisma.

# Stream Entry and the Dream Running Out

Stream entry is only the beginning of Theravāda's four-stage map. Once-returning, non-returning, and arahantship come after it. For this article, the first stage is the one that matters. Craving, aversion, and ill will can weaken a lot at stream entry because the self-view behind them has been seen through, but most fetters remain.

[AN 10.92](https://suttacentral.net/an10.92/en/sujato) shows that stream-enterers gain confidence in the Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha, ethical restraint, and direct understanding of causality. [AN 3.87](https://suttacentral.net/an3.87/en/sujato) ties stream-entry to the ending of identity view, uncertainty about the path, and grasping at habits and practices as sufficient in themselves. It calls the stream-enterer certain, headed for awakening, and no longer destined for states of woe. The later stages of awakening keep weakening passion, aversion, and delusion until the remaining fetters and deeper defilements end.

## Practice can carry into a future life

[AN 4.191](https://suttacentral.net/an4.191/en/sujato) says that a monk who has theoretical understanding of the teachings may be reborn among heavenly beings even if he dies unmindfully. The Dhamma can then return to mind slowly, and after that he can quickly reach stream entry ("the destination").

And we find a stronger guarantee in [SN 25.1](https://suttacentral.net/sn25.1/en/sujato). Someone who has fully accepted the Dhamma through faith or reflective deliberation is said to be irreversibly approaching stream entry by the time of death, and incapable of doing evil deeds that would lead to a rebirth in a lower realm.

## Streamy-entry requires more than a 2-factor authentication key

From the AtR debacle, I've learned that stream-entry self-authentication *and* authentication by someone else are both problematic. I think we also need to use some rubric like this, although it isn't sufficient by itself since it only describes the after-effects:

- Do other people notice a durable reduction in your selfishness?
- Can you be content with less? For example, the Buddha advised his monastics to wear robes they collected from dead corpses ([Kd 8.11](https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd8/en/brahmali)), and to use their own fermented urine ([Kd 1.64](https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd1/en/brahmali)) and feces ([Kd 6.2](https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd6/en/brahmali)) as medicine.
- Are you more joyful and more enjoyable to be around without turning into a people-pleaser, as in [MN 89](https://suttacentral.net/mn89/en/sujato)? Regarding Soh Wei Yu's false claim to be in bliss 24/7, that is not what a stream-enterer should expect. [AN 11.12](https://suttacentral.net/an11.12/en/sujato) says a noble disciple can reach ease and joy through recollection, while in [MN 75](https://suttacentral.net/mn75/en/bodhi) the Buddha himself said he felt pain when perceiving what ordinary people felt as sensual pleasures. So it isn't a simple equation of enlightenment making you more blissful.
- Do you now find the Dhamma intuitively coherent rather than merely memorized?
- Do you know that rebirth is now on the way out, with the same certainty that everyone knows they will die one day?
- Can you still recognize trauma without floating above it?

That last test matters because a stream-enterer still has most fetters. They still need to do reparenting like other folks, and even a genuine breakthrough does not turn somebody into a therapist, let alone a saint.

## Ending the dream of saṃsāra

Maybe the greatest prize for the stream-enterer is their "release date:" [AN 3.88](https://suttacentral.net/an3.88/en/sujato) says there are at most seven further lifetimes before final awakening. There is [a lot of modern academic evidence for rebirth](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0AtTM9hgCDw). But who has those lives if no permanent self exists? Buddhists don't imagine a soul traveling from body to body, since that would be a belief in an unchanging self-essence. Instead, we understand rebirth as causal continuity without a traveler (often called a mind-stream): the habit of craving and clinging leads to becoming, which becomes birth. The later Buddhist [*Milindapañha*](https://suttacentral.net/mil3.5.5/en/kelly)[ 3.5.5](https://suttacentral.net/mil3.5.5/en/kelly) compares the process of rebirth to one lamp lighting another. The flame doesn't migrate, yet the second lighting is not unrelated to the first.

## Why nibbāna is "awakening"

The lucid-dream comparison from earlier also shapes how I understand the seven-life limit said to be had by the stream-enterer. Once the dream is known as a dream, it becomes naturally difficult to remain stuck in it, similar to how hard it is to be absorbed in a movie when the lights go on and you remember you're in a theatre. If you've ever had a lucid dream, you know what I'm talking about.

I used to attribute "life is like a dream" directly to the historical Buddha. The famous stanza I loved actually comes from the later Mahāyāna [*Diamond Sutra*](https://read.84000.co/translation/toh16.html), which puts some words in his mouth, although the early Pāli texts offer closely related images. [SN 22.95](https://suttacentral.net/sn22.95/en/sujato) compares form to foam, feeling to a bubble, perception to a mirage, and consciousness to a magic trick. [Dhammapada 170](https://suttacentral.net/dhp170/en/sujato) asks us to see the world as a bubble and mirage.

The Diamond Sutra verse is still beautiful. Here is a widely circulated English rendering:

So should one view all of the fleeting world:

A star at dawn, a bubble in a stream,

A flash of lightning in a summer cloud,

A flickering lamp, a phantom, and a dream.

## What Counts as Success, Confusion, or Danger

Here's what success actually feels like for me when the loosening works:

My happy head and happy feet both feel vivid as simultaneous yet independent experience-flows. If my attention is bouncing back and forth, that's still reg mode, meaning I might need to keep trying the untying, but maybe a bit less trying and more relaxx-maxxing.

Several sensations can remain present without becoming "my body."

The witness gets hard to locate, and an unusually deep relief or bliss shows up.

I might get just a taste of that or it might feel like more.

If it still feels like one body watched by the Watcher, nothing has loosened yet. Numb, dull, or foggy might be indicators that you're sleepy and it's nap time.

The farther unification loosens, the less useful you become for normal tasks. That's why I’d suggest starting this practice while lying down… certainly not while driving, cycling, swimming, cooking over fire, crossing traffic, working near heights, operating machinery, skydiving, or flying your pet dragon.

# Bāhiya Got Enlightened and Then a Cow Killed Him

I told you before about the lovely Bahiya sutta. Bāhiya hears [one short instruction](https://suttacentral.net/ud1.10/en/sujato), becomes fully liberated, and then instead of appearing on book tours, he gets killed by a runaway cow. The Buddha later declares him totally unbound, never to be born again, and the person who [grasped the Dhamma faster than anyone else](https://suttacentral.net/an1.209/en/sujato).

Some people might read that and think Buddhism didn’t work that well for poor ol’ Bāhiya. But actually, the Buddhist end goal is to end existence itself, and since Bāhiya was not set to be reborn, he reached the end goal. Essentially, he untied and was released into *parinibbāna*.

It’s worth noting that Bāhiya was not just lucky to get enlightened so fast. [Ud 1.10](https://suttacentral.net/ud1.10/en/sujato) shows his urgency and immediate understanding, while the later [Bāhiya Apadāna](https://suttacentral.net/tha-ap538/en/walters) says he had aspired and practiced toward that quick comprehension for a hundred thousand eons. Most people are not as ready as Bāhiya was, so don’t be surprised if you can’t leap from complex PTSD, self-hatred, or a chaotic life directly into complete liberation just because I told you a sound heard itself. The Buddha’s [Noble Eightfold Path](https://suttacentral.net/sn45.42-47/en/bodhi) still needs to be followed, and I would mainly highlight the importance of ethical practice, [loving-kindness](https://love.u-dont-exist.com/), [inner-child reparenting](https://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/), [healthy relationships](http://romance.u-dont-exist.com/), [a supportive practice community](https://community.u-dont-exist.com/).

# Come and See for Yourself

I don't want Bāhiya's cow to get the last word. In [AN 3.55](https://suttacentral.net/an3.55/en/sujato), the Buddha says that when greed, hatred, and delusion are abandoned, a person does not plan to hurt themselves or anyone else and experiences mental peace and bliss. The formula that follows describes nibbāna as directly visible, not bounded by time, inviting one to come and see, leading inward, and personally realized by the wise.

Here's an invitation to ponder: In [SN 12.61](https://suttacentral.net/sn12.61/en/sujato), the Blessed One says that the mind arises as one thing and ceases as another, day and night, and in [AN 1.48](https://suttacentral.net/an1.41-50/en/sujato) he says nothing turns over faster, then refuses to even give a simile. So if that's the case, then the untangled ultimate reality isn't a faraway, long-forgotten Garden of Eden that requires an arm & a leg's worth of past-life hypnotherapy to remember. Rather, as the Dzogchen book [*Roaring Silence*](https://www.shambhala.com/roaring-silence-1270.html) declares, it appears in blessed moments as "the sparkling-through of our beginningless enlightenment."

Physics corroborates. In quantum field theory, even "empty" space measurably [seethes with fluctuations](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.05523). Light condenses into matter pairs and matter annihilates back into light, sparkling all day, everywhere. Permanence is the pattern made up of nature's impermanence, not a substantial something -- which is reminiscent of a mind that arises as one thing and ceases as another, and of flows that tangle, get heavy, and release.

I invite you to come and see for yourself! Ehipassiko!
