Then the question becomes: what’s next? A lot of people are afraid that nothing is next—that there is no next. But maybe they’re afraid of the wrong thing.</p><p>And if Buddhism is right about rebirth—I return to that evidence later on—the childish fantasy of immortality might be better understood as conditions recycling, and the subscription you forgot you had renewing itself without asking, like most of my Amazon Subscribe &amp; Save orders.</p><p>By “already perfect,” I mean that everything—including our efforts to improve it—is happening according to natural laws. question asked by a man dying of cancer, on the now defunct FB group of <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://awakeningtoreality.com/">AwakeningToReality</a>. As he was in the long process of dying, he thought to himself, “What if everything is already perfect without me?” For him, it was a liberating insight, while for someone at a different stage of life, it might be more challenging. This article itself is version 2.0 of my <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/what-if-everything-is-already-perfect?r=5vdc6m">old essay,</a> which was one page long. That was already perfect, so why on Earth did I spend the past weeks rewriting it?🤪</p><p>Because everything is just conditions, things work and kinda make sense; otherwise, it would be a bunch of random chaos. Even if we don’t fully understand anything, electrons keep orbiting according to quantum rules around atomic nuclei, the toaster predictably toasts, children <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/">hopefully grow up</a> (maybe a bad example), and the sun keeps rising and setting. There was never an electron that felt like it was in the wrong orbital and wished it had ended up around a more handsome nucleus! So, if everything is so perfect, why do we want things to be different? There is no self outside the web pulling its strings. Meditative training is the web learning to pull differently.</p><p>Once the little controller disappears, the mind can hide in a subtler place: “Fine, then I am the awareness watching everything happen.” I cannot find that witness either. What I find are appearances, followed by the processes that gather them into a body, world, and owner. </p><p>“Already perfect without you” means experience is complete before anyone owns it. Pain remains pain. A tree falls in the woods and makes a sound with nobody there to hear it. A color colors the coloring book with nobody holding the crayon. The reason nibbāna matters so much for me is because it was what launched me into Buddhist <strong>stream entry.</strong> Stream entry is the first stage of Buddhist enlightenment, where rebirth is ended, and it’s important because it changes you permanently. There’s a lot of confusion and debate over these two key terms, which is the reason for the rest of this article.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>How I Mistook An Interpretation For A Sutta</h1><p>Let me correct something I’ve been saying for about 15 years. I kept reading the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/KN/Ud/ud1_10.html"><em>Bāhiya Sutta</em>, Udāna 1.10</a>—<em>sutta</em> means teaching—as though the Buddha literally said there was no self <em>between the different sensory streams</em>. He didn’t explicitly say that.</p><p>I didn’t invent the reading out of nowhere, though, nor was it a Mandela Effect as I suspected. Soh Wei Yu and John Tan at AwakeningToReality.com relied heavily on <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2008/01/ajahn-amaro-on-non-duality-and.html">Ajahn Amaro’s contemplative rendering</a>, also published in <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.abhayagiri.org/media/books/amaro_small_boat_great_mountain.pdf"><em>Small Boat, Great Mountain</em></a>. I never understood this was not the consensus literal translation of the Pāli.</p><p>It’s such a beautiful sutta that I’ve read and repeated hundreds of times. I’d advise you to check it out to see if you also connect with it deeply. I will summarize here that basically, Bahiya was an ascetic, not a Buddhist, who thought he was enlightened, or on the path to enlightenment. A spirit came to him and told him he was nowhere near enlightenment. Bahiya humbly asked, “Then who is enlightened?” The spirit told him, “The Buddha is enlightened, go find him.” </p><p>So, Bahiya asked ChatGPT where to find the Buddha and used Google Maps for the 3 day walk there (just kidding, idk how people did anything back then!). When he finally found the Buddha, it was bad timing. The Buddha was on almsround begging for food and didn’t want to answer any questions. Bahiya pressed him 3 times (3 being the magic number back then), saying “life is uncertain, you never know when it may end, please teach me the dhamma briefly.”</p><p>This is how the Buddha finally advised Bahiya to practice, in the pithiest instructions ever (Amaro’s interpretive translation):</p><blockquote><p><em>In the seen, there is only the seen,</em><br><em>in the heard, there is only the heard,</em><br><em>in the sensed, there is only the sensed,</em><br><em>in the cognized, there is only the cognized.</em><br><em>Thus you should see that</em><br><em>indeed there is no thing here;</em><br><em>this, Bahiya, is how you should train yourself.</em><br><em>Since, Bahiya, there is for you</em><br><em>in the seen, only the seen,</em><br><em>in the heard, only the heard,</em><br><em>in the sensed, only the sensed,</em><br><em>in the cognized, only the cognized,</em><br><em>and you see that there is no thing here,</em><br><em>you will therefore see that</em><br><em>indeed there is no thing there.</em><br><em>As you see that there is no thing there,</em><br><em>you will see that</em><br><em>you are therefore located neither in the world of this,</em><br><em>nor in the world of that,</em><br><em>nor in any place</em><br><em>betwixt the two.</em><br><em>This alone is the end of suffering.</em></p></blockquote><p>The key here is that Amaro’s version says there is “no thing here” and “no thing there,” then explains that as no substantial subject, object, or self <strong>between them</strong>, then AtR and I extended “between” from subject and object to the different sensory streams.</p><p>The usual translations say something narrower. They agree with this part: When there is only the seen, heard, sensed, and cognized, there is no “you” connected with that. But this is where they differ: Then you are neither here (in this world/life), beyond (beyond this world/life), nor between the two: “just this is the end of suffering.” At first glance, that does look the same, but the translators explain the meanings of here/there differently from Amaro. The passage thus does not explain how the mind binds simultaneous disjoint sensory streams into one experience of the self &amp; world.</p><p>So when I say “sounds hear themselves,” I’m giving a direct-experience pointer inspired by “in the heard, only the heard,” while extrapolating a bit beyond the original Pāli. Another sutta, <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN12_44.html">SN 12.44</a>, and the other contact discourses explain that the “world” aka samsara, arises from contact between sense organs and sense objects, which creates sense consciousnesses, feeling, and all the rest. It seems almost obvious this is pointing at multisensory binding as the basis of the mind, but that part is not explicit:</p><blockquote><p><em>The Blessed One said: “And what is the origination of the world? Dependent on the eye &amp; forms there arises eye-consciousness. The meeting of the three is contact. From contact as a requisite condition comes feeling. From feeling as a requisite condition comes craving. From craving as a requisite condition comes clinging/sustenance. From clinging/sustenance as a requisite condition comes becoming. Understood <em>Nibbāna</em> &amp; No-self</h1><p>Maybe I was a meditator in a recent past life or something. My mom told me that when I was two or three, I told my mother, “I think this is one of my last lifetimes.” As a child, I also had recurring dreams in which there was only infinite vastness and absolutely nothing else—no “me” who was vast. Just vastness.</p><p>Skip forward a bit.<br>I was 26 when my high school history students told me they didn’t want to learn. I guess I was the one learning. They taught me I was dumb to have spent years training for a job that was apparently worthless. That’s when I started taking way toooo much ayahuasca, to get a different kind of training than the one normally prescribed by society. (Now I use my <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://soma.u-dont-exist.com">Haoma protocol</a>. It’s much nicer than regular ayahuasca.) </p><p>Each time I took a big aya dose, I felt like I was dying, and I actually had no idea I was just experiencing the effects of the drug. After the dying part, when I finally let go and my mind exploded, there was no more me, and no more body. I became the entire universe, seeing everything, being everything, knowing and loving everything. Each universe would expand for billions of years, contract to a point, and then explode. Those explosions were insane!</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN36.html">MN 36</a> says that on his awakening night the Buddha remembered lives across “many eons of cosmic contraction” and expansion. I hadn’t read it yet, so it didn’t prime me, but the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://youtu.be/RtuBerg5T4o?si=eVykSVS5Wht6sl2e">coinkydink</a> was cool. </p><div euphoric. Heck, unification is arguably the main motivating factor for humanity, if ya know what I mean. Expand that urge across the universe and it can resemble sex multiplied by infinity. I have spent hours yelling “OH MY GOD” after experiences like that.</p><p>Almost everything I found in the modern seeker marketplace confirmed my experience was basically the ultimate one. Browse through the teachers on <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://batgap.com/">Buddha at the Gas Pump</a>, and universal consciousness is like the default spiritual answer. <em>Advaita</em> (or <em>neo-advaita</em>) supplied a complete philosophy for what I’d already experienced. It was beautiful, intoxicating, and comparatively easy: we are all one universal, blissful awareness.</p><p>Many people get stuck at that stage, because it’s a nice one. But I’m a curious cat with less than 9 lives left, and I wanted to know about this guy called Buddha who was strangely absent from <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" could challenge myself &amp; go further. The Buddha’s habit of breaking experience into aggregates—the five changing components of a person—sense bases, contacts, feelings, and conditioned events seemed almost perversely backward. I had found the One. Why was this man taking everything apart?</p><p>Most Buddhist books I read felt like a waste of time. They copied the same lists and told me there was no self, usually without one useful paragraph about what the absence of unified awareness actually felt like.</p><h2>Then I Found The Map Past Oneness</h2><p>Then I found <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/">Awakening to Reality</a> (AtR) and its main author, Soh Wei Yu. Soh repeatedly described his ordinary 24/7 experience as fully disjoint “mind-body drop:” each appearance self-aware without a knower. Hearing someone report that as ordinary daily experience gave me confidence that I might be able to experience it, too. He called this, “mind-body drop,” because he did not categorize nibbāna as an experience. But his description made life without a hidden knower imaginable enough to practice toward.</p><p>I owe him a great deal. Our vocabulary is not identical. I use “unification” for the functional coordination through which a person speaks, crosses a road, recognizes a friend, and answers a message, even when no separate knower is experienced. Most of my own daily life includes that ordinary unification and often feels almost like everybody else’s day (well, if they’re happy).</p><p>AtR theory proposes, on the other hand, that unification of the senses is not required experientially for functioning in the world. This really makes no sense to me at all, and I suspected for a long time that AtR practitioners were over-claiming, especially since Soh’s teacher, John Tan (aka Thusness) always scolded Soh for overclaiming. One example of overclaiming that I proved definitively was Soh’s claim to be in bliss 24/7. When I met him in Singapore a few years ago, I noticed he wasn’t smiling at all. I asked, “So, you’re in bliss 24/7?” He said, “Yes.” I said, “Even now?” He said, “Well, no, not now.” 😜</p><p>John Tan’s stage map was another major help. His <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2007/03/thusnesss-six-stages-of-experience.html">public stages post</a> says it is “nothing authoritative, merely for sharing purposes,” and John does not present the stages as universal nor prescriptive.</p><p>I used to think that John’s experience was mostly a modern theme. But now I can’t understand how I thought that. After all, I already knew from <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/mn26/en/sujato">MN 26</a> and <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/mn36/en/sujato">MN 36</a> that Buddha learned the mind-unification meditations (jhānas) from his pre-Buddhist teachers. While those teachers thought they were enlightened, the Buddha saw that the jhānas were not in themselves liberating, but they were very useful to develop a stable and pure mind from which to develop penetrative insight into the nature of reality. He called that type of discerning/penetrating insight <em>vipassanā</em>—and no, it doesn’t mean Goenka-style body-scanning as many people now believe.</p><p>Then we have the famous <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/mn137/en/sujato">MN 137</a>, in which the Buddha advises to use unification of the mind to relinquish diversity, then to use non-identification to relinquish unity.</p><p>My own journey did include all of the meditative states of absorption from the four material jhānas up through the four immaterial absorptions/attainments (which are referred to in commentaries as the 5th–8th jhānas), thanks to practicing the techniques in Ajahn Brahm’s book, <em>Mindfulness, Bliss, and Beyond</em>. They were super amazing, psychedelic experiences which I probably did need to have, in order to stop thinking that psychedelics were required to produce those states. In the first jhāna, my body dissolved into a cloud of blissful atoms that were in love with each other 😶‍🌫️💗🥰 for idk how long! But I wouldn’t make those “hard” jhānas a prerequisite, since they aren’t made such in the suttas.</p><p>The general recommendation is collectedness: gather the mind before trying to untie the gathering. You have to collect the trash before you can toss it out. That’s made explicit in <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/an4.170/en/sujato">AN 4.170</a>, where Ānanda, the Buddha’s main attendant, explains that all of the monastics who achieved full liberation did so with some form of cultivation of mental unification, or at least mental serenity, although not necessarily prior to insight practice.</p><p>Most people on a spiritual path these days, as before Buddha came along, don’t get beyond the unification stages. If they consider themselves to be advanced, like many of the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://batgap.com/">BatGap</a> and Liberation Unleashed claimers, they generally settle into oneness, a luminous witness, or cosmic consciousness. They almost never talk about the suttas, which probably explains why. That is a major reason this article exists. The map and suttas gave me a direction beyond oneness, but they didn’t open me up by themselves.<br><br><br class="ProseMirror-trailingBreak"></p><h2>Desperation Did The Trick</h2><p>Some people go to retreats whenever they can, hoping to experience what happened to me. I didn’t have the money for that, but luckily, I did have a severely unstable marriage that was making me feel like I had completely failed at life!</p><p> My first wife had become unexpectedly pregnant with our son about a month before I had to commit her to a psych ward when I found out how serious her mental issues were.  Imagining my life as a poor guy trying to raise a kid (or more) with a woman like this was too much. I would have killed myself if I had not believed that suicide would simply lead to a worse rebirth. </p><p>Speaking of avoiding a worse rebirth, I had just watched a beautiful documentary called <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://garchen.net/for-the-benefit-of-all-beings/"><em>For the Benefit of All Beings</em></a> which affected me deeply. It was the biographical documentary of a Tibetan Buddhist master named Garchen Rinpoche, who spent roughly twenty years in a Chinese prison labor camp after fighting the Chinese invasion of Tibet with CIA weapons &amp; training. He knew he was going to hell for killing people, but he felt he had no choice. And in fact, he already was in hell in his own mind of hatred.</p><p>Ironically, it was in that brutal Chinese prison camp where he found his true master. His master taught him to love the Chinese and be happy that he was growing rice for them, since the people were not their government. That’s where his practice of loving-kindness blossomed, and he became a light of love upon the world. I thought: if he could find love there, perhaps I could stop treating my own hell as an excuse.</p><p>So anyway, back to desperate little me, in bed, next to my then-wife that I was afraid of. I had just watched this beautiful documentary. I was inspired by it, as well as by Soh Wei Yu’s experience of life, and his explanation that everything is just conditions. I began chanting the beautiful mantra from the documentary while I meditated, with the intent of experiencing what Soh Wei Yu was pointing at. Eventually, I fell asleep. Then I woke up, sat up, and my entire awareness coalesced around my navel. Then it seemed to leave my body. That part had happened before, and usually produced oneness with the room, world, or universe.</p><p>This time, my awareness dispersed, or popped like a balloon! 🎈</p><p>Awareness as one thing was gone. I was gone. What remained were the room’s color-light flows, all experiencing themselves in indescribable perfection. They were disjoint, but not isolated: the flows ran into and through one another, connected everywhere without becoming one experience or one witnessed scene. Nothing observed them from behind. There was no cosmic knower celebrating its achievement. There was also no black void. The flows were more intimate and more alive than ordinary perception, while possessing no owner at all.</p><p>This is what I refer to now as <em>Nibbāna</em>: unified consciousness disassembled, multisensory binding untangled, while the room’s color-light flows remained. It felt entirely different from the ayahuasca ego deaths: no cosmic orgasms and no screaming. The bliss was deeper, subtler, and vastly more peaceful. I believe the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/KN/Dhp/Ch15.html"><em>Dhammapada</em></a> is right to call unbinding the foremost ease—the traditional line often rendered “<em>nibbāna</em> is the highest happiness.”</p><p>I do not know how long this lasted. Time belonged to the unified world that was absent. Eventually the ordinary body, room, witness, and biography formed again.</p><p>I believe that was stream entry. If a coherent argument knocks that down, good. Turning it into an unquestionable spiritual identity would be a fairly embarrassing souvenir from an experience of no identity.</p><p>But since then, life has never felt like a Chinese finger trap as it did before. The sense of self and “my story” went from something like infinite authority to something much smaller and harder to believe. Selfishness and suffering dropped dramatically. I never wanted to kill myself again. I became angry less often, although anger had not been my dominant problem; I became sad far less often and felt trapped far less. By some measures I behaved less foolishly. I had not been a habitual liar, but honesty became more serious and more natural: I was less inclined to hide or leave things unsaid.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a I had no interest in tasks, plans, or becoming an impressive person on a farm. Each one includes, in varying proportions, a high-pitched <em>nāda</em>-like inner sound and a dissolution of the felt self. <em>Nāda</em> is a Sanskrit word for sound; some Hindu contemplative traditions use it for an inwardly perceived sound. Sometimes the sound dominates. Sometimes the dissolution does. This is the closest traditional comparison I know.</p><p>The effect often begins on one side of the felt head-space. By tuning attention, I can sometimes bring the other side into it. As it deepens, the bodily sense of the head or brain area can dissolve along with the sense of existing. The tissue is still there, as far as I know. 😂 At first I wondered whether I should stop riding bicycles. What if “I” disappeared during a turn and the bicycle continued its own spiritual journey into a ditch? So far I have never crashed, and I have always returned.</p><p>A friend told me he had much stronger episodes while driving: for long stretches, his own car and the traffic seemed to disappear. Luck is one possibility; so is a misunderstanding of what was happening, some feature of how these states operate, a combination, or something else.</p><div So that’s when I started practicing <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://love.u-dont-exist.com">loving-kindness (<em>metta </em>in Pali) meditation</a>. I was astounded at how it cured my depression instantly, and it worked every time. So then I started preaching that, but a lot of people couldn’t get it to work, because the Buddha’s advice was missing something. </p><p>In <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/ud5.1/en/anandajoti?lang=en&amp;reference=none&amp;highlight=false">Ud 5.1</a>, the Buddha exclaims:</p><blockquote><p>Having gone around in all directions with the mind,<br>There is surely no one found who is loved more than oneself.</p><p>In the same way others each love themselves,<br>Therefore one who cares for himself should not harm another.</p></blockquote><p><br>In the modern world, tho, I meet many people who seem to hate themselves. It took me many years to finally learn that these folks need to <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com">build a loving inner adult</a> before dissolving the cosmic witness.  And actually Buddha hints at this in a <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://substack.com/profile/355021294-u-dont-existcom/note/c-297324975?r=5vdc6m&amp;utm_source=notes-share-action&amp;utm_medium=web">new translation of the Metta sutta</a>.  A traumatized nervous system does not become healed because one finger was enlightened for three seconds.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>What “<em>Nibbāna</em>” Is Actually Saying</h1><p>The oldest image behind <em>nibbāna</em> is a fire going out. My book treated “unweaving” as the word’s direct etymology. That was wrong.</p><p>Buddhaghosa’s fifth-century <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/nanamoli/PathofPurification2011.pdf"><em>Visuddhimagga</em></a> derives <em>nibbāna</em> from <em>vāna</em>, “binding” or “fastening”: craving joins, binds, and laces one becoming to the next. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/books/MindLikeFire/Section0007.html">Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu</a> thinks this later derivation comes closest to the early fire image. A fire depends on fuel—<em>upādāna</em>, the same word used for clinging or sustenance—and becomes free when that dependence ends.</p><p>That is why his translations use “unbinding” for <em>nibbāna</em> in <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/KN/Ud/ud8_3.html">Ud 8.1–4</a>, <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an09/an09.034.than.html">AN 9.34</a>, <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN26.html">MN 26</a>, <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/dn16/en/sujato">DN 16</a>, and elsewhere. The Pāli in those passages is still <em>nibbāna</em> or <em>parinibbāna</em>; “unbinding” is the English translation. It fits my experience almost suspiciously well. The argument itself comes from the sensory and causation passages below, rather than from an etymology doing all the work.</p><p>The suttas themselves already speak in tangles, knots, and nets. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/sn1.23/en/bodhi">SN 1.23</a> describes a “tangle within” and a “tangle without,” then says the <em>arahants</em> (fully enlightened ones)’ tangle is disentangled. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/dhp90/en/suddhaso"><em>Dhammapada</em> 90</a> says the liberated person has abandoned every <em>gantha</em>—knot, tie, or bond. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/dhp180/en/sujato"><em>Dhammapada</em> 180</a> calls craving <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/dhp180/pli/ms"><em>jālinī</em></a>, net-like or ensnaring, and says it can no longer lead the Buddha anywhere. Nobody in ancient India says “multisensory binding.” Still, unbinding and disentangling are deeply native ways to describe liberation.</p><p>What came apart in my “room experience” was the fastening of separate sensory flows which ordinarily cohere into one established consciousness, one self, one world, and renewed existence. The room looked as though it were unweaving, and the suttas’ tangles, knots, and nets make that visual metaphor feel less accidental. </p><p>My old “hard nibbāna / soft nibbāna” language was also my taxonomy. But here’s a better breakdown of how I’d explain it now:</p><ul><li><p><strong>Partial loosening:</strong> a finger, bodily region, or pair of sense fields becomes less unified while the witness and world remain.</p></li><li><p><strong>Nibbāna:</strong> the unified witness and scene disappear while sensory appearances remain self-presenting.</p></li><li><p><strong>Full Nibbāna</strong>: same as regular nibbana, but not limited to sensations in the body. </p></li><li><p><strong>Ordinary functioning afterward:</strong> the body-world package assembles again.</p></li><li><p><strong>Remaining fetters and stabilization:</strong> craving, aversion, conceit, and habit can keep weakening long after the breakthrough.</p></li></ul><h2>How Contact Builds a World</h2><p>Start with the finger and room experiences.</p><p>Colors, sounds, pressures, and tingles were each present on their own, with no witness behind them. I use <em>awareness</em> for that simple presenting. The color and its appearing are one event.</p><p>These appearances meet locally, like a <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.meetup.com/">MeetUp</a> group (although MeetUp actually banned me for trying to organize a study group about this). I call those MeetUps “initial contact.” That is my term, broader than the Pāli word for contact, <em>phassa</em>. Those local MeetUps can help organize a sense door and the sense-consciousness that goes with it.</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN12_44.html">SN 12.44</a> gives the canonical sequence: eye and forms condition eye-consciousness; the meeting of all three is contact. Consciousness arises through that relation. It does not arrive from a warehouse of primordial knowing. The sutta reserves <em>phassa</em> for the threefold meeting, while eye and forms are already related enough to condition consciousness.</p><p>My proposed sequence starts one step earlier and then not print it for us.</p><p>The crucial move comes near the bottom. Once enough of the scene has been gathered together, it can remember itself, compare moments, anticipate, plan, and report. Then that late-arriving unity gets projected backward as a primordial witness that must have been there before the flows it is made from.</p><p>A committee assembles a report and then decides the report created the committee.</p><p>Craving keeps the mistake going. A pleasant contact becomes something <em>I</em> need again. An unpleasant one becomes something happening <em>to me</em>. Memory gives the owner a past; anticipation gives it a future. The newly formed field extends itself through time, building the “origin of the world” (from SN 12.44) toward becoming and birth.</p><h2>The Myriad Things Experience Themselves</h2><p>Dōgen, the 13th century Japanese founder of Soto Zen, described awakening with almost exactly this reversal. In the Tanahashi/Aitken translation of his <em>Genjōkōan</em>, he says:</p><blockquote><p>“To carry yourself forward and experience myriad things is delusion. That myriad things come forth and experience themselves is awakening.”</p></blockquote><p>The translation is available in full <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/GenjoKoan_Aitken.htm">here</a>. Other legitimate translations say that myriad things “illuminate,” “verify,” or “actualize” the self. The Japanese itself does not secretly contain my entire theory. Tanahashi’s version remains a serious interpretive translation, and it points beautifully toward the same reversal I am describing.</p><p>I am generally suspicious of later <em>Mahāyāna</em> scriptures that put new teachings into the historical Buddha’s mouth. Still, when Dōgen gives me one this good, I am willing to accept help from the other team.</p><p>Even the old Zen riddle (<em>koan</em>) about a tree falling in the forest brings us back to the Bahiya sutta: without anybody there to hear it, does it make a sound? The question challenges whether sound requires a separate hearer. My answer is that auditory appearing does not require a little person behind it. “The sound hears itself” is imperfect language, but ordinary grammar is even more committed to the imaginary owner.</p><h1>The Suttas Hid the Machine in Several Drawers</h1><p>The annoying thing is that I still can’t point to one sutta that lays out this whole model. The Buddha, or those who recorded his sayings, left the pieces scattered around.</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/KN/Ud/ud1_10.html">Ud 1.10</a> gives one puzzle piece: in the seen, heard, sensed, and cognized, there is no extra “you” attached to them. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN12_44.html">SN 12.44</a> gives another piece: Eye, form, and eye-consciousness meet as contact (meet as contact almost sounds tautological, sorry); contact gives rise to feeling, then craving, clinging, becoming, and birth follow along naturally from there. The sutta calls that the “origination of the world,” which is generally understood to be sutta shorthand for self/world/saṃsāra.</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/sn12.67/en/bodhi">SN 12.67</a> adds the feedback loop between all those links. Consciousness and name-and-form (<em>nāma</em> and <em>rūpa,</em> sometimes translated as mind and body) lean on each other like two bundles of reeds. Like the game of Jenga, when we pull either bundle away, the other falls (which actually supports mind/body as a better translation). All of this is part of the overall teaching of Dependent Origination (<em>Paṭiccasamuppāda</em>), which is the basis of Buddhism and is further detailed in <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/dn15/en/sujato">DN 15</a>. The Chinese parallels <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/da13/lzh/taisho">DĀ 13</a> and <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/ma97/lzh/taisho">MĀ 97</a> preserve the same basic reciprocal relationship. This is one way in which the Buddha said his teaching is a “middle path” between two extremes: On the one extreme we have Advaita, perennial philosophy, and new age spirituality, where nearly everyone falls into these days, claiming that consciousness comes first and constructs everything else. On the other extreme, we have the modern materialist view that matter comes first and produces consciousness. In Buddha’s view, they both keep each other standing.</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN140.html">MN 140</a> also repeats these themes, but what it adds is a treatment of the meditative absorptions (<em>jhānas</em>) of infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception, without treating any of them as the finish line. Apparently even a very impressive nothing can still be fabricated, and I can testify to that, because I went through those also prior to my nibbana experience.</p><p>When we put the sutta drawers together we get more than what I had tried to squeeze out of the Bāhiya Sutta by itself: separate sense contacts, separate consciousnesses and feelings, consciousness and name-and-form propping each other up, and craving and clinging keeping the whole thing personal and running.</p><p>As I mentioned before, the texts don’t explicitly say that the sensory streams are each aware in parallel, or that binding them together is what creates one owner and one world. That is the model I think they point toward when I combine them with what I experienced, and honestly at this point it seems like common sense to me, but hindsight is 20/20 as they say.</p><h2>How the Experiential Key Can Fade</h2><p>After 15 years of reading popular &amp; obscure Buddhist books, plus countless hours on forums like SuttaCentral.net, I never once found a positive definition of what nibbāna actually is. Everyone says what nibbana does (liberates), what it ends (rebirth, consciousness, defilements), what it isn’t (anything), and then gives me the aggregates, sense bases, dependent origination, and no-self again. But what is the experience? What are all those lists pointing to? Why is it no Theravadan monk ever told me anything useful despite devoting their life to this?</p><p>These books did at least tell me that everything arises from conditions. That was interesting, but it also begged the free-will question, which seemed to me like the elephant in the room. If my choices are conditioned, what exactly is free about them? I don’t think I’ve ever even seen the phrase “free will” in the suttas at all, and that makes sense because the phrase itself is illogical. Instead, we find debates about what conditions choices. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN6_63.html">AN 6.63</a> says intention is <em>kamma</em>, the Pāli word usually translated as karma, and contact causes it. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/an3.61/en/sujato">AN 3.61</a> rejects theories that make effort meaningless. Choices happen. They’re important because they become conditions for what happens next. That part makes sense to me. Where I get lost is, who’s making the choices and based on what?</p><p>I think both questions share the same missing key. And they were never explained concisely for the same reason.</p><p>Obviously, this is where I start sounding like every guy on the internet who says everybody lost the real teaching except him. 😂 I’m willing to make that argument because the evidence is still public and because the canon itself describes the teaching degrading. The lost-key story only explains how the words could survive after the experiential meaning faded. The model still has to be judged from the texts and experience.</p><p>So as the people who directly understood nibbāna became rarer, the words kept getting interpreted by people who were still experiencing one unified consciousness and one world, and the hints stopped landing. I’m reminded of <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/ud6.4/en/sujato">Ud. 6.4</a>, when the Buddha talks about how sectarian teachers quarrel over incomplete understandings of reality, much like blind men who each touch a different part of an elephant and argue over its true nature.</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/sn16.13/en/bodhi">SN 16.13</a> says this kind of degradation was already happening in the Buddha’s lifetime. Mahā Kassapa asks why there are more training rules but fewer monks reaching final knowledge. The Buddha says counterfeit Dhamma gradually makes the true Dhamma disappear.</p><p>And the funniest part is that the words themselves may have survived surprisingly well. The discourses were transmitted orally for centuries, but they were transmitted communally, and the separately preserved early collections agree on an enormous amount. There are variants, rearrangements, and later material. Still, the words surviving is not the same as the meaning surviving. Even having the right pieces to the puzzle doesn’t ensure you can put them together to see the picture on the box.</p><p><br class="ProseMirror-trailingBreak"></p><h1>Am I wrong to reveal the “secret” teaching?</h1><p>I think the Buddha was deliberately vague on some dangerous central questions. In <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/sn44.10/en/sujato">SN 44.10</a>, he refuses to directly say whether the self exists or not, because it would confuse an already confused person further. And in other suttas, such as <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://suttacentral.net/an6.61/en/sujato">AN 6.61</a>, he was misunderstood even by many of his senior monks, and his clarification remained cryptically terse. He seems to have been intentionally vague, requiring his students to piece together many of his discourses in an intelligent way to arrive at their own correct interpretation of nibbāna. </p><p>I don’t think he wanted Dhamma copycats. He wanted liberating insight to strike people like a lightning bolt, from their own realization, with just enough hints. When I studied for my Masters in Education at Temple University, we learned that when students learn by memorizing, they don’t actually understand. Our job as teachers is to scaffold, by bringing students only to the level just beyond where they presently understand. </p><p>So I think the Buddha was a brilliant teacher who understood that even if someone could learn the steps to reach nibbāna, if they hadn’t struggled to really grasp the importance of it deeply, it might not produce Stream Entry. But this <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedagogy_of_the_Oppressed"><em>Pedagogy of the Oppressed</em></a> method only worked while he was around (or his fully enlightened disciples were) to give the hints with proper meaning and scaffolding. Once they were gone, the same terseness could help hide the fact that the key was left in their pockets.</p><p>I’m publishing this because universal awareness is a comfortable place to spend twenty years while calling it the destination. The map that helped me move beyond it is hard to find. It can save a prepared person years or even lifetimes, and it might reach the rare ripe reader whom a private teacher might never meet. It can also create extremely convincing counterfeits. </p><p>AtR and Soh were crucial inspirations, and the potential benefit of such pointers is enormous. AtR also discusses <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2022/06/the-awakening-to-reality-practice-guide.html">ethics and the Noble Eightfold Path</a>, so I won’t caricature it as a phenomenology cult. The same public archive repeatedly shows John correcting the difference between an experience, an insight, a view, and stabilization—sometimes gently, sometimes with <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2022/06/someone-asked-about-pemako-stages.html">“kok ur head.”</a> Soh often posted the corrections himself. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2018/11/no-mind-and-anatta-focusing-on-insight.html">Temporary no-mind and stable anatta insight are different matters.</a></p><p>On the other hand, Soh ironically became the one who would “authenticate” people’s stages of awakening, despite John Tan saying that awakening was ba“self-authenticated by doubtless realization.”</p><p>I stayed for 6 days with one of the people Soh authenticated as a stream-enterer, Frank Yang, and I can tell you for sure he didn’t even understand the very basics of what Buddha taught (including the word “conditions”), and he got angry when I questioned his understandings. Although I can’t fault a YouTube star for not understanding conditions, since around that same time period, even Ayasma Ariyadhammika, the head teacher at the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="http://sasanarakkha.org/">Theravada Buddhist center</a> that gives “advanced training” to monastics, didn’t understand “conditions” when I met him. He thanked me when I explained the term, and it seemed like he had a “lightbulb moment.”</p><p>Frank did feel that he suffered much less after softening his identity, so that’s valuable, but it doesn’t equate to stream entry. He was a good, if shallow, AtR parrot, and he was becoming somewhat of an exalted guru thanks to <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.youtube.com/@frankyang">his beautiful, crazy YouTube channel </a>and his charisma.</p><p>The precise public teaching most readers now call AtR seems to me largely Soh’s editorial creation from John’s insights, fragments, and corrections. John supplied much of the insight; Soh systematized it into a searchable stage map, defended it, and made it teachable.</p><p>My interpretation is that John preferred Soh as a mouthpiece. That is a smart arrangement: the student can promote the teacher while the teacher keeps describing his own posts as simple sharing or nothing authoritative. If the teacher promotes themself, that’s often seen as boasting. This is my reading of a public pattern, not proof of a private motive.</p><p>A map can point; it cannot perform the seeing. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://suttacentral.net/dhp276/en/buddharakkhita">Dhammapada 276</a> says awakened teachers point out the path while practitioners make the effort. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://suttacentral.net/mn38/en/sujato">MN 38</a> speaks of the Dhamma as something to be known, seen, and realized for oneself. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://suttacentral.net/dn16/en/sujato">DN 16</a> pairs reliance on oneself with reliance on the Dhamma, and <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://suttacentral.net/mn22/en/sujato">MN 22</a> compares the Dhamma to a raft used for crossing rather than an identity to drag around after reaching shore.</p><p>The more detailed the map becomes, the more curiosity, honesty, and empirical self-scrutiny it demands. AI has the same two-sided effect. Used as a partner in inquiry, it can amplify intelligence. Used as an outsourced mind, it lets a person skip the struggle that would have built understanding. A spiritual map can do the same: lower the search cost while raising the counterfeit risk.</p><p>An unprepared reader can use the answer key to promote a psychedelic rupture, depersonalization, derealization, trauma, or a temporary no-self state into stream entry; family and relationships can be neglected while the language sounds terrifyingly precise. Secrecy (as in <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sg-5CDOcsTM">Tibetan Buddhism’s secret teachings</a>) won’t solve this. Readiness warnings, anti-counterfeit framing, ethical and relational outcome checks, and personal verification are the best protection I know.</p><h1>Stream Entry and the Dream Running Out</h1><p><em>Theravāda</em> Buddhism (the oldest surviving sect) describes four stages of awakening: stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, and final enlightenment, called <em>arahantship</em>. For this article, the first one matters. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN3_87.html">AN 3.87</a> ties stream-entry to the ending of identity view, uncertainty about the path, and grasping at habits and practices as sufficient in themselves. It calls the stream-enterer certain, headed for awakening, and no longer destined for states of woe. The later stages of awakening keep weakening passion, aversion, and delusion until the remaining fetters and effluents end.</p><p>The first three fetters the stream-enterer abandons are important, so let’s examine: <strong>Identity view</strong> means taking any of the five aggregates as self, belonging to self, containing self, or contained inside a self. <strong>Doubt</strong> here means the fundamental uncertainty about the Buddha’s awakening and whether this path actually leads out—not the ordinary habit of asking difficult questions. <strong>Grasping at precepts and practices</strong> means treating rules, rituals, vows, or techniques as sufficient by themselves for liberation. Ethics and practice still matter; their magical authority ends.</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN10_92.html">AN 10.92</a> also names confidence in the Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha, ethical restraint, and direct understanding of causality. A person can report all of that, behave well, and still be repeating a Buddhist identity. Lights, bliss, and subscriber counts prove even less.</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.theravada.gr/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Reincarnation-or-Rebirth-Scientific-Research-and-Evidence.pdf">There is a lot of evidence for rebirth</a>. Maybe the greatest prize for the stream-enterer is their “release date:” <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN3_88.html">AN 3.88</a> says there are at most seven further lifetimes before final awakening. But who has those lives if no permanent self exists? Buddhists do not imagine a soul traveling from body to body, since that would be a belief in an unchanging self-essence. Instead they understand rebirth as causal continuity without a traveler: craving and clinging condition becoming, and becoming conditions birth. The later Buddhist <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/mil3.5.5/en/kelly"><em>Milindapañha</em></a> compares this to one lamp lighting another. The flame does not migrate, yet the second lighting is not unrelated to the first. Consequences can continue without a metaphysical owner being transferred.</p><p>This question became personal after my father died. In the video below, I tell the story of an experience in which I met him in a heavenly realm and helped him understand why he was headed for rebirth on Earth. It did not feel like a permanent soul surviving unchanged; it felt more like a continuity of tendencies and understanding that could still change.</p><div class="native-video-embed" data-attrs="{&quot;mediaUploadId&quot;:&quot;030449e4-9c54-4d62-91b8-ea1cc3ee3bcc&quot;,&quot;duration&quot;:null}" contenteditable="false" draggable="true"><div data-component-name="VideoEmbedPlayer" id="media-030449e4-9c54-4d62-91b8-ea1cc3ee3bcc" class="videoScrollTarget-SzB20Y"><div class="videoEmbed-_FycLU"><div role="region" aria-label="Video player" class="undefined with-preview video-player-with-background video-player-wrapper"><div class="video-player video-player video-player-with-background videoPlayer-vlcedM" style="padding-bottom: 60%;"><video controlslist="nodownload" preload="metadata" data-video-id="030449e4-9c54-4d62-91b8-ea1cc3ee3bcc" poster="https://substack-video.s3.amazonaws.com/video_upload/post/207860466/030449e4-9c54-4d62-91b8-ea1cc3ee3bcc/transcoded-1785531215.png?refresh=Sat Aug 01 2026 02:17:21 GMT+0000 (heure moyenne de Greenwich)" crossorigin="anonymous" class="video-P2qgwZ"><source src="/api/v1/video/upload/030449e4-9c54-4d62-91b8-ea1cc3ee3bcc/src?type=hls" type="application/x-mpegURL"><source src="/api/v1/video/upload/030449e4-9c54-4d62-91b8-ea1cc3ee3bcc/src?type=mp4" type="video/mp4"></video><div class="pencraft pc-position-absolute pc-reset buttonContainer-tH3LP9 video-player-button"><button tabindex="0" type="button" aria-label="s" class="pencraft pc-reset pencraft video-player-play-pause-button media-XfKJl4 size_md-gCDS3o priority_primary-RfbeYt iconButton-mq_Et5 iconButtonBase-dJGHgN buttonBase-GK1x3M buttonStyle-r7yGCK size_md-gCDS3o priority_primary-RfbeYt rounded-SYxRdz" style="width: 56px; That reading is disputed, and I give the fuller textual case in the appendix.</strong></p><p>I believe my room experience was stream-entry because it combined direct <em>nibbāna</em>—the unified body-world-witness coming apart while the sensory flows remained—with irreversible consequences. This is where I want any serious objection to hit me. These are my practical tests, adapted from canonical markers of irreversible view, confidence, conduct, and reduced selfishness. They are not a canonical diagnostic checklist:</p><ul><li><p>Do other people notice a durable reduction in selfishness?</p></li><li><p>Can the person be content with less?</p></li><li><p>Do they become warmer and more enjoyable to be around without turning into a people-pleaser?</p></li><li><p>Does the Dhamma become intuitively coherent rather than merely memorized?</p></li><li><p>Do they know, with unusual certainty, that rebirth is moving toward its end?</p></li><li><p>Can they still recognize trauma, apologize, and do ordinary psychological repair instead of floating above it?</p></li></ul><p>The last test matters because a stream-enterer still has most fetters. Even a genuine breakthrough does not turn somebody into a fully enlightened <em>arahant</em>, a therapist, a saint, or even a competent roommate.</p><p>The lucid-dream comparison from earlier also shapes how I understand the seven-life limit. Once the dream is known as a dream, complete unquestioned absorption becomes unstable. The dream may continue, and lucidity may fade for stretches, but its authority has cracked and the time remaining feels numbered.</p><p>I used to attribute “life is like a dream” directly to the historical Buddha. The famous stanza I loved actually comes from the later <em>Mahāyāna</em> <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://read.84000.co/translation/toh16.html"><em>Diamond Sutra</em></a>. The text presents it as the Buddha’s speech; historically, the scripture appeared centuries later, with a known Chinese translation by 402 CE, as this <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://shc.stanford.edu/stanford-humanities-center/news/buddhist-diamond-sutra">Stanford overview</a> explains. Apparently my Mandela-effect exhibit has a <em>Mahāyāna</em> annex.</p><p>The verse is still beautiful. Here is a widely circulated English rendering:</p><blockquote><p>So should one view all of the fleeting world:</p><p>A star at dawn, a bubble in a stream,</p><p>A flash of lightning in a summer rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/KN/Dhp/Ch13.html"><em>Dhammapada</em> 170</a> asks us to see the world as a bubble and mirage.</p><div like waking up from a dream, except that nobody woke up. Reasoning can make the dream doubtful. The event made doubt about the dream impossible to reverse.</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>What Science Can Map</h1><p>If you can remember the last thunderstorm, then you don’t need neuroscience to know that the mind binds different sensory streams together. When you see lightning, you count the seconds until you hear the thunder. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-science-thunder">Five seconds means the lightning was about a mile away.</a> When they seem to happen at the same time, it means the lightning is close, but you understand they didn’t truly reach you at the same time. It’s just that our brain naturally merges stimuli that are close enough together.</p><p>But have you ever wondered whether several experiences can happen in parallel before—or without—the mind turning them into one apparent scene?</p><p>The neuroscientist <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10448004/">Semir Zeki</a> has argued for something close to this within vision: color, motion, form, and location are processed separately and can become conscious at different times. He calls the local results <em>microconsciousnesses</em>. In his theory, the completed visual picture is a later <em>macroconsciousness</em>: binding joins experiences that were already conscious.</p><p>The strongest evidence is visual, and Zeki’s conclusion reaches beyond the evidence. Still, he places consciousness before final unity instead of waiting for the finished scene. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16997611/">Recurrent Processing Theory</a> offers one possible mechanism: feedback inside a local sensory network may be enough for experience before the information becomes globally available. Whether local recurrence is sufficient to produce conscious experience remains disputed, though.</p><p>If you’re a mom, you might already know about doing many things at the same time, but science is still figuring that out. In one small <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00494/full">Descriptive Experience Sampling study</a>, intrepid researchers had the audacity to interrupt five people 180 times altogether and ask what was happening in experience at the exact moment before each beep. I would have smacked those researchers! But these subjects were more enlightened than me, and they repeatedly reported several things at once: sounds, bodily sensations, inner images, thoughts, and events inside and outside the body. This tells us one thing: experience isn’t obviously restricted to one item at a time.</p><p>But if you’re ready to jump down the Twilight Zone rabbit hole, then let’s look at the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/140/7/2051/3892700">split-brain experiments</a>. Don’t worry, these people weren’t sawed apart for no reason. The corpus callosum, which joins the two brain hemispheres, had been surgically severed as an epilepsy treatment. These case studies point to how consciousness can function without a single owner. In these folks, the right hand can answer from information available to the left hemisphere, while the left hand can point to or draw what the right hemisphere knows. The speaking left hemisphere may say it saw nothing, while the left hand chooses the correct object.</p><p>In the famous chicken-claw and snow-scene experiment, the left hemisphere was shown a chicken claw, while the right hemisphere was shown snow. When given a choice of images, the right hand chose a chicken, and the left chose a shovel. The speaking left hemisphere could see the shovel choice, but it had never seen the snow scene. When asked why the shovel had been chosen, it invented a reason: the shovel was for cleaning the chicken shed. That’s called confabulation.</p><p>It doesn’t look like there was one overall owner or witness to all that. The speaking mind didn’t merely lack part of the event. It didn’t know that it lacked it, and it confidently explained behavior caused somewhere else. To me, that shows how readily we replace what actually happens in reality with a story that validates what we think reality should be, and it could explain how the story of the self emerges from simple conditions.</p><p>I first read a good summary of this research in Sam Harris’s <em>Waking Up</em>. Interestingly, I don’t think Harris followed his own evidence all the way through. He got rid of the personal self while leaving pure awareness behind as the final witness, which lands back in Advaita territory.</p><p>What’s still unclear from the split-brainers is whether there are actually two self-aware hemispheres, since we can’t easily ask the mostly non-speaking right hemisphere.</p><p>Although the model I’m proposing isn’t fully addressed by science, there is a recent theory from 2024 called <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2024/1/niae010/7631826">Nested Observer Windows</a> which comes close. It models the brain as many observer windows operating at different sizes and speeds, each generating its own cognitive stream while higher windows gather information from lower ones (talk about voyeurism!). The authors ask whether only the highest window is conscious, whether consciousness begins somewhere above a threshold, or whether every window has experience.</p><h2>The Limit of Science</h2><p>None of this proves my interpretation of the room experience, but it gives the argument a third leg, which makes it more safe to ride, like a tricycle. So where we’re at now with science is, it breaks down our ordinary perception into: 1) parallel processing, 2) several simultaneous experiential strands, 3) fragmented access, and 4) a narrator that confuses its partial report with the whole system. It also contains serious theories in which local consciousness appears before final binding, which is part of what I’m talking about.</p><p>Unsurprisingly, though, you won’t find a study which documents the exact event I’m describing: visual, auditory, bodily, and other flows remaining self-presenting after the common scene, body-world, and witness disappear.</p><p>Objective experimentation may never be able to settle that last step. It can track how the color red is discriminated, remembered, joined with other information, and reported. But red itself never climbs out of the scanner and testifies that it was already its own awareness. That part has to be investigated from inside experience.</p><div class="captioned-image-container"><figure><a class="image-link image2 is-viewable-img" target="_blank" Essentially, he untied and was released into the final nibbāna, called <em>parinibbāna</em>. Parinibbāna is not different from full nibbāna, except that there’s no coming back when the meditation’s done. The living breakthrough to nibbāna is what makes the final direction knowable.</p><p>It’s worth noting that Bāhiya was not just lucky to get enlightened so fast. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/ud1.10/en/sujato">Ud 1.10</a> shows his urgency and immediate understanding, while the later <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/tha-ap538/en/walters">Bāhiya Apadāna</a> says he had aspired and practiced toward that quick comprehension for a hundred thousand eons. Yourself</h1><p>I don’t want Bāhiya’s cow to get the last word. In <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/an3.55/en/sujato">AN 3.55</a>, the Buddha exclaims that, “When one has abandoned greed, hatred, and delusion, one does not plan for self-affliction, for the affliction of others, or for the affliction of both, and one experiences mental peace and bliss. It is in this way that Nibbāna is directly visible, timeless, inviting one to come and see, pertinent, and to be experienced personally by the wise.”</p><p>That’s why I put the practice before the appendix. You can investigate the experience without agreeing with my explanation.</p><p>So, I invite you to come and see for yourself!</p><hr contenteditable="false"><h1>Appendix: The Doctrinal Arguments Behind This Interpretation</h1><p>This is the nerd cellar under the article. You can skip it and still use the practice. If you want to see why I’m making these doctrinal claims, the links below include my SuttaCentral research trail, the Pāli discourses, Chinese parallels, and main scholarly disagreement.</p><h2>Has Anyone on SuttaCentral Discussed This?</h2><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/introduction">SuttaCentral</a> is known as the main repository of the Pāli Canon and its translations. It’s the most authoritative public discussion forum for the Early Buddhist Texts. So that’s where I went to peruse whether anyone already put my understanding together or found something that ruled it out. If I’d found either, this article would be very different—or I probably wouldn’t be publishing it at all. I did find support for several pieces of my multisensory binding synthesis, because those pieces are in the suttas, but I still haven’t found anyone combining them into the full model I’m proposing.</p><p>One of the most relevant earlier threads, posted by a moderator, asks whether <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/sense-consciousness-arising-one-at-a-time/21530">sense-consciousness arises one at a time</a>. This question is posed because in the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.abhidhamma.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/introduction-to-the-Abhidhamma.html">later Abhidhamma commentaries on the suttas</a>, it is taught that the mind is like a movie made of individual experience frames in a series. Because that understanding is not in the suttas, it often contributes to controversy and confusion. As we can see from the replies, the suttas themselves don’t specify exactly how the streams coordinate, but nor do they lean toward strict serialization. What interested me was that the OP asked just the right question, and nobody had an answer from the suttas. They had common sense wisdom, they had some later commentary theory, and some resorted to modern science.</p><p>Ironically, the closest direct answer I found on the forum, before Charlie replied to <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/does-sn-12-44-explain-the-missing-mechanism-behind-ud-1-10-and-nibbana/44650">my thread,</a> was literally just one sentence: <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/spin-off-from-bhante-sujato-s-essay-self-no-self-not-self/32483/24">“It’s called the binding problem.”</a> 😂</p><p>Charlie went further with me. He pointed to <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/sn22.79/en/bodhi">SN 22.79</a>, where volitional formations are constructed by and construct the conditioned five aggregates that make up the self: forms, sensations, perceptions, mental formations, consciousness. Later, it says the liberated ones have dismantled, scattered, and are no longer accumulating those aggregates (called <em>khandhas</em> in Pāli). Charlie calls that “de-conditioning.” I wouldn’t say the living arahant’s remaining aggregates have become unconditioned, because the sutta doesn’t say that. But the construction and dismantling language is right there, and it’s common in many suttas.</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/mn43/en/suddhaso">MN 43</a> adds another piece: each physical sense has its own domain, while mind is their common resort. That gets us separate sensory streams, and the meeting room. It still doesn’t specify how they become one body-world scene.</p><p>His reading of the Bāhiya instruction came closer still. He sees the ordinary graspable objects giving way to an underlying flow, with ordinary recognition later returning in a way that “transcends and includes” it. That’s the closest substantial convergence I’ve found on the forum.</p><p>My model still goes one step further: during nibbāna, the common body-world-witness assembly drops out while visual, auditory, and bodily flows remain self-presenting. Ordinary functional integration returns afterward. I still don’t see Charlie or the suttas explicitly describing the cross-sensory binding mechanism itself, which is the part my model is trying to explain.</p><p>The fact that this is so hard to imagine also supports it, ironically. After all, the Buddha was pointing toward something which ordinary consciousness could never fathom until he began showing people where to look. In the same vein, we wouldn’t expect a fish to realize it’s in water. If an explanation of nibbāna feels completely familiar and easy to picture from inside ordinary experience, I’d wager that is probably evidence against it.</p><h2>What the suttas say about nibbāna as the gate to stream-entry</h2><p>The main article already gave my personal evidence for nibbāna as a necessary but insufficient condition for stream entry. What the suttas say on this question is disputed.</p><p>The suttas do not present stream entry as finally agreeing with Buddhist doctrine, nor as accepting the Buddha as your savior. The Buddha is concise in <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/sn25">SN 25.1–10</a>:</p><blockquote><p>“Someone who accepts these teachings after deliberating them with a degree of wisdom is called a follower of teachings. They’ve stepped into the sure path, they’ve stepped into the level of the true person, and they’ve passed beyond the level of the untrue person. They can’t do any deed which would make them be reborn in hell, the animal realm, or the ghost realm. They can’t die without realizing the fruit of stream-entry. Someone who understands and sees these principles is called a stream-enterer.”</p></blockquote><p>The special seeing is further detailed in suttas like <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/an8.22/en/sujato">AN 8.22</a>. In this sutta, when the Buddha saw Ugga was ready, he gave him precise teachings that led him past the gate of stream entry. Ugga explains: “I saw, attained, understood, and fathomed the Dhamma. I went beyond doubt, got rid of indecision, and became self-assured and independent of others regarding the Teacher’s instructions.”</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/an10.92/en/sujato">AN 10.92</a> adds direct understanding of dependent origination/arising, confidence in the Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha, and ethical restraint.</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN3_87.html">AN 3.87</a> gives the classic three fetters that break at stream entry, which I explained in the main article. Most fetters still remain, to lesser degrees, after that, disappearing at later stages. In my experience, <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN48.html">MN 48</a> nails it: defilements can still arise, but they no longer take over in the same way, and they’re recognized much faster. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN13_1.html">SN 13.1</a> compares the suffering that remains after stream entry to dust on a fingernail beside the whole earth; in my experience, that’s vastly exaggerated.</p><h2>Infinity and nothingness are not <em>nibbāna</em></h2><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/mn121/en/sujato">MN 121</a> moves through increasingly refined unities: wilderness, earth, infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and finally signlessness. Each earlier stage still contains a remaining disturbance. Even signless immersion is recognized as conditioned, chosen, and liable to cease before liberating insight finishes the path.</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/ma190/lzh/taisho">MĀ 190</a> and a Tibetan parallel preserve the main descent from ordinary perception through infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and signlessness. The Pāli version adds one more formless step. The common spine matters more: infinite cosmic consciousness is reached and then left behind. So is the sphere of nothingness.</p><p>The sphere of nothingness cannot be <em>Nibbāna</em>: it is a conditioned meditative attainment with perception still operating, and the path continues beyond it. A permanent being is not annihilated in cessation because early Buddhism denies that such an owner was there in the first place. The serious question is what ceases during a living realization while sights, sounds, sensations, and cognized events can still occur.</p><h2>Unity is cultivated and then Untied</h2><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/mn137/en/sujato">MN 137</a> gives the clearest textual version of the sequence described above. It distinguishes equanimity based on sensory diversity from equanimity based on unity. Its unity side includes infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception. Then the discourse says to use unity to relinquish diversity and non-identification to relinquish unity. Interestingly, in the Chinese āgama translation, <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/ma163/lzh/taisho">MĀ 163</a>, that final critical advice to abandon unity is left out.</p><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/mn44/en/sujato">MN 44</a> defines <em>samādhi</em> as <em>cittassa ekaggatā</em>, which means the unification of mind. The various stages of samādhi meditation are called jhānas, and they predate Buddhism, but remain a preliminary practice in it. In the 2nd jhāna, the standard formula uses the Pāli phrase <em>cetaso ekodibhāva</em>, the mind becoming unified. Then, in <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/dn1/en/sujato">DN 1</a>, we find a crucial warning for those contemplatives who take that unified state to be supreme nibbāna. Buddha says they’re stuck in wrong view. Finally, in <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/mn111/en/sujato">MN 111</a>, we see unification as thematic through all the jhānas, including 6th jhāna (infinite consciousness), and 7th jhāna (nothingness), followed by repeated acknowledgement of “an escape beyond.”</p><p>When the suttas talk about release, the language changes. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/sn1.23/en/sujato">SN 1.23</a> describes the tangle inside and outside, says arahants have disentangled it, and locates the cutting of the tangle where nama-rupa (name-and-form or mind-and-body) and perceptions of sensory impingement cease without remainder. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/dhp90-99/en/sujato">Dhammapada 90</a> says all <em>ganthā</em>—knots or ties—have been abandoned. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/dhp179-196/en/sujato">Dhammapada 180</a> calls craving <em>jālinī</em>, net-like or ensnaring. <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/an9.36/en/sujato">AN 9.36</a> describes nibbāna as stilling formations, relinquishing attachments, ending craving, fading, and cessation.</p><p>Then when I read <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" href="https://suttacentral.net/an6.61/en/sujato">AN 6.61</a>, I noticed the weaving imagery was unusually explicit. In that sutta, you can see how several senior monks offer different interpretations on one of Buddha’s teachings on nibbāna. The Buddha says they all spoke well “in a way,” then explains what he meant by it: “Contact, mendicants, is one end. The origin of contact is the second end. The cessation of contact is the middle. And craving is the seamstress, for craving weaves one to being regenerated in one state of existence or another.” This shows us again how contact, cessation, becoming, and weaving already occupy the same doctrinal neighborhood. It’s also interesting to me because even for senior monks at that time, Buddha’s teaching was difficult to understand, and he almost seems to have intended that with the way he explained so tersely what they already had trouble with.</p><p>There’s still nothing in the Pāli canon that directly translates to my modern phrase, “Global multisensory unbinding.” But you can see the direction they point in: the Buddha advises us to gather the mind, warns us against mistaking unity for nibbāna, then describes liberation not only as escape, relinquishment, cessation, but also as disentangling/untying, abandoned knots, and craving’s net losing its grip. I don’t see what it is that could be untied in nibbana aside from the recurring assembly of the six sensory streams which Buddha says precede the sense of one witness and one world.</p><h2>Why I Think This Is the Only Logical Possibility</h2><p>My goal here is to approach this question as rigorously as possible so the explanation is actually useful. That’s why I’ve tried to see how the other explanations might fit.</p><p>One interpretation is that nibbāna means literal extinction, like a lamp going out—poof—so being gone in deep sleep can start to sound almost like what everyone already experiences nightly. But that doesn’t fit Ud 1.10, where there is still the seen, heard, sensed, and cognized. Some people might think of it more like the perception of nothingness, but that’s another conditioned attainment the Buddha went beyond.</p><p>A lot of Mahayana folk see nibbana as some type of cosmic consciousness, but that doesn’t fit either, because infinite consciousness is another conditioned attainment. A hidden witness still leaves someone behind the experience. I loved cosmic consciousness, but it’s definitely the opposite of nibbana in my experience.</p><p>The only other contender from Theravāda I can think of is the later Mahāsi/Abhidhamma one: path and fruition consciousness take nibbāna as an unconditioned object. Aside from being almost too vague to be worth talking about, that still has consciousness over here knowing nibbāna over there, which again doesn’t fit the Bāhiya instruction. It preserves the same basic split Thusness’s map treats as prior to anatta: awareness over here, reality over there—even if the knowing consciousness is momentary rather than an eternal Self.</p><p>Zen is famous for its koans which are meant to break the mind itself. But Theravada has them as well, just not packaged as such. For example, AN 9.34 says nibbāna is bliss precisely because nothing is felt. Ordinarily we might say, uh, well, how do you feel bliss if you don’t feel anything? But feeling in the suttas arises from contact, so it’s a precise term implying something feeling something. In nibbāna, there’s no contact, just disjoint self-known flows. There’s no eye seeing the seen, only colors coloring. In my experience, it seems that bliss is the “base state” of nature before anything is there to feel anything else. Colors coloring are way happier than a kid coloring a coloring book, basically.</p><p>Another Theravāda riddle is when nibbāna is referred to as the cessation of consciousness. That’s difficult to understand only if we think consciousness is the same as experience. In the suttas, eye-consciousness is conditioned by the eye and the colors it sees. So consciousness is not the colors themselves, which remain blissfully aware without a consciousness over here knowing them over there.</p><p>And some folks think there’s just nothing we can know about nibbana, but AN 4.45 puts the world, its arising, its cessation, and the path to cessation right here in the body, perception, and mind, which means nibbana isn’t some unreachable mystery but something a person can actually investigate and realize directly.</p><h2>Primary Texts and Research</h2><ul><li><p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow"
