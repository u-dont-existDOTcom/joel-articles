## Then I Found The Map Past Oneness
Then I found [Awakening to Reality](https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/) (AtR) and its main author, Soh Wei Yu. Soh 
repeatedly described his ordinary 24/7 experience as fully disjoint “mind-body drop:” each appearance self-aware without a 
knower. Hearing someone report that as ordinary daily experience gave me confidence that I might be able to experience it, 
too. He called this, “mind-body drop,” because he did not categorize nibbāna as an experience. But his description made life 
without a hidden knower imaginable enough to practice toward.
I owe him a great deal. Our vocabulary is not identical. I use “unification” for the functional coordination through which a 
person speaks, crosses a road, recognizes a friend, and answers a message, even when no separate knower is experienced. Most
of my own daily life includes that ordinary unification and often feels almost like everybody else’s day (well, if they’re happy).
AtR theory proposes, on the other hand, that unification of the senses is not required experientially for functioning in the world.
This really makes no sense to me at all, and I suspected for a long time that AtR practitioners were over-claiming, especially 
since Soh’s teacher, John Tan (aka Thusness) always scolded Soh for overclaiming. One example of overclaiming that I proved 
definitively was Soh’s claim to be in bliss 24/7. When I met him in Singapore a few years ago, I noticed he wasn’t smiling at all. I 
asked, “So, you’re in bliss 24/7?” He said, “Yes.” I said, “Even now?” He said, “Well, no, not now.” 😜
John Tan’s stage map was another major help. His [public stages post](https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2007/03/thusnesss￾six-stages-of-experience.html) says it is “nothing authoritative, merely for sharing purposes,” and John does not present the 
stages as universal nor prescriptive.
I used to think that John’s experience was mostly a modern theme. But now I can’t understand how I thought that. After all, I 
already knew from [MN 26](https://suttacentral.net/mn26/en/sujato) and [MN 36](https://suttacentral.net/mn36/en/sujato) that 
Buddha learned the mind-unification meditations (*jhānas*) from his pre-Buddhist teachers. While those teachers thought they 
were enlightened, the Buddha saw that the jhānas were not in themselves liberating, but they were very useful to develop a 
stable and pure mind from which to develop penetrative insight into the nature of reality. He called that type of 
discerning/penetrating insight *vipassanā*—and no, it doesn’t mean Goenka-style body-scanning as many people now believe.
Then we have the famous [MN 137](https://suttacentral.net/mn137/en/sujato), in which the Buddha advises to use unification of 
the mind to relinquish diversity, then to use non-identification to relinquish unity.
My own journey did include all of the meditative states of absorption from the four material jhānas up through the four 
immaterial absorptions/attainments (which are referred to in commentaries as the 5th–8th jhānas), thanks to practicing the 
techniques in Ajahn Brahm’s book, *Mindfulness, Bliss, and Beyond*. They were super amazing, psychedelic experiences which
I probably did need to have, in order to stop thinking that psychedelics were required to produce those states. In the first jhāna, 
my body dissolved into a cloud of blissful atoms that were in love with each other for idk how long! But I wouldn’t 😶‍🌫️💗🥰
make those “hard” jhānas a prerequisite, since they aren’t made such in the suttas.
The general recommendation is collectedness: gather the mind before trying to untie the gathering. You have to collect the trash
before you can toss it out. That’s made explicit in [AN 4.170](https://suttacentral.net/an4.170/en/sujato), where Ānanda, the 
Buddha’s main attendant, explains that all of the monastics who achieved full liberation did so with some form of cultivation of 
mental unification, or at least mental serenity, although not necessarily prior to insight practice.
Most people on a spiritual path these days, as before Buddha came along, don’t get beyond the unification stages. If they 
consider themselves to be advanced, like many of the [BatGap](https://batgap.com/) and [Liberation 
Unleashed](https://liberationunleashed.com/) claimers, they generally settle into oneness, a luminous witness, or cosmic 
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 4 / 51>
consciousness. They almost never talk about the suttas, which probably explains why. That is a major reason this article exists. 
The map and suttas gave me a direction beyond oneness, but they didn’t open me up by themselves.

## Nibbāna Has a Volume Knob
In my experience, nibbāna comes in degrees.
The Buddha did not issue monks little meters marked “3.7% unbound.” My percentages are playful shorthand for how much the
usual body-world-witness package has loosened at a given moment.
At something I would jokingly call 5% nibbāna, I become so peaceful and blissful that doing anything feels ridiculous. Once, at a
[Workaway](https://www.workaway.info/) farm in Malaysia, I remained around that level for four days. I had no interest in 
tasks, plans, or becoming an impressive person on a farm. Talking and interacting with people gradually brought the ordinary 
unification back toward 0-1%, which is useful when people expect answers instead of a beatific vegetable.

a7295953cafa_1672x941.png" caption="Mfw I realized why monastics beg for food" alt="" imageSize="None" fullscreen="None"]]
Most of the time, I function through a mostly unified body, one present scene, and some degree of witnessing—just like other 
people. Independently perfect finger-flows and a cloud of independent colors aren’t so useful for using a shovel. But what’s 
always different from before is I no longer take this unified sense of self to be more than a bad approximation of reality. Nature
assembled this unified self for survival purposes, so life could reproduce, and so forth. I get it now.
The finger event was one of many smaller, yet profound, nibbāna experiences that I’ve had since the earlier room unweaving 
event, which I call full nibbāna. You can get a small taste of that self-knot loosening via the guided meditation video later in this 
article. Even that can feel like discovering your name is Sisyphus, and you have carried a boulder on your back your entire life 
and, for the first time, it falls away. The release comes as a surprise because the burden had never been noticed.

spoken to, [including Soh Wei Yu & John Tan](https://share.gemini.google/GRDCthrtWxrB), and some advanced 
teachers like Ayasma Ariyadhammika, didn't understand the free will question, even though the Buddha is 
explicit about choices in [AN 3.61](https://suttacentral.net/an3.61/en/bodhi), and links determinism (or Dependent 
Origination) with ethics:
“Those who fall back on absence of cause and condition as the essential truth have no desire to do what should be 
done and to avoid doing what should not be done, nor do they make an effort in this respect."
Regarding the question of the existence of the self, the Buddha is famous for teaching "anattā" which means non￾self, but many have been confused about whether Buddha taught "no self" or just that the self is beyond 
conditions. In [SN 44.10](https://suttacentral.net/sn44.10/en/sujato), he refuses to directly say whether the self 
exists or not, because it would confuse an already confused person further. And in other suttas, such as [AN 6.61]
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 23 / 51>
(https://suttacentral.net/an6.61/en/sujato), he was misunderstood even by many of his senior monks with regard to
Dependent Origination, and his clarification remained cryptically terse.
So, while it's clear from the suttas that Buddha did succeed in liberating thousands of people (500 of them fully 
enilghtened when he died), the problem is that as the people who directly understood nibbāna, no self, and 
Dependent Origination, became rarer, the words kept getting interpreted by people who were still experiencing 
one unified consciousness and one world, and the hints stopped landing. I'm reminded of [Ud. 6.4]
(https://suttacentral.net/ud6.4/en/sujato), when the Buddha talks about how sectarian teachers quarrel over 
incomplete understandings of reality, much like blind men who each touch a different part of an elephant and 
argue over its true nature.
The canon itself says this kind of degradation was already happening in the Buddha's lifetime. In [SN 16.13]
(https://suttacentral.net/sn16.13/en/sujato), the arahant Mahā Kassapa asks why there are more training rules but 
fewer monks reaching final knowledge. The Buddha says counterfeit Dhamma gradually makes the true Dhamma 
disappear.
And the funniest part is that the words themselves may have survived surprisingly well. The discourses were 
transmitted orally for centuries, but they were transmitted communally, and the separately preserved early 
collections agree on an enormous amount. There are variants, rearrangements, and later material. Still, the words 
surviving is not the same as the meaning surviving. Even having the right pieces to the puzzle doesn't ensure you 
can put them together to see the picture on the box.

# Am I wrong to reveal the “secret” teaching?
I think the Buddha was deliberately vague on some dangerous central questions. In [SN 
44.10](https://suttacentral.net/sn44.10/en/sujato), he refuses to directly say whether the self exists or not, because it would 
confuse an already confused person further. And in other suttas, such as [AN 6.61](https://suttacentral.net/an6.61/en/sujato), he 
was misunderstood even by many of his senior monks, and his clarification remained cryptically terse. He seems to have been 
intentionally vague, requiring his students to piece together many of his discourses in an intelligent way to arrive at their own 
correct interpretation of nibbāna.
I don’t think he wanted Dhamma copycats. He wanted liberating insight to strike people like a lightning bolt, from their own 
realization, with just enough hints. When I studied for my Masters in Education at Temple University, we learned that when 
students learn by memorizing—what Paulo Freire called the [“banking” 
model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banking_model_of_education), depositing facts into students like empty containers—they 
don’t actually understand. Our job as teachers is to scaffold, [Vygotsky’s 
idea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_of_proximal_development) of bringing students only to the level just beyond where 
they presently understand.
So I think the Buddha was a brilliant teacher who understood that even if someone could learn the steps to reach nibbāna, if 
they hadn’t struggled to really grasp the importance of it deeply, it might not produce stream entry. But this problem-posing 
method—Freire’s alternative to banking education in [*Pedagogy of the 
Oppressed*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedagogy_of_the_Oppressed)—only worked while he was around (or his fully 
enlightened disciples were) to give the hints with proper meaning and scaffolding. Once they were gone, the same terseness 
could help hide the fact that the key was left in their pockets.
The Buddha seems to have made students load the ammunition before pulling the trigger. They were practicing the [Noble 
Eightfold Path](https://suttacentral.net/sn45.42-47/en/bodhi)—ethical conduct, meditation, and wisdom—because they wanted to
end rebirth and self-view while working to understand his scattered pointers about nibbāna. These days somebody can copy an
answer map from AtR or [Dharma Overground](http://dharmaoverground.org/) out of curiosity, competition, or the hope of 
claiming an attainment.
I am sure there can’t be stream entry without direct nibbāna, because then what exactly was recognized? Where did the 
intuitive certainty about the Dhamma come from? Pulling the trigger is necessary, but without the “ammunition” of 
accumulated desire and readiness for a radical insight aimed at ending rebirth and attachment to self-view, it only makes a cool
sound. The event can get filed under “awesome experience” while the old metaphysics quietly returns.
I’m publishing this because universal awareness is a comfortable place to spend twenty years while calling it the destination. 
The map that helped me move beyond it is hard to find. It can save a prepared person years or even lifetimes, and it might 
reach the rare ripe reader whom a private teacher might never meet. It can also create extremely convincing counterfeits.

# A Map Can Save Years—and Create Counterfeits
I think the Buddha was deliberately vague on some dangerous central questions in most of the suttas. He 
preferred to have his students piece together many of his discourses in an intelligent way to arrive at their own 
correct interpretation of nibbāna and anattā.. As explained in suttas like DN 3 and [MN 
56](https://suttacentral.net/mn56/en/sujato), he would only give clear instructions when the student’s “mind 
was ready, pliable, rid of hindrances, elated, and confident.”
These days, somebody can seemingly skip all that and just pull the trigger by copying a stages map from AtR or 
[Dharma Overground](http://dharmaoverground.org/) out of curiosity, competition, or the hope of claiming an 
attainment.
And nibbana is genuinely needed as the "trigger" for stream entry. If nibbāna is never experienced, then what 
exactly was recognized? Where did the intuitive certainty about the Dhamma come from? Again from MN 56 (and 
others) we see that when one attains stream entry, the “stainless, immaculate vision of the 
Dhamma” arises in them. It’s a powerful experience.
But without the “ammunition” of accumulated desire, practice, and readiness for a radical insight aimed at ending
rebirth and attachment to self-view, it only makes a cool sound. The event can get filed under “awesome 
experience” while the old metaphysics quietly returns. That's why Buddha taught [loving-kindness meditation]
(https://love.u-dont-exist.com), and the [Noble Eightfold Path](https://suttacentral.net/sn45.42-47/en/bodhi) -- 
ethical conduct, meditation, and wisdom about ending rebirth and self-view.
AwakeningToReality's map saved me, but I can't recommend it to others, for a number of reasons besides their 
lack of clarity about nibbana vs daily life, as mentioned before.

much of the insight while Soh systematized it into a searchable, teachable, generalized stage map. Soh sent me his

general, but maybe like me, he felt called to do so because nobody else was teaching anything of substance. As 
such, I think John allowed Soh to function as his mouthpiece. That's a smart arrangement: the student can 
promote the teacher while the teacher keeps low-key describing his own posts as "a simple sharing" or "nothing 
authoritative."

# What Science Can Map
If you can remember the last thunderstorm, then you don’t need neuroscience to know that the mind binds different sensory 
streams together. When you see lightning, you count the seconds until you hear the thunder. [Five seconds means the lightning 
was about a mile away.](https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-science-thunder) When they seem to happen at the same 
time, it means the lightning is close, but you understand they didn’t truly reach you at the same time. It’s just that our brain 
naturally merges stimuli that are close enough together.
But have you ever wondered whether several experiences can happen in parallel before—or without—the mind turning them 
into one apparent scene?
The neuroscientist [Semir Zeki](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10448004/) has argued for something close to this within 
vision: color, motion, form, and location are processed separately and can become conscious at different times. He calls the 
local results *microconsciousnesses*. In his theory, the completed visual picture is a later *macroconsciousness*: binding joins 
experiences that were already conscious.
The strongest evidence is visual, and Zeki’s conclusion reaches beyond the evidence. Still, he places consciousness before final 
unity instead of waiting for the finished scene. [Recurrent Processing Theory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16997611/) 
offers one possible mechanism: feedback inside a local sensory network may be enough for experience before the information 
becomes globally available. Whether local recurrence is sufficient to produce conscious experience remains disputed, though.
If you’re a mom, you might already know about doing many things at the same time, but science is still figuring that out. In one 
small [Descriptive Experience Sampling study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/
fnhum.2018.00494/full), intrepid researchers had the audacity to interrupt five people 180 times altogether and ask what was 
happening in experience at the exact moment before each beep. I would have smacked those researchers! But these subjects 
were more enlightened than me, and they repeatedly reported several things at once: sounds, bodily sensations, inner images, 
thoughts, and events inside and outside the body. This tells us one thing: experience isn’t obviously restricted to one item at a 
time.
But if you’re ready to jump down the Twilight Zone rabbit hole, then let’s look at the [split-brain 
experiments](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/140/7/2051/3892700). Don’t worry, these people weren’t sawed apart for no
reason. The corpus callosum, which joins the two brain hemispheres, had been surgically severed as an epilepsy treatment. 
These case studies point to how consciousness can function without a single owner. In these folks, the right hand can answer 
from information available to the left hemisphere, while the left hand can point to or draw what the right hemisphere knows. 
The speaking left hemisphere may say it saw nothing, while the left hand chooses the correct object.
In the famous chicken-claw and snow-scene experiment, the left hemisphere was shown a chicken claw, while the right 
hemisphere was shown snow. When given a choice of images, the right hand chose a chicken, and the left chose a shovel. The 
speaking left hemisphere could see the shovel choice, but it had never seen the snow scene. When asked why the shovel had 
been chosen, it invented a reason: the shovel was for cleaning the chicken shed. That’s called confabulation.
It doesn’t look like there was one overall owner or witness to all that. The speaking mind didn’t merely lack part of the event. It 
didn’t know that it lacked it, and it confidently explained behavior caused somewhere else. To me, that shows how readily we 
replace what actually happens in reality with a story that validates what we think reality should be, and it could explain how 
the story of the self emerges from simple conditions.
I first read a good summary of this research in Sam Harris’s *Waking Up*. Interestingly, I don’t think Harris followed his own 
evidence all the way through. He got rid of the personal self while leaving pure awareness behind as the final witness, which 
lands back in Advaita territory.
What’s still unclear from the split-brainers is whether there are actually two self-aware hemispheres, since we can’t easily ask 
the mostly non-speaking right hemisphere.
Although the model I’m proposing isn’t fully addressed by science, there is a recent theory from 2024 called [Nested Observer 
Windows](https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2024/1/niae010/7631826) which comes close. It models the brain as many 
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 33 / 51>
observer windows operating at different sizes and speeds, each generating its own cognitive stream while higher windows 
gather information from lower ones (talk about voyeurism!). The authors ask whether only the highest window is conscious, 
whether consciousness begins somewhere above a threshold, or whether every window has experience.
## The Limit of Science
None of this proves my interpretation of the room experience, but it gives the argument a third leg, which makes it more safe to
ride, like a tricycle. So where we’re at now with science is, it breaks down our ordinary perception into: 1) parallel processing, 
2) several simultaneous experiential strands, 3) fragmented access, and 4) a narrator that confuses its partial report with the 
whole system. It also contains serious theories in which local consciousness appears before final binding, which is part of what 
I’m talking about.
Unsurprisingly, though, you won’t find a study which documents the exact event I’m describing: visual, auditory, bodily, and 
other flows remaining self-presenting after the common scene, body-world, and witness disappear.
Objective experimentation may never be able to settle that last step. It can track how the color red is discriminated, 
remembered, joined with other information, and reported. But red itself never climbs out of the scanner and testifies that it was
already its own awareness. That part has to be investigated from inside experience.

## The Limit of Science
None of this proves my interpretation of the room experience, but it gives the argument a third leg, which makes it more safe to ride, 
like a tricycle. So where we’rewe're at now with science is, it breaks down our ordinary perception into: 1) parallel processing, 2) 
several simultaneous experiential strands, 3) fragmented access, and 4) a narrator that confuses its partial report with the whole 
system. It also contains serious theories in which local consciousness appears before final binding, which is part of what I’mI'm talking 
about.
Unsurprisingly, though, you won’twon't find a study which documents the exact event I’m describing: visual, auditory, bodily, and other
flows remaining self-presenting after the common scene, body-world, and witness disappear.
Objective experimentation may never be able to settle that last step. It can track how the color red is discriminated, remembered, joined
with other information, and reported. But red itself never climbs out of the scanner and testifies that it was already its own awareness.

## What the suttas say about nibbāna as the gate to stream-entry
The main article already gave my personal evidence for nibbāna as a necessary but insufficient condition for stream entry. 
What the suttas say on this question is disputed.
The suttas do not present stream entry as finally agreeing with Buddhist doctrine, nor as accepting the Buddha as your savior. 
The Buddha is concise in [SN 25.1–10](https://suttacentral.net/sn25):
> “Someone who accepts these teachings after deliberating them with a degree of wisdom is called a follower of teachings. 
They’ve stepped into the sure path, they’ve stepped into the level of the true person, and they’ve passed beyond the level of the 
untrue person. They can’t do any deed which would make them be reborn in hell, the animal realm, or the ghost realm. They 
can’t die without realizing the fruit of stream-entry. Someone who understands and sees these principles is called a stream￾enterer.”
The special seeing is further detailed in suttas like [AN 8.22](https://suttacentral.net/an8.22/en/sujato). In this sutta, when the 
Buddha saw Ugga was ready, he gave him precise teachings that led him past the gate of stream entry. Ugga explains: “I saw, 
attained, understood, and fathomed the Dhamma. I went beyond doubt, got rid of indecision, and became self-assured and 
independent of others regarding the Teacher’s instructions.”
[AN 10.92](https://suttacentral.net/an10.92/en/sujato) adds direct understanding of dependent origination/arising, confidence in 
the Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha, and ethical restraint.
[AN 3.87](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN3_87.html) gives the classic three fetters that break at stream entry, which
I explained in the main article. Most fetters still remain, to lesser degrees, after that, disappearing at later stages. In my 
experience, [MN 48](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN48.html) nails it: defilements can still arise, but they no longer
take over in the same way, and they’re recognized much faster. [SN 13.1](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN13_1.html) 
compares the suffering that remains after stream entry to dust on a fingernail beside the whole earth; in my experience, that’s 
vastly exaggerated.

#What “Nibbāna” Is Actually Saying
The original usage of the word nibbāna was to describe a fire going out. My book treated “unweaving” as the 
word’s direct etymology. That was wrong.
Buddhaghosa wrote a famous systematization of the Buddha's teaching in the fifth century, called the 
Visuddhimagga. In it, he derives nibbāna from vāna, “binding” or “fastening”, based on the understanding that 
craving joins, binds, and laces one becoming to the next. Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu agrees with this explanation of 
nibbāna as more fitting, even if less academically strict. He traces it back to the early fire image also. A fire 
depends on fuel (upādāna, the same word used for clinging or sustenance) and it becomes free when that binding 
to fuel ends.
That is why Ṭhānissaro's sutta translations use “unbinding” for nibbāna. My argument for the meaning of nibbāna
will be described in the next sections. Like Buddhaghosa and Ṭhānissaro, I believe the strict etymological 
definition is not as helpful as an interpretive one for truly understanding the word.
The suttas themselves already speak in tangles, knots, and nets. SN 1.23 describes a “tangle within” and a “tangle 
without,” then says the arahants' (fully enlightened ones') tangle is disentangled. Dhammapada 90 says the 
liberated person has abandoned every gantha (knot, tie, or bond). Dhammapada 180 calls craving jālinī (like a net,
ensnaring). Unbinding and disentangling are deeply native ways to describe liberation from the binding up of the 
senses.
What came apart in my “room experience” was the fastening of separate sensory flows which ordinarily cohere 
into one established consciousness, one self, one world, and renewed existence. The room looked as though it 
were unweaving, and the suttas’ tangles, knots, and nets make that visual metaphor feel less accidental.
My old “hard nibbāna / soft nibbāna” language was also my taxonomy. But here’s a better breakdown of how I’d 
explain it now:
Partial loosening: a finger, bodily region, or pair of sense fields becomes less unified while the witness and world 
remain.
Nibbāna: the unified witness and scene disappear while sensory appearances remain self-presenting.
Full nibbāna: same as regular nibbāna, but not limited to sensations in the body.

Ordinary functioning afterward: the body-world package assembles again.

#How Contact Builds a World
The way I understand how my "world" of coherent experience is constructed starts with my finger and room 
experiences.
Blissful color-flows were each present on their own, with no witness behind them. As Soh likes to say, there is no 
awareness apart from the awared. Meaning, experience is already aware. It's tautological. If we were to say that 
there is an experience apart from the awareness of it, then we run into the age-old riddle of what that experience 
would be without awareness of it.
These appearances or awareness flows meet locally, like a MeetUp group (although MeetUp actually banned me 
for trying to organize a study group about this, which is why I'm just a Substack author instead of the next [Jim 
Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Jones), perhaps). I call those MeetUps “initial contact.” That is my term, 
broader than the Pāli word for contact, phassa. Those local MeetUps can help organize a sense door and the sense￾consciousness that goes with it.
SN 12.44 gives the canonical sequence: eye and forms condition eye-consciousness; the meeting of all three is 
contact.
The crucial distinction here is between awareness, and consciousness. The Pali does not have separate nouns for 
each, but it does distinguish sensations themselves (which I refer to as awareness flows) from the knowledge of 
the sensations (which I refer to as consciousness, or reflexive awareness).
Notice that in SN 12.44, consciousness, and then feeling, arises dependent on one thing perceiving the other. And 
in my borrowed AtR terminology, there's still no primordial awareness that comes before appearance-flows. 
That's one difference between Buddhism and Advaita or new-age spirituality.

#Stream Entry and the Dream Running Out
Theravāda Buddhism describes four stages of awakening: stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, and final 
enlightenment, called arahantship. For this article, the first one matters.
AN 10.92 shows that stream-enterers gain confidence in the Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha (the community of 
people who have directly realized the path, although can be sometimes defined more broadly as Buddhists), 
ethical restraint, and direct understanding of causality. AN 3.87 ties stream-entry to the ending of identity view, 
uncertainty about the path, and grasping at habits and practices as sufficient in themselves. It calls the stream￾enterer certain, headed for awakening, and no longer destined for states of woe. The later stages of awakening 
keep weakening passion, aversion, and delusion until the remaining fetters and effluents end.
##Stream entry is easier than it seems
While stream-entry is rare these days while alive, the good news is that according to [AN 4.191]
(https://suttacentral.net/an4.191/en/sujato), you're almost guaranteed to become a stream enterer within a couple 
lifetimes if you try. Even if you die unmindful of nibbana, because you practiced the Eightfold Path to purification,
you may end up just in a heavenly realm in the next life (which I recall as one of my past lives). If you develop 
deep experiential insight into the dhamma beyond intellectual understanding in this life, you're guaranteed to

##Ending the dream of samsara
Maybe the greatest prize for the stream-enterer is their “release date:” AN 3.88 says there are at most seven 
further lifetimes before final awakening. There is [a lot of modern academic evidence for rebirth]
(https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0AtTM9hgCDw). But who has those lives if no permanent self exists? 
Buddhists do not imagine a soul traveling from body to body, since that would be a belief in an unchanging self￾essence. Instead, we understand rebirth as causal continuity without a traveler (often called a mind-stream): 
craving and clinging condition becoming, and becoming conditions birth. The later Buddhist Milindapañha 
compares the process of rebirth to one lamp lighting another. The flame doesn't migrate, yet the second lighting is 
not unrelated to the first.

##Why nibbana is "awakening"
The lucid-dream comparison from earlier also shapes how I understand the seven-life limit said to be had by the 
stream-enterer. Once the dream is known as a dream, it becomes naturally difficult to remain stuck in it, similar to
how hard it is to be absorbed in a movie when the lights go on and you remember you're in a theatre. If you've 
ever had a lucid dream, you know what I'm talking about.
I used to attribute “life is like a dream” directly to the historical Buddha. The famous stanza I loved actually comes
from the later Mahāyāna Diamond Sutra, which puts some words in his mouth, although the early Pāli texts offer 
closely related images. SN 22.95 compares form to foam, feeling to a bubble, perception to a mirage, and 
consciousness to a magic trick. Dhammapada 170 asks us to see the world as a bubble and mirage.
The verse is still beautiful. Here is a widely circulated English rendering:
So should one view all of the fleeting world:·
A star at dawn, a bubble in a stream,
A flash of lightning in a summer cloud,
A flickering lamp, a phantom, and a dream.
For me, nibbāna was really like waking up from a dream, except that nobody woke up. Reasoning can make the 
dream doubtful. The event made doubt about the dream impossible to reverse.
