# What “Already Perfect” Means

Life has its ups and downs. The ups feel great, but the downs are the diamonds in the rough! I’ve always had my deepest realizations and releases when I was fed up with life. I started thinking, the average life goes like this: You have a few birthdays, try to satisfy desires as they arise, watch your health decline, and then die.

[[SUBSTACK_IMAGE 02 src="https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/915fb5a5-dd91-4c7e-b3a7-78b0e479490f_1467x978.jpeg" caption="" alt="" imageSize="None" fullscreen="None"]]

Maybe, if you had a good life, you leave the world a better place as well, and those who survive you continue through the same cycle. Then the question becomes: what’s next? A lot of people are afraid that nothing is next -- that there is no next. But maybe they’re afraid of the wrong thing.

And if Buddhism is right about rebirth -- I return to that evidence later on -- life is a beginningless cycle called *saṃsāra*, the cycle of rebirth and suffering. The childish fantasy of immortality might be better understood as conditions recycling, and the subscription you forgot you had renewing itself without asking, like most of my Amazon Subscribe & Save orders.

By “already perfect,” I mean that everything -- including our efforts to improve it -- is happening according to natural laws. Pain, abuse, illness, and injustice still hurt and still call for a response, but they don’t hold the same ultimate psychological power over us when we see the light of reality at the end of [Plato’s Cave](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SWlUKJIMge4).

[[SUBSTACK_IMAGE 03 src="https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/1ee8b871-5a97-42bb-8280-712038631b1a_1672x941.png" caption="Plato’s Cave" alt="" imageSize="None" fullscreen="None"]]

I originally stole the title of this essay about 15 years ago from a question asked by a man dying of cancer, on the now defunct FB group of [AwakeningToReality](http://awakeningtoreality.com/). As he was in the long process of dying, he thought to himself, “What if everything is already perfect without me?” For him, it was a liberating insight, while for someone at a different stage of life, it might be more challenging. This article itself is version 2.0 of my [old essay,](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/what-if-everything-is-already-perfect?r=5vdc6m) which was one page long. That was already perfect, so why on Earth did I spend the past weeks rewriting it? 🤪

Because everything is just conditions, things work and kinda make sense; otherwise, it would be a bunch of random chaos. Even if we don’t fully understand anything, electrons keep orbiting according to quantum rules around atomic nuclei, the toaster predictably toasts, children [hopefully grow up](https://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/) (maybe a bad example), and the sun keeps rising and setting. There was never an electron that felt like it was in the wrong orbital and wished it had ended up around a more handsome nucleus! So, if everything is so perfect, why do we want things to be different? Maybe it’s just a hilarious misunderstanding!

[[SUBSTACK_IMAGE 04 src="https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/84cf9dd6-df38-4cd0-a552-bd11e580c167_1254x1254.png" caption="" alt="" imageSize="None" fullscreen="None"]]

How would your life be different if, instead of having to drink some whack Amazon jungle plants and go on a Hero’s Quest to figure this out (like I did), you learned in kindergarten that nothing happens apart from conditions?

Actually, if you ask a kindergartner, you might realize they already understand this before it gets stamped out of them. I’ve seen a number of kids, when parents tell them that “God created the universe,” respond: “Then who created God?”

[[SUBSTACK_IMAGE 05 src="https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/d9420712-d8c6-48b4-9ea6-cf78ed56cd17_1672x941.png" caption="The identity illusion" alt="" imageSize="None" fullscreen="None"]]

Libertarian free will -- a chooser standing outside all conditions and causing a choice from nowhere -- is logically impossible. Choices still happen and they matter precisely because they become conditions for what happens next. Attention, speech, conduct, relationships, and meditation shape what follows. There is no self outside the web pulling its strings. Meditative training is the web learning to pull differently.

Once the little controller disappears, the mind can hide in a subtler place: “Fine, then I am the awareness watching everything happen.” I cannot find that witness either. What I find are appearances, followed by the processes that gather them into a body, world, and owner.

“Already perfect without you” means experience is complete before anyone owns it. Pain remains pain. A tree falls in the woods and makes a sound with nobody there to hear it. A color colors the coloring book with nobody holding the crayon. It sounds a bit abstract, but I’ll give you a direct “pointer” from my experience:

## Your fingers are already enlightened, but your hand is deluded.

One time about 15 years ago, I fell asleep in my car while meditating. It was parked, don’t worry!

Before “I” woke up to notice them, my fingers came online all by themselves. Each finger was its own complete experiential flow. I mean it, there were literally 10 distinct, self-presenting, indescribably blissful, and absolutely perfect, finger flows happening at the same time.

Then “my” mind came back, the perfection vanished, and “I” realized how silly those perfections were: “Oh, those are just my fingers!” 😂

That was it, the magic was over.

The finger episode was a small replay of something much bigger that had happened earlier. That event extended across the room: colors and other appearances remained as infinitely perfect, disjoint flows instead of forming one witnessed scene. The finger version had the same basic texture, localized to the bodily field.

[[SUBSTACK_NATIVE_VIDEO 06 mediaUploadId="53c03e22-e18d-4807-9d64-6cf05faa9ec9"]]

The fingers and the full room unweaving experiences were incredible, and I understand them to be versions of the deathless, perfect reality which the Buddha called *nibbāna*, the Buddhist goal usually translated as “unbinding.” The reason nibbāna matters so much for me is because it was what launched me into Buddhist **stream entry.** Stream entry is the first stage of Buddhist enlightenment, where the ending of rebirth becomes irreversible, and it’s important because it changes you permanently. There’s a lot of confusion and debate over these two key terms, which is the reason for the rest of this article.

# How I Mistook An Interpretation For A Sutta

Let me correct something I’ve been saying for about 15 years. I kept reading the [*Bāhiya Sutta*, Udāna 1.10](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/KN/Ud/ud1_10.html) -- *sutta* means teaching -- as though the Buddha literally said there was no self *between the different sensory streams*. He didn’t explicitly say that.

I didn’t invent the reading out of nowhere, though, nor was it a Mandela Effect as I suspected. Soh Wei Yu and John Tan at AwakeningToReality.com relied heavily on [Ajahn Amaro’s contemplative rendering](https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2008/01/ajahn-amaro-on-non-duality-and.html), also published in [*Small Boat, Great Mountain*](https://www.abhayagiri.org/media/books/amaro_small_boat_great_mountain.pdf). I never understood this was not the consensus literal translation from *Pāli*, the language of this early Buddhist text.

It’s such a beautiful sutta that I’ve read and repeated hundreds of times. I’d advise you to check it out to see if you also connect with it deeply. I will summarize here that basically, Bāhiya was an ascetic, not a Buddhist, who thought he was enlightened, or on the path to enlightenment. A spirit came to him and told him he was nowhere near enlightenment. Bāhiya humbly asked, “Then who is enlightened?” The spirit told him, “The Buddha is enlightened, go find him.”

So, Bāhiya asked ChatGPT where to find the Buddha and used Google Maps for the 3 day walk there (just kidding, idk how people did anything back then!). When he finally found the Buddha, it was bad timing. The Buddha was on almsround begging for food and didn’t want to answer any questions. Bāhiya pressed him 3 times (3 being the magic number back then), saying “life is uncertain, you never know when it may end, please teach me the *Dhamma* briefly.” Dhamma means the Buddha’s teaching and the reality it points to.

This is how the Buddha finally advised Bāhiya to practice, in the pithiest instructions ever (Amaro’s interpretive translation):

> *In the seen, there is only the seen,*
> *in the heard, there is only the heard,*
> *in the sensed, there is only the sensed,*
> *in the cognized, there is only the cognized.*
> *Thus you should see that*
> *indeed there is no thing here;*
> *this, Bāhiya, is how you should train yourself.*
> *Since, Bāhiya, there is for you*
> *in the seen, only the seen,*
> *in the heard, only the heard,*
> *in the sensed, only the sensed,*
> *in the cognized, only the cognized,*
> *and you see that there is no thing here,*
> *you will therefore see that*
> *indeed there is no thing there.*
> *As you see that there is no thing there,*
> *you will see that*
> *you are therefore located neither in the world of this,*
> *nor in the world of that,*
> *nor in any place*
> *betwixt the two.*
> *This alone is the end of suffering.*

The key here is that Amaro’s version says there is “no thing here” and “no thing there,” then explains that as no substantial subject, object, or self **between them**, then AtR and I extended “between” from subject and object to the different sensory streams.

The usual translations say something narrower. They agree with this part: When there is only the seen, heard, sensed, and cognized, there is no “you” connected with that. But this is where they differ: Then you are neither here (in this world/life), beyond (beyond this world/life), nor between the two: “just this is the end of suffering.” At first glance, that does look the same, but the translators explain the meanings of here/there differently from Amaro. The passage thus does not explain how the mind binds simultaneous disjoint sensory streams into one experience of the self & world.

So when I say “sounds hear themselves,” I’m giving a direct-experience pointer inspired by “in the heard, only the heard,” while extrapolating a bit beyond the original Pāli. Another sutta, [SN 12.44](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN12_44.html), and the other contact discourses explain that the “world,” aka saṃsāra, arises from contact between sense organs and sense objects, which creates sense consciousnesses, feeling, and all the rest. It seems almost obvious this is pointing at multisensory binding as the basis of the mind, but that part is not explicit:

> *The Blessed One said: “And what is the origination of the world? Dependent on the eye & forms there arises eye-consciousness. The meeting of the three is contact. From contact as a requisite condition comes feeling. From feeling as a requisite condition comes craving. From craving as a requisite condition comes clinging/sustenance. From clinging/sustenance as a requisite condition comes becoming. From becoming as a requisite condition comes birth. From birth as a requisite condition, then aging-&-death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, & despair come into play. This is the origination of the world.”*

# I Got to Infinity Before I Understood Nibbāna & No-self

Maybe I was a meditator in a recent past life or something. My mom told me that when I was two or three, I told my mother, “I think this is one of my last lifetimes.” As a child, I also had recurring dreams in which there was only infinite vastness and absolutely nothing else -- no “me” who was vast. Just vastness.

Skip forward a bit.
I was 26 when my high school history students told me they didn’t want to learn. I guess I was the one learning. They taught me I was dumb to have spent years training for a job that was apparently worthless. That’s when I started taking way toooo much ayahuasca, to get a different kind of training than the one normally prescribed by society. (Now I use my [Haoma protocol](http://soma.u-dont-exist.com). It’s much nicer than regular ayahuasca.)

Each time I took a big aya dose, I felt like I was dying, and I actually had no idea I was just experiencing the effects of the drug. After the dying part, when I finally let go and my mind exploded, there was no more me, and no more body. I became the entire universe, seeing everything, being everything, knowing and loving everything. Each universe would expand for billions of years, contract to a point, and then explode. Those explosions were insane!

[MN 36](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN36.html) says that on his awakening night the Buddha remembered lives across “many eons of cosmic contraction” and expansion. I hadn’t read it yet, so it didn’t prime me, but the [coinkydink](https://youtu.be/RtuBerg5T4o?si=eVykSVS5Wht6sl2e) was cool.

[[SUBSTACK_IMAGE 08 src="https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/dda6435d-a89d-44de-a323-678b3305e571_736x1308.jpeg" caption="Tfw the universes are beginningless" alt="" imageSize="None" fullscreen="None"]]

These unity experiences could be insanely euphoric. Heck, unification is arguably the main motivating factor for humanity, if ya know what I mean. Expand that urge across the universe and it can resemble sex multiplied by infinity. I have spent hours yelling “OH MY GOD” after experiences like that.

Almost everything I found in the modern seeker marketplace confirmed my experience was basically the ultimate one. Browse through the teachers on [Buddha at the Gas Pump](https://batgap.com/), and universal consciousness is like the default spiritual answer. *Advaita*, the Hindu nondual tradition, and its modern *neo-Advaita* offshoot supplied a complete philosophy for what I’d already experienced. It was beautiful, intoxicating, and comparatively easy: we are all one universal, blissful awareness.

Many people get stuck at that stage, because it’s a nice one. But I’m a curious cat with less than 9 lives left, and I wanted to know about this guy called Buddha who was strangely absent from [Buddha at the Gas Pump](https://batgap.com/) interviews!

[[SUBSTACK_IMAGE 09 src="https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/a7fba44b-aff1-4ec3-9533-2f85335252cf_1448x1086.png" caption="The irony was palpable" alt="" imageSize="None" fullscreen="None"]]

So, I started branching off into Buddhist books to see if I could challenge myself & go further. The Buddha’s habit of breaking experience into aggregates -- the five changing components of a person -- sense bases, contacts, feelings, and conditioned events seemed almost perversely backward. I had found the One. Why was this man taking everything apart?

Most Buddhist books I read felt like a waste of time. They copied the same lists and told me there was no self, usually without one useful paragraph about what the absence of unified awareness actually felt like.

Later I found out that part of the uselessness was built into the monastic rules. The [*Vinaya*](https://www.dhammatalks.org/vinaya/bmc/Section0000.html), the monastic code of *Theravāda* Buddhism -- the oldest surviving Buddhist school -- includes [*Pācittiya* 8](https://www.dhammatalks.org/vinaya/bmc/Section0016.html), a rule requiring confession if a monk tells anyone who isn't fully ordained about his own genuine "superior human state," including meditative absorption, psychic knowledge, or a path or fruit of awakening. And a monk who makes a false attainment claim faces [*Pārājika* 4](https://www.dhammatalks.org/vinaya/bmc/Section0010.html), a defeat rule that permanently ends his status as a monk. So as usual, CYA (cover your ass) applies for anyone with some status to protect. Theravāda monk-authors have an excellent reason to repeat lists, explain techniques, and remain maddeningly vague about what they themselves have actually experienced. It's like when you go to a medical doctor and ask them if they ever tried any of the medicine they prescribe, and they tell you they studied medicine.

## Then I Found The Map Past Oneness

Then I found [Awakening to Reality](https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/) (AtR) and its main author, Soh Wei Yu. Soh repeatedly described his ordinary 24/7 experience as fully disjoint "mind-body drop:" each appearance self-aware without a knower. Hearing someone report that as ordinary daily experience gave me confidence that I might be able to experience it, too. He called this "mind-body drop" because he did not categorize nibbāna as an experience. But his description made life without a hidden knower imaginable enough to practice toward.

I owe him a great deal. Our vocabulary is not identical. I use "unification" for the functional coordination through which a person speaks, crosses a road, recognizes a friend, and answers a message, even when no separate knower is experienced. Most of my own daily life includes that ordinary unification and often feels almost like everybody else's day (well, if they're happy).

One key difference I now have with AtR theory: Soh says that unification of the senses is not required experientially for functioning in the world. This really makes no sense to me at all, and I suspected for a long time that AtR practitioners were overclaiming, especially since Soh's teacher, John Tan (aka Thusness), always scolded Soh for overclaiming. One example of overclaiming that I proved definitively was Soh's claim to be in bliss 24/7. When I met him in Singapore a few years ago, I noticed he wasn't smiling at all. I asked, "So, you're in bliss 24/7?" He said, "Yes." I said, "Even now?" He said, "Well, no, not now." 😜

John Tan's stage map was another major help. His [public stages post](https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2007/03/thusnesss-six-stages-of-experience.html) says it is "nothing authoritative, merely for sharing purposes," and John does not present the stages as universal nor prescriptive.

I used to think that John's experience was mostly a modern theme. But now I can't understand how I thought that. After all, I already knew from [MN 26](https://suttacentral.net/mn26/en/sujato) and [MN 36](https://suttacentral.net/mn36/en/sujato) that Buddha learned the mind-unification meditations (*jhānas*) from his pre-Buddhist teachers. While those teachers thought they were enlightened, the Buddha saw that the jhānas were not in themselves liberating, but they were very useful to develop a stable and pure mind from which to develop penetrative insight into the nature of reality. He called that type of discerning/penetrating insight *vipassanā* -- and no, it doesn't mean [Goenka-style body-scanning](https://goenka.u-dont-exist.com) as many people now believe.

Then we have the famous [MN 137](https://suttacentral.net/mn137/en/sujato), in which the Buddha advises using unification of the mind to relinquish diversity, then using non-identification to relinquish unity.

My own journey did include all of the meditative states of absorption from the four material jhānas up through the four immaterial absorptions/attainments (which are referred to in commentaries as the 5th-8th jhānas), thanks to practicing the techniques in Ajahn Brahm's book, *Mindfulness, Bliss, and Beyond*. They were super amazing psychedelic experiences which I probably did need to have in order to stop thinking that psychedelics were required to produce those states. In the first jhāna, my body dissolved into a cloud of blissful atoms that were in love with each other 😶‍🌫️💗🥰 for idk how long! But I wouldn't make those "hard" jhānas a prerequisite, since they aren't made such in the suttas.

The general recommendation is collectedness: gather the mind before trying to untie the unity. You have to collect the trash before you can toss it out. That's made explicit in [AN 4.170](https://suttacentral.net/an4.170/en/sujato), where Ānanda, the Buddha's main attendant, explains that all of the monastics who achieved full liberation did so with some form of cultivation of mental unification, or at least mental serenity, although not necessarily prior to insight practice.

Most people on a spiritual path these days, as before Buddha came along, don't get beyond the unification stages. If they consider themselves to be advanced, like many of the [BatGap](https://batgap.com/) and [Liberation Unleashed](https://liberationunleashed.com/) claimers, they generally settle into oneness, a luminous witness, or cosmic consciousness. They almost never talk about the suttas, which probably explains why. That is a major reason this article exists. The map and suttas gave me a direction beyond oneness, but they didn't open me up by themselves.

## Desperation Did The Trick

Some people go to retreats whenever they can, hoping to experience what happened to me. I didn’t have the money for that, but luckily, I did have a severely unstable marriage that was making me feel like I had completely failed at life!

My first wife had become unexpectedly pregnant with our son about a month before I had to commit her to a psych ward when I found out how serious her mental issues were. Imagining my life as a poor guy trying to raise a kid (or more) with a woman like this was too much. I would have killed myself if I had not believed that suicide would simply lead to a worse rebirth.

Speaking of avoiding a worse rebirth, I had just watched a beautiful documentary called [*For the Benefit of All Beings*](https://garchen.net/for-the-benefit-of-all-beings/) which affected me deeply. It was the biographical documentary of a Tibetan Buddhist master named Garchen Rinpoche, who spent roughly twenty years in a Chinese prison labor camp after fighting the Chinese invasion of Tibet with CIA weapons & training. He knew he was going to hell for killing people, but he felt he had no choice. And in fact, he already was in hell in his own mind of hatred.

Ironically, it was in that brutal Chinese prison camp where he found his true master. His master taught him to love the Chinese and be happy that he was growing rice for them, since the people were not their government. That’s where his practice of loving-kindness blossomed, and he became a light of love upon the world. I thought: if he could find love there, perhaps I could stop treating my own hell as an excuse.

So anyway, back to desperate little me, in bed, next to my then-wife that I was afraid of. I had just watched this beautiful documentary. I was inspired by it, as well as by Soh Wei Yu’s experience of life, and his explanation that everything is just conditions. I began chanting the beautiful mantra from the documentary while I meditated, with the intent of experiencing what Soh Wei Yu was pointing at. Eventually, I fell asleep. Then I woke up, sat up, and my entire awareness coalesced around my navel. Then it seemed to leave my body. That part had happened before, and usually produced oneness with the room, world, or universe.

This time, my awareness dispersed, or popped like a balloon! 🎈

Awareness as one thing was gone. I was gone. What remained were the room’s color-light flows, all experiencing themselves in indescribable perfection. They were disjoint, but not isolated: the flows ran into and through one another, connected everywhere without becoming one experience or one witnessed scene. Nothing observed them from behind. There was no cosmic knower celebrating its achievement. There was also no black void. The flows were infinitely more intimate, beautiful, and alive than ordinary perception, while possessing no owner at all.

This is what I refer to now as full nibbāna: unified consciousness disassembled, multisensory binding untangled and not limited to what the sense organs could perceive, while the room’s color-light flows remained, seeing themselves from every corner and angle, not just from the angle the eyes could see them from. The fingers knowing themselves experience was still nibbāna to me, but not *full* nibbāna because it was limited to the body itself still.

It felt entirely different from the ayahuasca ego deaths: no cosmic orgasms and no screaming. The bliss was deeper, subtler, and vastly more peaceful. I believe the [*Dhammapada*](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/KN/Dhp/Ch15.html) is right to call unbinding the foremost ease -- the traditional line often rendered “nibbāna is the highest happiness.”

I do not know how long this lasted. Time belonged to the unified world that was absent. Eventually the ordinary body, room, witness, and biography formed again.

I believe that was stream entry. If a coherent argument knocks that down, good. Turning it into an unquestionable spiritual identity would be a fairly embarrassing souvenir from an experience of no identity. Most people avoid claiming attainments so they won’t sound boastful. I don’t want to boast either, but I do want to be honest.

Although the suttas are silent on whether full nibbāna can happen without stream entry, I now believe it can. Stream entry requires an irreversible recognition and path shift: identity view -- the belief that one of the changing components of experience is really “me” or belongs to “me” -- loses its old authority; the Dhamma, meaning reality and the Buddha’s teaching about it, stops being merely faith or conceptual understanding; and fundamental doubt about the path breaks. My position is that both direct nibbāna and that irreversible shift are required. Full nibbāna supplies the necessary trigger, but the event alone is not sufficient. The detailed sutta case is in the nerdy appendix.

But since then, life has never felt like a Chinese finger trap as it did before. The sense of self and “my story” went from something like infinite authority to something much smaller and harder to believe. Selfishness and suffering dropped dramatically. I never wanted to kill myself again. I became angry less often (even after my first wife tried to kill my family and stole my son), although anger had not been my dominant problem; I became sad far less often and felt trapped far less. By some measures I behaved less foolishly. I had not been a habitual liar, but honesty became more serious and more natural: I was less inclined to hide or leave things unsaid.

Ironically, realizing that the self I had taken to be real did not exist gave me a new confidence -- the way confidence can surge inside a dream once you know it is a dream. It didn’t immediately liberate me from the marital situation nor prevent future bad relationship decisions, but I never again felt the infinite desperation of being the owner of an irreparable life.

## Nibbāna Has a Volume Knob

In my experience, nibbāna comes in degrees.

This part is not laid out explicitly in the suttas. [AN 11.12](https://suttacentral.net/an11.12/en/sujato) says that a noble disciple can, at any time, reach ease and joy by recollecting the Buddha, the Dhamma, the *Saṅgha* -- the community of realized disciples -- their own ethical conduct and generosity, or the qualities they share with heavenly beings. In my experience, that is true, but there can also be physical loosening of the sense of unification. My percentages are playful shorthand for how much the usual body-world-witness package has loosened at a given moment.

At something I would jokingly call 5% nibbāna, I become so peaceful and blissful that doing anything feels ridiculous. Once, at a [Workaway](https://www.workaway.info/) farm in Malaysia, I remained around that level for four days. I had no interest in tasks, plans, or becoming an impressive person on a farm. Talking and interacting with people gradually brought the ordinary unification back toward 0-1%, which is useful when people expect answers instead of a beatific vegetable.

[[SUBSTACK_IMAGE 11 src="https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/8fcf4352-a32b-45cd-b8e8-a7295953cafa_1672x941.png" caption="Mfw I realized why monastics beg for food" alt="" imageSize="None" fullscreen="None"]]

Most of the time, I function through a mostly unified body, one present scene, and some degree of witnessing -- just like other people. Independently perfect finger-flows and a cloud of independent colors aren't so useful for using a shovel. But what's always different from before is I no longer take this unified sense of self to be more than a bad approximation of reality. Nature assembled this unified self for survival purposes, so life could reproduce, and so forth. I get it now.

The finger event was one of many smaller, yet profound, nibbāna experiences that I've had since the earlier room unweaving event, which I call full nibbāna. You can get a small taste of that self-knot loosening via the guided meditation video later in this article. Even that can feel like discovering your name is Sisyphus, and you have carried a boulder on your back your entire life and, for the first time, it falls away. The release comes as a surprise because the burden had never been noticed.

## Are disappearing flashbacks dangerous or cool?

For roughly fifteen years I have also had semi-random micro-disappearances in which the felt “I” vanishes, usually with bliss rather than distress. Each one includes, in varying proportions, a high-pitched *nāda*-like inner sound and a dissolution of the felt self. Nāda is a Sanskrit word for sound; some Hindu contemplative traditions use it for an inwardly perceived sound. Sometimes the sound dominates. Sometimes the dissolution does. This is the closest traditional comparison I know.

The effect often begins on one side of the felt head-space. By tuning attention, I can sometimes bring the other side into it. As it deepens, the bodily sense of the head or brain area can dissolve along with the sense of existing. The tissue is still there, as far as I know. 😂 At first I wondered whether I should stop riding bicycles. What if “I” disappeared during a turn and the bicycle continued its own spiritual journey into a ditch? So far I have never crashed, and I have always returned.

A friend told me he had much stronger episodes while driving: for long stretches, his own car and the traffic seemed to disappear. He never had an accident either. I do not know why. Luck is one possibility; so is a misunderstanding of what was happening, some feature of how these states operate, a combination, or something else.

# A Peak State Can Be Useless Or Worse

One reason I hesitate to talk about enlightenment these days is that spectacular experiences do not reliably transform people.

To give but one example from my life, after months of hearing me like a broken record saying, "Everything is just conditions, all your probs are imaginary!" my first wife entered some kind of [*satori*](https://wikipedia.org/wiki/Satori) (peak awakening experience) for about a week. She became the loveliest presence -- peaceful, gentle, and seemingly free of the personality machinery that had made our lives so difficult.

Then she started slowly coming back to her old self. I asked, "Don't you want to go back to being enlightened?"

She said, "No, that's done now."

Her answer still blows my mind. Some people are content with less. They don't need so much enlightenment. Ironic! 😅

[[SUBSTACK_IMAGE 13 src="https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/f5f6c6be-969d-422f-a57f-5866974503c6_1672x941.png" caption="Some people are content with less. They don’t need so much enlightenment." alt="" imageSize="None" fullscreen="None"]]

My mom did something equally strange while sick with cancer, roughly ten years before she died. Her hip was in terrible pain, so she had a loving, two-way conversation with it. She told me her whole body then dissolved into infinite cosmic orgasms for hours. She never tried the practice again. Later, she forgot the experience altogether.

As they say, "life is stranger than fiction." It really does seem like a dream, to me anyway.

I'm reminded of that Jack Kornfield book I read at the time, [*After the Ecstasy, the Laundry*](https://jackkornfield.com/after-the-ecstasy-the-laundry/). Not to endorse Kornfield about other things, but he is right that a real satori can still leave the moldy sheets in the closet. Less selfishness, less grasping, and more capacity to love ordinary people lack the glamour of that peak, but at least they remain useful on Tuesday.

I know a few people who appear to have had several genuine nibbāna openings. Afterward, their interpretation kept moving between Advaita-style Unity and Buddhist Untying, then back again. One of them, [Jackson Peterson](https://www.amazon.com/Natural-Bliss-Being-Jackson-Peterson/dp/1482020173), posted publicly like a ridiculous daily pendulum on the AtR Facebook, and he's something of a guru.

What happens in the experience is one thing. How it changes someone is another. I would ask what path they were following, whether anything became irreversible, and what entered their conduct, family life, sexuality, relationships, emotional repair, and ordinary functioning.

Conduct shows what lasted; phenomenology -- the shape of the experience itself -- still tells us what happened.

# What “Nibbāna” Is Actually Saying

The original usage of the word nibbāna was to describe a fire going out. [My book](https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/nibbana-book) treated "unweaving" as the word's direct etymology. That was wrong.

Buddhaghosa wrote a famous systematization of the Buddha's teaching in the fifth century, called the [*Visuddhimagga*](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/nanamoli/PathofPurification2011.pdf). In it, he derives nibbāna from *vāna*, "binding" or "fastening," based on the understanding that craving joins, binds, and laces one becoming to the next. [Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu](https://www.dhammatalks.org/books/MindLikeFire/Section0007.html) agrees with this explanation of nibbāna as more fitting, even if less academically strict. He traces it back to the early fire image also. A fire depends on fuel (*upādāna*, the same word used for clinging or sustenance), and it becomes free when that binding to fuel ends.

That is why Ṭhānissaro's sutta translations use "unbinding" for nibbāna. My argument for the meaning of nibbāna will be described in the next sections. Like Buddhaghosa and Ṭhānissaro, I believe the strict etymological definition is not as helpful as an interpretive one for truly understanding the word.

The suttas themselves already speak in tangles, knots, and nets. [SN 1.23](https://suttacentral.net/sn1.23/en/sujato) describes a "tangle within" and a "tangle without," then says the *arahants'* (fully enlightened ones') tangle is disentangled. [Dhammapada 90](https://suttacentral.net/dhp90/en/sujato) says the liberated person has abandoned every *gantha* (knot, tie, or bond). [Dhammapada 180](https://suttacentral.net/dhp180/en/sujato) calls craving *jālinī* (like a net, ensnaring). Unbinding and disentangling are deeply native ways to describe liberation from the binding up of the senses.

What came apart in my "room experience" was the fastening of separate sensory flows which ordinarily cohere into one established consciousness, one self, one world, and renewed existence. The room looked as though it were unweaving, and the suttas' tangles, knots, and nets make that visual metaphor feel less accidental.

My old "hard nibbāna / soft nibbāna" language was also my taxonomy. But here's a better breakdown of how I'd explain it now:

- **Partial loosening:** a finger, bodily region, or pair of sense fields becomes less unified while the witness and world remain.
- **Nibbāna:** the unified witness and scene disappear while sensory appearances remain self-presenting.
- **Full nibbāna:** same as regular nibbāna, but not limited to sensations in the body.
- **Ordinary functioning afterward:** the body-world package assembles again.
- **Remaining fetters and stabilization:** the patterns the early Buddhist texts say keep beings bound to rebirth -- craving, aversion, conceit, and habit -- can keep weakening long after the breakthrough.

## How Contact Builds a World

The way I understand how my "world" of coherent experience is constructed starts with my finger and room experiences.

Blissful color-flows were each present on their own, with no witness behind them. As Soh likes to say, there is no awareness apart from the awared. Meaning, experience is already aware. It's tautological. If we were to say that there is an experience apart from the awareness of it, then we run into the age-old riddle of what that experience would be without awareness of it.

These appearances or awareness-flows meet locally, like a [MeetUp](https://www.meetup.com/) group (although MeetUp actually banned me for trying to organize a study group about this, which is why I'm just a Substack author instead of the next [Jim Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Jones), perhaps). I call those MeetUps "initial contact." That is my term, broader than the Pāli word for contact, *phassa*. Those local MeetUps can help organize a sense door and the sense-consciousness that goes with it.

[SN 12.44](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN12_44.html) gives the canonical sequence: eye and forms condition eye-consciousness; the meeting of all three is contact.

The crucial distinction here is between awareness and consciousness. The Pāli does not have separate nouns for each, but it does distinguish sensations themselves (which I refer to as awareness-flows) from the knowledge of the sensations (which I refer to as consciousness, or reflexive awareness).

Notice that in [SN 12.44](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN12_44.html), consciousness, and then feeling, arises dependent on one thing perceiving the other. And in my borrowed AtR terminology, there's still no primordial awareness that comes before appearance-flows. That's one difference between Buddhism and Advaita or new-age spirituality.

**Multisensory integration makes the mind**

## The Myriad Things Experience Themselves

Dōgen, the 13th century Japanese founder of Soto Zen, described awakening with almost exactly this reversal. In the Tanahashi/Aitken translation of his *Genjōkōan*, he says:

> “To carry yourself forward and experience myriad things is delusion. That myriad things come forth and experience themselves is awakening.”

The translation is available in full [here](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/GenjoKoan_Aitken.htm). Other legitimate translations say that myriad things “illuminate,” “verify,” or “actualize” the self. The Japanese itself does not secretly contain my entire theory. Tanahashi’s version remains a serious interpretive translation, and it points beautifully toward the same reversal I am describing.

I am generally suspicious of later *Mahāyāna* scriptures -- a broad family of Buddhist traditions that developed after the earliest discourses and includes Zen -- that put new teachings into the historical Buddha’s mouth. Still, when Dōgen gives me one this good, I am willing to accept help from the other team.

Even the old Zen riddle (*koan*) about a tree falling in the forest brings us back to the Bāhiya Sutta: without anybody there to hear it, does it make a sound? The question challenges whether sound requires a separate hearer. My answer is that auditory appearing does not require a little person behind it. “The sound hears itself” is imperfect language, but ordinary grammar is even more committed to the imaginary owner.

# The Suttas Hid the Answer in Several Drawers

The annoying thing is I still can't point to one sutta that lays out this whole model explicitly. The Buddha, or those who recorded his sayings, left the pieces scattered around, and I guess somehow, I feel like I'm hired to put the socks back in the drawer. 😛

[Ud 1.10](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/KN/Ud/ud1_10.html) gives us the most concise instruction for experiencing nibbāna, but still just one puzzle piece: in the seen, heard, sensed, and cognized, there is no extra "you" attached to them. [SN 12.44](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN12_44.html) gives another piece: eye, form, and eye-consciousness meet as contact (meet as contact almost sounds tautological, sorry). Then contact produces feeling (which, when limited by [Ud 1.10](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/KN/Ud/ud1_10.html), must mean something feeling something, not just feeling itself). Then craving, clinging, becoming, and birth follow along naturally from there. The sutta calls that chain the "origination of the world," which is generally understood to be sutta shorthand for saṃsāra.

[SN 12.67](https://suttacentral.net/sn12.67/en/bodhi) adds the feedback loop between all those links. Consciousness and mind-and-body (*nāma-rūpa*, often erroneously translated as name and form) lean on each other like two bundles of reeds. Like the game of Jenga, when we pull either bundle away, the other falls (which is one reason the "name and form" translation makes no sense). All of this is part of the overall teaching of *Paṭiccasamuppāda*, the Buddha's account of how conditions produce suffering and rebirth, which is further detailed in [DN 15](https://suttacentral.net/dn15/en/sujato). The Chinese *Āgamas*, early Buddhist discourse collections preserved in Chinese, [DĀ 13](https://suttacentral.net/da13/lzh/taisho) and [MĀ 97](https://suttacentral.net/ma97/lzh/taisho), preserve the same basic reciprocal relationship.

This is one way in which the Buddha said his teaching is a "middle path" between two extremes: on one extreme we have Advaita, perennial philosophy, and new-age spirituality, where nearly everyone falls into these days, claiming that consciousness comes first and constructs everything else. At the other extreme, we have the materialist view that matter comes first and produces consciousness. As the Buddha saw it, they both keep each other standing.

[MN 140](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN140.html) also repeats these themes, but what it adds is a treatment of the meditative absorptions (jhānas) of infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception, without treating any of them as the finish line. Apparently even a very impressive nothing can still be fabricated, and I can testify to that, because I went through those also prior to my nibbāna experience.

When we put all the socks back into one drawer, we get more than what I had tried to squeeze out of the Bāhiya Sutta by itself: separate sense contacts, separate consciousnesses and feelings, consciousness and name-and-form propping each other up, and craving and clinging keeping the whole thing personal and running.

As I mentioned before, the texts don't explicitly say that the sensory streams are each aware in parallel, or that binding them together is what creates one owner and one world. That's the model I think they point toward when I combine them with what I experienced. Honestly, at this point it seems like common sense to me, but hindsight is 20/20, as they say.

## How the Experiential Key Can Fade

After 15 years of reading popular & obscure Buddhist books, plus countless hours on forums like SuttaCentral.net, I never once found a positive definition of what nibbāna actually is. Everyone says what nibbāna does (liberates), what it ends (rebirth, consciousness, defilements), what it isn't (anything), and then gives me the aggregates, sense bases, dependent origination, and no-self again. But what is the experience? What are all those lists pointing to? Why is it no Theravāda monk ever told me anything useful despite devoting their life to this?

These books did at least tell me that everything arises from conditions. That raised two questions that seemed like elephants in the room: does a self exist, and what could free will mean if choices are conditioned?

Most Buddhists I've spoken with didn't understand the free-will question, even though it's explained clearly in suttas like [AN 3.61](https://suttacentral.net/an3.61/en/bodhi): choices are conditioned, but they still matter because they become conditions for what happens next.

Regarding the self, the Buddha is famous for teaching *anattā*, or non-self. But many people remain confused about whether he denied a self or merely denied that conditioned experience is the true self. In [SN 44.10](https://suttacentral.net/sn44.10/en/sujato), he refuses to answer the question directly because either answer would confuse an already confused person further. But in many other suttas he specifically states that self-view itself is the problem, and the self is just composed of changing aggregates.

And in [AN 6.61](https://suttacentral.net/an6.61/en/sujato), he was misunderstood even by many of his senior monks with regard to dependent origination, and his clarification remained cryptically terse.

The suttas clearly show that the Buddha succeeded in liberating thousands of people, and the Vinaya says [500 fully enlightened monks took part in the first council after his death](https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd21?view=normal&lang=en). The problem is that as the people who directly understood nibbāna, no-self, and dependent origination became rarer, the words kept getting interpreted by people who were still experiencing one unified consciousness and one world, and the hints stopped landing. I'm reminded of [Ud 6.4](https://suttacentral.net/ud6.4/en/sujato), when the Buddha talks about how sectarian teachers quarrel over incomplete understandings of reality, much like blind men who each touch a different part of an elephant and argue over its true nature.

The canon itself says this kind of degradation was already happening in the Buddha's lifetime. In [SN 16.13](https://suttacentral.net/sn16.13/en/sujato), the arahant Mahā Kassapa asks why there are more training rules but fewer monks reaching final knowledge. The Buddha says counterfeit Dhamma gradually makes the true Dhamma disappear.

And the funniest part is that the words themselves may have survived surprisingly well. The discourses were transmitted orally for centuries, but they were transmitted communally, and the separately preserved early collections agree on an enormous amount. There are variants, rearrangements, and later material. Still, the words surviving is not the same as the meaning surviving. Even having the right pieces to the puzzle doesn't ensure you can put them together to see the picture on the box.

# A Map Can Save Years -- and Create Counterfeits

That leaves a practical problem. In [DN 3](https://suttacentral.net/dn3/en/sujato) and [MN 56](https://suttacentral.net/mn56/en/sujato), the Buddha waits until a student's mind is "ready, pliable, rid of hindrances, elated, and confident" before giving the teaching that produces the Dhamma-eye, the breakthrough vision associated with stream entry. What happens when a stage map lets anyone skip straight to the answer?

These days, somebody can seemingly pull the trigger by copying a stage map from AtR or [Dharma Overground](http://dharmaoverground.org/) out of curiosity, competition, or the hope of claiming an attainment.

And nibbāna is genuinely needed as the "trigger" for stream entry. If nibbāna is never experienced, then what exactly was recognized? Where did the intuitive certainty about the Dhamma come from? Again, from [MN 56](https://suttacentral.net/mn56/en/sujato) (and others), we see that when one attains stream entry, the "stainless, immaculate vision of the Dhamma" arises in them. It's a powerful experience.

But without the "ammunition" of accumulated desire, practice, and readiness for a radical insight aimed at ending rebirth and attachment to self-view, it only makes a cool sound. The event can get filed under "awesome experience" while the old metaphysics quietly returns. That's why Buddha taught [loving-kindness meditation](https://love.u-dont-exist.com) and the [Noble Eightfold Path](https://suttacentral.net/sn45.42-47/en/bodhi) -- ethical conduct, meditation, and wisdom about ending rebirth and self-view.

Awakening to Reality's map saved me, but I can't recommend it to others, for a number of reasons besides their lack of clarity about nibbāna versus daily life, as mentioned before.

It seems to me that AtR was largely Soh's semi-authorized editorial creation from John's journals. John supplied much of the insight while Soh systematized it into a searchable, teachable, generalized stage map. Soh sent me his many years of chats with John, and from that I gathered that John was somewhat ambivalent about teaching in general, but maybe like me, he felt called to do so because nobody else was teaching anything of substance. As such, I think John allowed Soh to function as his mouthpiece. That's a smart arrangement: the student can promote the teacher while the teacher keeps low-key describing his own posts as "a simple sharing" or "nothing authoritative."

I don't want to caricature AtR as just a Buddhist version of Google Maps. Their blog also discusses [ethics and the Noble Eightfold Path](https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2022/06/the-awakening-to-reality-practice-guide.html), and John was often correcting Soh on [the difference between an experience, an insight, a view, and stabilization](https://www.awakeningtoreality.com/2022/06/someone-asked-about-pemako-stages.html).

AtR is also unclear on determinism, by which I mean that everything happens due to causes and conditions. Determinism does not make effort meaningless. It makes effort meaningful as another condition.

On the other hand, Soh did seem to fancy himself as the map king. Ironically, he became the one who would "authenticate" people's stages of awakening, despite John Tan saying it was only his own experience and that awakening was "self-authenticated by doubtless realization."

I stayed for 6 days with one of the people Soh authenticated as a stream-enterer, Frank Yang, and I can tell you for sure he didn't even understand the very basics of what Buddha taught (including the word "conditions"), and he got angry when I questioned his understandings. Although I can't fault a YouTube star for not understanding conditions, since around that same time period, even Āyasmā Ariyadhammika, the head teacher at the [Theravāda Buddhist center](http://sasanarakkha.org/) that gives "advanced training" to monastics, didn't understand "conditions" when I met him. He thanked me when I explained the term, and it seemed like he had a "lightbulb moment."

Frank did feel that he suffered much less after softening his identity, so that's valuable, but it doesn't equate to stream entry. He was a good, if shallow, AtR parrot, and he was becoming somewhat of an exalted guru thanks to [his beautiful, crazy YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@frankyang) and his charisma.

## A map can point; it cannot perform the seeing

[Dhammapada 276](https://suttacentral.net/dhp276/en/buddharakkhita) says awakened teachers point out the path while practitioners make the effort. [MN 38](https://suttacentral.net/mn38/en/sujato) speaks of the Dhamma as something to be known, seen, and realized for oneself. [DN 16](https://suttacentral.net/dn16/en/sujato) pairs reliance on oneself with reliance on the Dhamma, and [MN 22](https://suttacentral.net/mn22/en/sujato) compares the Dhamma to a raft used for crossing rather than an identity to drag around after reaching shore.

The more detailed the map becomes, the more curiosity, honesty, and empirical self-scrutiny it demands.

An unprepared reader can use the answer key to exalt a psychedelic rupture, depersonalization, derealization, trauma, or a temporary no-self state into stream entry.

Secrecy (as in [Tibetan Buddhism's secret teachings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sg-5CDOcsTM)) won't solve this. Readiness warnings, anti-counterfeit framing, ethical and relational outcome checks, and personal verification are the best protection I know.

# Stream Entry and the Dream Running Out

Theravāda Buddhism describes four stages of awakening: stream-entry, once-returning, non-returning, and final enlightenment, called arahantship. For this article, the first one matters.

[AN 10.92](https://suttacentral.net/an10.92/en/sujato) shows that stream-enterers gain confidence in the Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha, ethical restraint, and direct understanding of causality. [AN 3.87](https://suttacentral.net/an3.87/en/sujato) ties stream-entry to the ending of identity view, uncertainty about the path, and grasping at habits and practices as sufficient in themselves. It calls the stream-enterer certain, headed for awakening, and no longer destined for states of woe. The later stages of awakening keep weakening passion, aversion, and delusion until the remaining fetters and deeper defilements end.

## Practice can carry into a future life

[AN 4.191](https://suttacentral.net/an4.191/en/sujato) says that a monk who has theoretical understanding of the teachings may be reborn among heavenly beings even if he dies unmindfully. The Dhamma can then return to mind slowly, and after that he can quickly reach stream entry ("the destination").

And we find a stronger guarantee in [SN 25.1](https://suttacentral.net/sn25.1/en/sujato). Someone who has fully accepted the Dhamma through faith or reflective deliberation is said to be irreversibly approaching stream entry by the time of death, and incapable of doing evil deeds that would lead to a rebirth in a lower realm.

## Authentication tips

From the AtR debacle, I've learned that stream-entry self-authentication *and* authentication by someone else are both problematic. I think we also need to use some rubric like this, although it isn't sufficient by itself since it only describes the after-effects:

- Do other people notice a durable reduction in your selfishness?
- Can you be content with less? For example, the Buddha advised his monastics to wear robes they collected from dead corpses ([Kd 8.11](https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd8/en/brahmali)), and to use their own fermented urine ([Kd 1.64](https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd1/en/brahmali)) and feces ([Kd 6.2](https://suttacentral.net/pli-tv-kd6/en/brahmali)) as medicine.
- Are you more joyful and more enjoyable to be around without turning into a people-pleaser, as in [MN 89](https://suttacentral.net/mn89/en/sujato)? Regarding Soh Wei Yu's false claim to be in bliss 24/7, that is not what a stream-enterer should expect. [AN 11.12](https://suttacentral.net/an11.12/en/sujato) says a noble disciple can reach ease and joy through recollection, while in [MN 75](https://suttacentral.net/mn75/en/bodhi) the Buddha himself said he felt pain when perceiving what ordinary people felt as sensual pleasures. So it isn't a simple equation of enlightenment making you more blissful.
- Do you now find the Dhamma intuitively coherent rather than merely memorized?
- Do you know, with unusual certainty, that rebirth is moving toward its end?
- Can you still recognize trauma, apologize, and do ordinary psychological repair instead of floating above it?

The last test matters because a stream-enterer still has most fetters. Even a genuine breakthrough does not turn somebody into a therapist, let alone a saint.


## Ending the dream of saṃsāra

Maybe the greatest prize for the stream-enterer is their "release date:" [AN 3.88](https://suttacentral.net/an3.88/en/sujato) says there are at most seven further lifetimes before final awakening. There is [a lot of modern academic evidence for rebirth](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0AtTM9hgCDw). But who has those lives if no permanent self exists? Buddhists do not imagine a soul traveling from body to body, since that would be a belief in an unchanging self-essence. Instead, we understand rebirth as causal continuity without a traveler (often called a mind-stream): craving and clinging condition becoming, and becoming conditions birth. The later Buddhist [*Milindapañha* 3.5.5](https://suttacentral.net/mil3.5.5/en/kelly) compares the process of rebirth to one lamp lighting another. The flame doesn't migrate, yet the second lighting is not unrelated to the first.

## Why nibbāna is "awakening"

The lucid-dream comparison from earlier also shapes how I understand the seven-life limit said to be had by the stream-enterer. Once the dream is known as a dream, it becomes naturally difficult to remain stuck in it, similar to how hard it is to be absorbed in a movie when the lights go on and you remember you're in a theatre. If you've ever had a lucid dream, you know what I'm talking about.

I used to attribute "life is like a dream" directly to the historical Buddha. The famous stanza I loved actually comes from the later Mahāyāna [*Diamond Sutra*](https://read.84000.co/translation/toh16.html), which puts some words in his mouth, although the early Pāli texts offer closely related images. [SN 22.95](https://suttacentral.net/sn22.95/en/sujato) compares form to foam, feeling to a bubble, perception to a mirage, and consciousness to a magic trick. [Dhammapada 170](https://suttacentral.net/dhp170/en/sujato) asks us to see the world as a bubble and mirage.

The verse is still beautiful. Here is a widely circulated English rendering:

> So should one view all of the fleeting world:
>
> A star at dawn, a bubble in a stream,
>
> A flash of lightning in a summer cloud,
>
> A flickering lamp, a phantom, and a dream.

For me, nibbāna was really like waking up from a dream, except that nobody woke up. Reasoning can make the dream doubtful. The event made doubt about the dream impossible to reverse.

# What Science Can Map

If you can remember the last thunderstorm, then you don’t need neuroscience to know that the mind binds different sensory streams together. When you see lightning, you count the seconds until you hear the thunder. [Five seconds means the lightning was about a mile away.](https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-science-thunder) When they seem to happen at the same time, it means the lightning is close, but you understand they didn't truly reach you at the same time. It's just that our brain naturally merges stimuli that are close enough together.

But have you ever wondered whether several experiences can happen in parallel before -- or without -- the mind turning them into one apparent scene?

The neuroscientist [Semir Zeki](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10448004/) has argued for something close to this within vision: color, motion, form, and location are processed separately and can become conscious at different times. He calls the local results *microconsciousnesses*. In his theory, the completed visual picture is a later *macroconsciousness*: binding joins experiences that were already conscious.

The strongest evidence is visual, and Zeki’s conclusion reaches beyond the evidence. Still, he places consciousness before final unity instead of waiting for the finished scene. [Recurrent Processing Theory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16997611/) offers one possible mechanism: feedback inside a local sensory network may be enough for experience before the information becomes globally available. Whether local recurrence is sufficient to produce conscious experience remains disputed, though.

If you're a mom, you might already know about doing many things at the same time, but science is still figuring that out. In one small [Descriptive Experience Sampling study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00494/full), intrepid researchers had the audacity to interrupt five people 180 times altogether and ask what was happening in experience at the exact moment before each beep. I would have smacked those researchers! But these subjects were more enlightened than me, and they repeatedly reported several things at once: sounds, bodily sensations, inner images, thoughts, and events inside and outside the body. This tells us one thing: experience isn’t obviously restricted to one item at a time.

But if you're ready to jump down the Twilight Zone rabbit hole, then let's look at the [split-brain experiments](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/140/7/2051/3892700). Don't worry, these people weren't sawed apart for no reason. The corpus callosum, which joins the two brain hemispheres, had been surgically severed as an epilepsy treatment. These case studies point to how consciousness can function without a single owner. In these folks, the right hand can answer from information available to the left hemisphere, while the left hand can point to or draw what the right hemisphere knows. The speaking left hemisphere may say it saw nothing, while the left hand chooses the correct object.

In the famous chicken-claw and snow-scene experiment, the left hemisphere was shown a chicken claw, while the right hemisphere was shown snow. When given a choice of images, the right hand chose a chicken, and the left chose a shovel. The speaking left hemisphere could see the shovel choice, but it had never seen the snow scene. When asked why the shovel had been chosen, it invented a reason: the shovel was for cleaning the chicken shed. That's called confabulation.

It doesn’t look like there was one overall owner or witness to all that. The speaking mind didn’t merely lack part of the event. It didn’t know that it lacked it, and it confidently explained behavior caused somewhere else. To me, that shows how readily we replace what actually happens in reality with a story that validates what we think reality should be, and it could explain how the story of the self emerges from simple conditions.

I first read a good summary of this research in Sam Harris’s *Waking Up*. Interestingly, I don’t think Harris followed his own evidence all the way through. He got rid of the personal self while leaving pure awareness behind as the final witness, which lands back in Advaita territory.

What's still unclear from the split-brainers is whether there are actually two self-aware hemispheres, since we can't easily ask the mostly non-speaking right hemisphere.

Although the model I'm proposing isn't fully addressed by science, there is a recent theory from 2024 called [Nested Observer Windows](https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2024/1/niae010/7631826) which comes close. It models the brain as many observer windows operating at different sizes and speeds, each generating its own cognitive stream while higher windows gather information from lower ones (talk about voyeurism!). The authors ask whether only the highest window is conscious, whether consciousness begins somewhere above a threshold, or whether every window has experience.

## The Limit of Science

None of this proves my interpretation of the room experience, but it gives the argument a third leg, which makes it more safe to ride, like a tricycle. So where we're at now with science is, it breaks down our ordinary perception into: 1) parallel processing, 2) several simultaneous experiential strands, 3) fragmented access, and 4) a narrator that confuses its partial report with the whole system. It also contains serious theories in which local consciousness appears before final binding, which is part of what I'm talking about.

Unsurprisingly, though, you won't find a study which documents the exact event I’m describing: visual, auditory, bodily, and other flows remaining self-presenting after the common scene, body-world, and witness disappear.

Objective experimentation may never be able to settle that last step. It can track how the color red is discriminated, remembered, joined with other information, and reported. But red itself never climbs out of the scanner and testifies that it was already its own awareness. That part has to be investigated from inside experience.

# Citizen Science: Try a Tiny Bit of Nibbāna

Most people who try the guided practice won't go POOF directly into nibbāna at first, but it does have some uniquely interesting effects compared to other meditations you might try.

The exercise starts with just feeling your head and feet at the same time without the middle getting in the way. See if those parts can become independent experiences without always combining into "my body, my choice."

[[SUBSTACK_DIGEST_POST 19 postId="169928087" videoUploadId="34cdde8e-5cfc-450b-9d83-57c004e50cba" canonicalUrl="https://ibogaqueen.substack.com/p/nibbana-a-quick-and-effective-new" title="nibbāna: a quick and effective new meditation technique"]]

Notice what happens.

Did your head and feet feel like two separate streams for a moment? Or did your attention just bounce between them? Take note.

Then try fingers, sounds, colors, or whatever else is present, and see whether the sense of self or body unification loosens a tad.

## What Counts as Success, Confusion, or Danger

Here's what success actually feels like for me when the loosening works:

My happy head and happy feet both feel vivid as simultaneous yet independent experience-flows. If my attention is bouncing back and forth, that's still reg mode, meaning I might need to keep trying the untying, but maybe a bit less trying and more relax-maxxing.

Several sensations can remain present without becoming "my body."

The witness gets hard to locate, and an unusually deep relief or bliss shows up.

I might get just a taste of that or it might feel like more. Sometimes it can be hard to understand though if it's imagining what I know I should feel, when it's just a small taste.

If it still feels like one body watched by the Watcher, nothing has loosened yet. Numb, dull, or foggy might be indicators that you're sleepy and it's naptime.

The farther unification loosens, the less useful you become for normal tasks. That's why I prefer to do this while lying down, not while driving, cycling, swimming, cooking over fire, crossing traffic, working near heights, operating machinery, skydiving, or flying your pet dragon.

[[SUBSTACK_IMAGE 20 src="https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/99377198-a0f5-41d7-8b26-87ddcb628783_1672x941.png" caption="Yfw you thought I was kidding about meditating while skydiving." alt="" imageSize="None" fullscreen="None"]]

If it becomes frightening or chaotic, come back. Open your eyes, wiggle your toes, name things in the room. Talk to your cat or pet elephant.

I've always come back so far. That isn't a promise from the future.

Most of the unification usually returns afterward. Usually.

The afterglow can last for hours or days, sometimes longer, depending partly on where I am and who is yelling at me to feed the sheep.

In a calm moment, I can still get maybe a 1% taste -- a release valve I didn't have before. Going deeper takes more time. When the body and world come back together, they still don't feel primordial.

Try it somewhere safe and see what happens. Was it just an experience or did you have some actual insight, or some realization? Do you notice any permanent changes? Less selfish, less delusions, more love?

# Bāhiya Got Enlightened and Then a Cow Killed Him

Bāhiya hears [one short instruction](https://suttacentral.net/ud1.10/en/sujato), becomes fully liberated, and then instead of appearing on book tours, he gets killed by a runaway cow. The Buddha later declares him totally unbound, never to be born again, and the person who [grasped the Dhamma faster than anyone else](https://suttacentral.net/an1.209/en/sujato).

Some people might read that and think Buddhism didn’t work that well for poor ol’ Bāhiya. But actually, the Buddhist end goal is to end existence itself, and since Bāhiya was not set to be reborn, he reached the end goal. Essentially, he untied and was released into the final nibbāna, called *parinibbāna*. Parinibbāna is not different from full nibbāna, except that there’s no coming back when the meditation’s done. The living breakthrough to nibbāna is what makes the final direction knowable.

It’s worth noting that Bāhiya was not just lucky to get enlightened so fast. [Ud 1.10](https://suttacentral.net/ud1.10/en/sujato) shows his urgency and immediate understanding, while the later [Bāhiya Apadāna](https://suttacentral.net/tha-ap538/en/walters) says he had aspired and practiced toward that quick comprehension for a hundred thousand eons. Most people are not as ready as Bāhiya was, so don’t be surprised if you can’t leap from complex PTSD (cPTSD), self-hatred, or a chaotic life directly into complete liberation just because I told you a sound heard itself. The Buddha’s [Noble Eightfold Path](https://suttacentral.net/sn45.42-47/en/bodhi) still needs to be followed, and I would mainly highlight the importance of ethical practice, [loving-kindness](https://love.u-dont-exist.com/), [inner-child reparenting](https://innerchild.u-dont-exist.com/), [healthy relationships](http://romance.u-dont-exist.com/), [a supportive practice community](https://community.u-dont-exist.com/).

# Appendix: The Doctrinal Arguments Behind This Interpretation

This is the nerd cellar under the article. You can skip it and still use the practice. If you want to see why I’m making these doctrinal claims, the links below include my SuttaCentral research trail, the Pāli discourses, Chinese parallels, and main scholarly disagreement.

## Has Anyone on SuttaCentral Discussed This?

[SuttaCentral](https://suttacentral.net) is known as the main repository of the Pāli Canon and its translations. It's the most authoritative public discussion forum for the Early Buddhist Texts. So that's where I went to peruse whether anyone already put my understanding together or found something that ruled it out. If I'd found either, this article would be very different -- or I probably wouldn’t be publishing it at all. I did find support for several pieces of my multisensory binding synthesis, because those pieces are in the suttas, but I still haven’t found anyone combining them into the full model I’m proposing.

One of the most relevant earlier threads, posted by a moderator, asks whether [sense-consciousness arises one at a time](https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/sense-consciousness-arising-one-at-a-time/21530). This question is posed because in the later Abhidhamma commentaries on the suttas, it is taught that the mind is like a movie made of individual experience frames in a series. Because that understanding is not in the suttas, it often contributes to controversy and confusion. As we can see from the replies, the suttas themselves don’t specify exactly how the streams coordinate, but nor do they lean toward strict serialization. What interested me was that the OP asked just the right question, and nobody had an answer from the suttas. They had common sense wisdom, they had some later commentary theory, and some resorted to modern science.

Ironically, the closest direct answer I found on the forum, before Charlie replied to [my thread,](https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/does-sn-12-44-explain-the-missing-mechanism-behind-ud-1-10-and-nibbana/44650) was literally just one sentence: [“It’s called the binding problem.”](https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/spin-off-from-bhante-sujato-s-essay-self-no-self-not-self/32483/24) 😂

Charlie went further with me. He pointed to [SN 22.79](https://suttacentral.net/sn22.79/en/bodhi), where volitional formations are constructed by and construct the conditioned five aggregates that make up the self: forms, sensations, perceptions, mental formations, consciousness. Later, it says the liberated ones have dismantled, scattered, and are no longer accumulating those aggregates (called *khandhas* in Pāli). Charlie calls that “de-conditioning.” I wouldn’t say the living arahant’s remaining aggregates have become unconditioned, because the sutta doesn’t say that. But the construction and dismantling language is right there, and it's common in many suttas.

[MN 43](https://suttacentral.net/mn43/en/suddhaso) adds another piece: each physical sense has its own domain, while mind is their common resort. That gets us separate sensory streams, and the meeting room. It still doesn’t specify how they become one body-world scene.

His reading of the Bāhiya instruction came closer still. He sees the ordinary graspable objects giving way to an underlying flow, with ordinary recognition later returning in a way that “transcends and includes” it. That’s the closest substantial convergence I’ve found on the forum.

My model still goes one step further: during nibbāna, the common body-world-witness assembly drops out while visual, auditory, and bodily flows remain self-presenting. Ordinary functional integration returns afterward. I still don’t see Charlie or the suttas explicitly describing the cross-sensory binding mechanism itself, which is the part my model is trying to explain.

The fact that this is so hard to imagine also supports it, ironically. After all, the Buddha was pointing toward something which ordinary consciousness could never fathom until he began showing people where to look. In the same vein, we wouldn’t expect a fish to realize it’s in water. If an explanation of nibbāna feels completely familiar and easy to picture from inside ordinary experience, I’d wager that is probably evidence against it.

## What the suttas say about nibbāna as the gate to stream-entry

The main article already gave my personal evidence for nibbāna as a necessary but insufficient condition for stream entry. What the suttas say on this question is disputed.

The suttas do not present stream entry as finally agreeing with Buddhist doctrine, nor as accepting the Buddha as your savior. The Buddha is concise in [SN 25.1-10](https://suttacentral.net/sn25):

> "Someone who accepts these teachings after deliberating them with a degree of wisdom is called a follower of teachings. They've stepped into the sure path, they've stepped into the level of the true person, and they've passed beyond the level of the untrue person. They can't do any deed which would make them be reborn in hell, the animal realm, or the ghost realm. They can't die without realizing the fruit of stream-entry. Someone who understands and sees these principles is called a stream-enterer."

The special seeing is further detailed in suttas like [AN 8.22](https://suttacentral.net/an8.22/en/sujato). In this sutta, when the Buddha saw Ugga was ready, he gave him precise teachings that led him past the gate of stream entry. Ugga explains: "I saw, attained, understood, and fathomed the Dhamma. I went beyond doubt, got rid of indecision, and became self-assured and independent of others regarding the Teacher's instructions."

[AN 10.92](https://suttacentral.net/an10.92/en/sujato) adds direct understanding of dependent origination/arising, confidence in the Buddha, Dhamma, and Saṅgha, and ethical restraint.

[AN 3.87](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN3_87.html) gives the classic three fetters that break at stream entry, which I explained in the main article. Most fetters still remain, to lesser degrees, after that, disappearing at later stages. My experience aligns with [MN 48](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN48.html): defilements can still arise, but they no longer take over in the same way, and they're recognized much faster. [SN 13.1](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN13_1.html) compares the suffering that remains after stream entry to dust on a fingernail beside the whole earth. Well, maybe I've forgotten how much ordinary people suffer, but I'm suspecting this one used some poetic license.

## Infinity & Nothingness: Unity is cultivated and then Untied

Several suttas show the Buddha teaching people to cultivate unity and then abandon it, which supports my model and explains John Tan's stages. The Buddha actually suggested that people pursue more unified states of consciousness (jhānas) to begin with, but then abandon unity to reach nibbāna. One example is [MN 137](https://suttacentral.net/mn137/en/sujato), which distinguishes equanimity based on sensory diversity from equanimity based on unity. Its unity side includes infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither-perception-nor-non-perception. Then the discourse says to use unity to relinquish diversity and non-identification to relinquish unity. Interestingly, in the Chinese Āgama parallel, [MĀ 163](https://suttacentral.net/ma163/lzh/taisho), that final critical advice to abandon unity is left out.

[MN 44](https://suttacentral.net/mn44/en/sujato) defines *samādhi* as *cittassa ekaggatā*, which means the unification of mind. The various stages of samādhi meditation are called jhānas, and they predate Buddhism, but remain a preliminary practice in it. In the 2nd jhāna, the standard formula uses the Pāli phrase *cetaso ekodibhāva*, the mind becoming unified. Then, in [DN 1](https://suttacentral.net/dn1/en/sujato), we find a crucial warning for those contemplatives who take that unified state to be supreme nibbāna. Buddha says they're stuck in wrong view. Finally, in [MN 111](https://suttacentral.net/mn111/en/sujato), we see unification as thematic through all the jhānas, including the formless 6th sphere (infinite consciousness) and 7th (nothingness), followed by repeated acknowledgement of "an escape beyond." The path gathers experience into unity, then uses that stability to let the unity go too.

When the suttas talk about release, the language changes. [SN 1.23](https://suttacentral.net/sn1.23/en/sujato) describes the tangle inside and outside, says arahants have disentangled it, and locates the cutting of the tangle where nāma-rūpa (name-and-form or mind-and-body) and perceptions of sensory impingement cease without remainder. [Dhammapada 90](https://suttacentral.net/dhp90-99/en/sujato) says all ganthā (knots or ties) have been abandoned. [Dhammapada 180](https://suttacentral.net/dhp179-196/en/sujato) calls craving jālinī, net-like or ensnaring. [AN 9.36](https://suttacentral.net/an9.36/en/sujato) describes nibbāna as stilling formations, relinquishing attachments, ending craving, fading, and cessation.

Then when I read [AN 6.61](https://suttacentral.net/an6.61/en/sujato), I noticed the weaving imagery was unusually explicit. In that sutta, you can see how several senior monks offer different interpretations on one of Buddha's teachings on nibbāna. The Buddha says they all spoke well "in a way," then explains what he meant by it: "Contact, mendicants, is one end. The origin of contact is the second end. The cessation of contact is the middle. And craving is the seamstress, for craving weaves one to being regenerated in one state of existence or another." This shows us again how contact, cessation, becoming, and weaving already occupy the same doctrinal neighborhood.

There's still nothing in the Pāli canon that directly translates to my modern phrase, "Global multisensory unbinding." But you can see the direction they point in: the Buddha advises us to gather the mind, warns us against mistaking unity for nibbāna, then describes liberation not only as escape, relinquishment, and cessation, but also as disentangling/untying, abandoned knots, and craving's net losing its grip. I don't see what it is that could be disentangled in nibbāna aside from the recurring assembly of the six sensory streams which Buddha says precede the sense of one witness and one world.

## Why I Think This Is the Only Logical Possibility

My goal here is to approach this question as rigorously as possible so the explanation is actually useful. That’s why I’ve tried to see how the other explanations might fit.

One interpretation is that nibbāna means literal extinction, like a lamp going out -- poof -- so being gone in deep sleep can start to sound almost like what everyone already experiences nightly. But that doesn’t fit [Ud 1.10](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/KN/Ud/ud1_10.html), where there is still the seen, heard, sensed, and cognized. Some people might think of it more like the perception of nothingness, but that’s another conditioned attainment the Buddha went beyond.

A lot of Mahāyāna folk see nibbāna as some type of cosmic consciousness, but that doesn’t fit either, because infinite consciousness is another conditioned attainment. A hidden witness still leaves someone behind the experience. I loved cosmic consciousness, but it’s definitely the opposite of nibbāna in my experience.

The only other contender from Theravāda I can think of is the later Mahāsi/Abhidhamma one: path and fruition consciousness take nibbāna as an unconditioned object. Aside from being almost too vague to be worth talking about, that still has consciousness over here knowing nibbāna over there, which again doesn’t fit the Bāhiya instruction. It preserves the same basic split Thusness’s map treats as prior to anattā: awareness over here, reality over there -- even if the knowing consciousness is momentary rather than an eternal Self.

Zen is famous for its koans which are meant to break the mind itself. But Theravāda has them as well, just not packaged as such. For example, [AN 9.34](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an09/an09.034.than.html) says nibbāna is bliss precisely because nothing is felt. Ordinarily we might say, uh, well, how do you feel bliss if you don’t feel anything? But feeling in the suttas arises from contact, so it’s a precise term implying something feeling something. In nibbāna, there’s no contact, just disjoint self-known flows. There’s no eye seeing the seen, only colors coloring. In my experience, it seems that bliss is the “base state” of nature before anything is there to feel anything else. Colors coloring are way happier than a kid coloring a coloring book, basically.

Another Theravāda riddle is when nibbāna is referred to as the cessation of consciousness. That’s difficult to understand only if we think consciousness is the same as experience. In the suttas, eye-consciousness is conditioned by the eye and the colors it sees. So consciousness is not the colors themselves, which remain blissfully aware without a consciousness over here knowing them over there.

And some folks think there’s just nothing we can know about nibbāna, but [AN 4.45](https://suttacentral.net/an4.45/en/sujato) puts the world, its arising, its cessation, and the path to cessation right here in the body, perception, and mind, which means nibbāna isn’t some unreachable mystery but something a person can actually investigate and realize directly.
