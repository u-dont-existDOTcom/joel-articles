- **neuro-2026-11-08j. A finding is stated for the tissue, the species, the level and the condition it was measured in,
  and a level in the blood is not the level where the claim needs it; a correction checks both before it says what a
  change does, and never swaps one simple story for its opposite.** His words: "look at what gpt is saying: Claude is
  correct about the basic electrophysiology of low extracellular potassium, but its proposed correction is still
  oversimplified." My card corrected "if you are low on potassium (common in high stress), your brakes fail" to "with
  less potassium outside them, nerve cells rest farther from firing, not closer", citing one patient's arm and leg nerves
  at a blood potassium of 2.1 (Kuwabara 2002). Three things were wrong with it. The study measured peripheral nerves in
  a severe attack, not the brain's neurons at the levels stress brings. In brain slices, lowering potassium from 4 to 2
  quiets the cells at first and then sets off seizure-like bursts (Gorji 2001), so "farther from firing" is the opposite
  oversimplification, not the correction. And the brain keeps the potassium around its neurons within tight limits
  whatever the blood does (Hladky and Barrand 2016), which settles the question for both stories: neither the drop
  stress brings to the blood nor what you eat sets the brakes. Before a card says what a level does to a cell, name the
  cell, the species, the level and the condition the study had; check whether the body holds that level steady where
  the claim needs it (blood, brain fluid, inside the cell); and where the evidence runs both ways, say where each holds.
  Then say what the reader is left to act on (lesson neuro-2026-11-08i): here, what holds for eating potassium is high
  blood pressure (Aburto 2013), which SC-106b puts in the paragraph for him to decide.
