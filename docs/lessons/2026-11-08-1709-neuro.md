brought GPT's answers to two of his questions about card SC-106b's reasons. On the first ("claude says there is no

second he was right: "claude used this study to say that more dietary potassium doesn't help mood, only lower sodium

([SCIENCE-P23.md](../../articles/neuro-de-armoring/humanize/fix-20261108/sci-p23/SCIENCE-P23.md), "SC-106b checked

- **neuro-2026-11-08k. A study cited for or against a claim is read in full first, and reported for what it found,
  including the results that cut against the card's point; a comparison that holds a factor equal says nothing about
  that factor, for or against.** My card said of Torres 2008: "The trial I found on potassium-rich diets and mood
  doesn't show it either: both diets were high in potassium, and the one lower in salt did better." I had read only its
  abstract. The sentence was literal enough, but it pointed to salt as the cause and left out what the full text says:
  more urinary potassium went with less fatigue, the authors offer magnesium as another explanation, and they leave each
  mineral's part to further study. Both test diets raised potassium by the same amount, so the comparison between them
  could not speak to potassium at all. That is the predicate-alignment check of UDA's `patterns/reasoning-selection.md`:
  a result that stays true whether or not potassium helps mood is not evidence on that question. Before a card uses a
  study against a claim, or for one, read the paper, not its summary. Name what the design can and can't separate. Give
  the results on both sides, with their size (here small, in healthy people). Say "open, not ruled out" where the paper
  says so.
- The other question confirmed what the card did: a role in the body is not a benefit of eating more. Potassium's
  gradient "is required for proper nerve transmission", and hypokalemia "is rarely caused by low dietary potassium intake
  alone" (NIH 2022). A paragraph gives the reader the reason that changes what they do, here blood pressure, not the
  physiology that holds whatever they eat. The same check found a gap. Once the paragraph sent people with high blood
  pressure toward potassium, the caution for kidney disease and for drugs that hold on to potassium (ACE inhibitors,
  ARBs) belonged beside the step, not only at the top of the article. When a card changes who a step is for, it checks
  that step's warnings for the new readers in the same card (lesson neuro-2026-11-08f's "warnings beside their tools").
